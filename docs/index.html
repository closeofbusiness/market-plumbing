What holds the market up

Why US equity prices rose and where the money came from — assembled with bands rather than false precision, and with the join between the two stated as a measured limit rather than a gap.

01 — the answer

Where the argument stands

Prices rose mostly because earnings rose — and that earnings growth is mostly real profit rather than buybacks shrinking the share count. The multiple held up because the earnings yield barely moved while real yields rose about a third of a point. Calling that a “compressing risk premium” adds less than it seems: the premium is defined as the earnings yield minus the real yield, so most of its fall is the same rate rise, restated (C-102). It arrived as drift rather than on news, and partly reversed in the first half of 2026.

On the money side, the headline finding is a measurement result, not an economic one. The line everyone reads as "equity issuance" in the Federal Reserve's accounts is mostly ETF share creation. Strip that wrapper out and US operating companies retired equity over 2024–26 — so the money arriving met a shrinking float of company stock.

Across the channels the goal names, the verdict is mostly negative, and that is a result. The collateral channel did not carry this repricing, and shadow money did not in 2024 — though over the whole window it stays in the picture as a ceiling (C-118). The AI build-out is funded mostly from operating cash flow, with the real financing innovation off balance sheet in lease commitments; the AI debt that does exist is held mainly by long money, and money-market funds hold almost none of it. The IPO boom is two-thirds a single deal.

What we cannot say is how much of the price move the money caused. Four designs to measure that have closed, and each closed for a different, stated reason (§05).

How to read every number here — the principal’s ruling, 22 September

“Nothing here is certain if not clearly mathematically proven, thus everything needs to be looked at as potential hypothesis that needs testing.” So every claim carries one of four grades, and the default is the weakest. Identity: true by construction — and therefore not evidence; a sum that cannot fail proves nothing. Measured: observed on a stated universe with a stated denominator. Bounded: a ceiling or a floor, never quoted as an estimate. Hypothesis: untested, or tested and survived — survival is not proof. The ledger in §07 carries these grades.

02 — what drove prices

Earnings did most of the work, and most of it is real profit

Two decompositions, each reproduced from source data: where the value sits, and what the earnings part is made of.

Corroborated in direction, not independently — and partly reversed

Our premium measure has no growth term, so we checked it against one that does: Damodaran's forward-looking, growth-adjusted equity risk premium fell 4.60% → 4.20% over the identical months. Ours fell 49 basis points; his fell 40; a survey measure fell 19. They agree for a shared structural reason rather than independently: in all three a bond leg rose faster than the equity leg (+34bp, +57bp, +35bp), and in the survey, equity expectations actually rose (C-102). The measure then gave back about 17bp in the first half of 2026 as earnings caught up — that part is measured.

What did not resolve: whether the compression was cheaper risk or better growth expectations. A survey measure seemed to settle it, until an outside review showed the survey premium fell on its bond leg while equity expectations rose. That split is withdrawn (C-082). Netting growth out with Damodaran’s model leaves 36–60% of our 49bp unexplained by growth, depending on which of two defensible rate bases is used (C-104) — a range, not a split.

03 — the supply side

What looked like a supply boom was a wrapper

The quantity of equity is not fixed — but what grew was the fund layer on top of company stock, not company stock itself.

The correction that reframes the money question — C-081 / C-083

The Federal Reserve's "corporate equities" instrument counts ETF shares as equity issuance — a wrapper layer above company stock, some of it backed by bonds. Classified properly, 2024:Q1–2026:Q2 reads: ETF share creation +$3,603.8bn and operating companies (non-financial corporates) −$540.7bn. The ETF figure is corroborated independently: the Investment Company Institute’s own census matches it to under $0.1bn in both years. “Issuance net of the wrapper”, −$360.7bn, is a derived blend that also nets in foreign issuers, so it understates the operating-company retirement by half (C-107); and the Fed’s issuer lines summing to the total is how its table is built, not a check (C-106). The bars below are real, but they measure the growth of the fund layer.

"Retired" does not mean "bought back"

Net retirement is predominantly M&A — companies disappearing into cash acquisitions. On the Fed's supplementary accounts for 2024:Q1–2026:Q1, M&A retirement was $858bn against net retirement of $679bn: M&A more than accounts for all of it.

This page used to support that with a decade total — buybacks $6.40trn against issuance $6.62trn, “fully offset”. That comparison is withdrawn (C-108): the Fed’s own methodology counts buybacks by public firms only, while its issuance series blends public and private — and only 18% of that issuance was identifiable public offerings. Against those, buybacks were 5.3 times larger. The near-equality came from netting in private placements that never touch a listed share count — which is also why the buyback test in §02 used company-level filings instead.

Still unreconciled: two Fed series for non-financial net issuance disagree — +$25.2bn against +$51.3bn for 2026:Q1 — and must not be mixed in one table.

04 — every channel the goal names

Where the money came from, channel by channel

The goal asks about every channel by name. Most verdicts are negative — and a negative with a reason is the useful kind.

ChannelVerdictOver 2024–26
Passive bid / ETF creationDominantThe wrapper is the issuance (§03). ETFs bought about $2.46trn while mutual funds sold about $2.01trn — an 82% offset, consistent with rotation. Whether it was the same money switching wrapper is not measurable on free data (C-111).
AI build-out fundingMostly self-fundedFive of six hyperscalers generate more operating cash than their capex; only Oracle cannot, by about $35bn. The financing that matters is off balance sheet: leases signed but not yet commenced sum to $1,122.9bn, about 4.9× the $228.3bn of guarantees contracted (this row said 13×, against live guarantees only — C-116). The AI debt that does exist is held mainly by long money; money-market funds hold about $4bn of it, mostly as repo collateral that finances dealers rather than the companies. Large-bank exposure to AI-adjacent industries is about $450bn committed and $150bn outstanding, but about $250bn of those commitments were already there in 2015, so lending to the build-out itself is a smaller, unmeasured part (C-115, C-120).
IPO boomMeasured, buyers unknownAbout $112bn of new company money in 2026 to date — two-thirds of it one deal (SpaceX, $75bn). Who was allocated the shares is not recoverable on free data; named cornerstones appear in 3 of 10 deals.
Direct money creationWeak, not a ceilingA small positive association with equity buying. The deposit stock is 6.3× the purchases, and ETF creation is often in-kind, so deposits bound nothing (C-089).
Shadow banking / shadow moneyNot in 2024; a ceiling sinceIn 2024, a handoff, not fresh money: the increase in wholesale repo came out of the Fed's reverse-repo facility (C-085). Over the whole window it is not ruled out — raw growth is the same order of magnitude as the purchases, and even net of the handoff about a quarter of them (C-118). No share of purchases can be attributed to nonbank money without manufacturing it.
The collateral channelNot the sourceCollateral re-use intensity did not rise. The dated step in the large banks' collateral stock came in H1 2026 — after most of the price rise.

Read together, this is a result about the original hypothesis, not a gap in it. The money that bought equities was mostly already inside the system — the deposit stock, and offsetting flows between fund types — rather than newly created through shadow or collateral channels (shadow money stays in the picture as a ceiling, C-118), and the largest capex programme in the market was funded mainly from profits and leases. That does not make those channels unimportant in general. It says they are not what carried this repricing. Whether the lease stack is the next source of fragility is a different question, and it is on the calendar for 30 September.

05 — the join

The bridge is closed, and each leg closed for a different reason

Both halves rest on published data — though not every number on them is a measurement (§07 grades them). The join between them — how much a dollar of buying moves a price — is not recoverable on free data. We know exactly why, four times over.

08 — what we don't know

The honest gaps

Fewer than a week ago, and different. Several gaps listed here then are now answered — earnings quality, which part of supply moved, whether deposits funded the buying, the Dutch transition, the join itself. What remains:

  • Who, inside the household line, actually bought. The Fed computes it as a residual. Its issuer side is now attributed to the wrapper; its holder side cannot be split by legal entity on free data, and the last candidate route closed negative.
  • The last of earnings quality. Depreciation lives, buybacks and the 2017 tax cut are now tested — the tax cut is about a tenth of earnings growth. Stock-based compensation and R&D turned out to be moot on the as-reported series (C-095). Revenue-recognition timing is what remains.
  • What the multiple’s resilience was made of. Most of the measured “compression” is the rate rise restated (C-102). Of what is left, 36–60% is not explained by growth expectations, depending on the rate basis (C-104). A third premium series that does not share a bond leg would settle it.
  • Whether the off-balance-sheet lease stack is fragile. A different question from the goal's, but the obvious next one — and two of its tests fall on 30 September.
  • Quarter-end funding stress. June showed none. September is the next test: practitioner chatter on 25 September and 1 October, scored against the 30 September prints.
  • Retirement flows after 2023. Official flow data stops there; only levels run to 2025, and the two must never be paired.

10 — confidence

What I would and would not bet on

These are credences, not proofs — surviving an attack is a fact about the attack. High on the earnings share and on the wrapper reclassification — both measured, and the ETF figure matched by an independent census. Medium, and lower than it reads, on the premium story: what is measured is that the earnings yield did not follow real yields up; three premium constructions agree on direction for a shared structural reason, not independently. Low, and explicitly bounded, on causation: there is no aggregate estimate, and §05 says why four times over.

Would. Earnings did most of the work, and it is mostly real profit. The multiple held up while real yields rose, and has partly given that back. Operating companies retired equity on net while the ETF layer grew. ETF buying and mutual-fund selling largely offset. Collateral did not carry this repricing, and shadow money did not in 2024.

Would not. Any multiplier. Any statement that flows caused a specific share of the repricing. Any split of the household line by wealth group or buyer type. A point estimate for buybacks' share. Anything about retirement flows after 2023. That the premium was “cheaper risk”. That bank lending funds the build-out at the scale of its headline figure.

11 — method

Why so much keeps getting retracted

Corrections went from 93 to 119 in four days. Almost none were arithmetic errors — nearly every figure in them reproduced exactly. They fall into five shapes, and each now has a guard.

1 · Caught from outside, never from inside

The largest corrections — the ETF wrapper, the risk–growth split — came from adversarial review by a second model, not from internal reading. Internal review reads the same reasoning that produced the error and agrees with it. Outside attack stays the highest-yield activity in the programme.

2 · Propagation, not detection

Several corrections were registered correctly while a second copy of the dead claim lived on elsewhere — in the synthesis, in a pointer file, and on this page. An edit made in place without a correction number is invisible to the checks. Every copy of a number is a place it can go stale; the answer document now restates almost none, and this page is scanned before it is republished.

3 · Tests that could not see their own hypothesis

Three designs returned nulls that were written up as rejections, when none could have detected the effect it was testing for. The minimum detectable effect is now computed before a design is commissioned — from a realised clustered standard error, not a formula that counts rows — and a kill switch on an underpowered test no longer counts as a result.

4 · Checks that could not fail

A decomposition’s “sum check: exact” was exact because one term had been solved for as the remainder; measured directly, that term was 7.6% different. A validation reporting “every year within tolerance, worst year 2019” was an algebraic identity. The corrections gate itself went blind to every correction numbered above 99 and kept printing green. A check that cannot fail is not a weak check — it is the absence of one. Residuals are now measured a second way where possible, and the gap is reported.

5 · Summaries stronger than their sources

Four times in two days a careful source document hedged a finding and the one-line summary standing on it did not — the shadow-money verdict, the premium, the pension flows, the bank-credit figure. The research was sound; the compression was not. The principal’s ruling of 22 September — everything is a hypothesis until proven — now grades every claim, and this page carries the grades.

The guard rails: the principal's statements recorded verbatim and never paraphrased; a corrections register with machine-enforced bans on dead phrasings, read by its pass lines rather than its exit code; every outside number re-derived locally before it is believed; and two agents that hand over after every piece of work with an integrity stamp, so that neither works from a stale copy of the record.
