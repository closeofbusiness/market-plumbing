## 2026-07-12 — You Can Lead a Horse to Water
- message_id: 19f56c621dfa7e38 | kind: essay | text: full
- thesis: New academic research, especially Hannah Unterberg's award-winning paper, empirically confirms that passive/index fund flows — not any decline in active-manager skill — mechanically penalize active stock-picking (Active Share) since 2010, vindicating a mechanism Green proposed years ago that the FT's Robin Wigglesworth dismissed in 2020. Green argues six separate strands of research now jointly identify one mechanism running from passive ownership to mega-cap price distortion, and that the 2022 sell-off was this mechanism running in reverse via forced rebalancing.
- channels:
  - (d) market structure and flows — core subject: cap-weighted index inflows, 401(k)/target-date rebalancing, and ETF/index ownership mechanically move prices independent of any view on value.
  - P — central claim: a "cap-weight concentration premium" flow effect (not fundamentals or skill) is inflating the largest/most-overvalued names, which he calls "stored energy, not banked return."
  - (e) fundamentals — invoked as the rival explanation he argues against (2022 as a "clean fundamentals" rate-repricing story; "paradox of skill" as an alternative reason for active underperformance), both of which he says the evidence rejects.
  - (f) other — market microstructure/limits-to-arbitrage: record market-maker (Citadel Securities et al.) profits from facilitating retail noise trading, plus "non-ergodic" ruin risk that permanently removes correcting capital, explain why the mispricing isn't arbitraged away.
- mechanism claims:
  - Cap-weighting sends the largest dollar share of every new passive inflow to the already-largest constituents, mechanically lifting their price and hence their claim on the next dollar in (a self-reinforcing "price-weight spiral").
  - Unterberg's fund-level flow-induced-demand measure, once controlled for, makes the post-2010 Active-Share performance penalty statistically vanish — the penalty loads onto flows, not skill.
  - The Active-Share penalty is concentrated in the first three trading days of the month, when mechanical 401(k) paycheck contributions land — a timing signature Green says only a mechanical-flow story, not a skill/competition story, can produce.
  - Six literatures form "one machine": incidence (Jiang/Vayanos/Zheng: flows hit largest, most-overvalued names hardest), elasticity (Haddad/Huebner/Loualiche: active managers' strategic response offsets only ~two-thirds of flow impact), clearing (Sammon & Shim: issuers, not arbitrageurs, supply shares to meet passive demand, mainly via stock-compensation issuance), information (Coles/Heath/Ringgenberg: higher index ownership causally reduces analyst coverage/research), returns (Unterberg), and stability (Green/Krishnan/Sturm: above a calibrated passive-share threshold, boom-bust volatility becomes endogenous).
  - The 2022 decline is reinterpreted as the mechanism in reverse: Fed rate surprises forced fixed-allocation rebalancers (pensions, target-date funds) to sell equities and buy bonds, hitting mega-caps hardest; evidence comes from dual-share-class firms where the class more heavily held by rebalancers fell more despite identical fundamentals (Lu & Wu).
  - Rejects the "poker table"/paradox-of-skill explanation (passive drove out unsophisticated traders, leaving only skilled "sharks" competing away alpha): retail noise trading is at record highs, but profits are captured by market-making intermediaries facilitating that flow, not by "corrector" active managers, who are instead starved of capital.
  - Corrective/short capital cannot arbitrage away flow-driven mispricing because it faces redemptions, benchmark constraints, and "non-ergodic" ruin (one bad trajectory, e.g. Melvin Capital's GameStop short, permanently removes that capacity rather than being averaged away across the market).
  - Argues "markets got more efficient" (active managers can't beat the index) is observationally identical to "efficiency by exhaustion" (correctors defunded, not competed away); only flow-decomposition distinguishes the two, and he says the data points to exhaustion.

## 2026-07-26 — A Semi-Theory of Almost Everything
- message_id: 19f9e82f9c32e743 | kind: essay | text: full
- thesis: Green argues that a large share of Q2 2026's extreme semiconductor rally and July reversal was mechanical rather than fundamental: a decade-long "recycling" behavior by leveraged-ETF holders (profit-skimming that offset the funds' own forced rebalancing) broke down abruptly in April 2026, turning a self-neutralizing complex into a momentum-injecting one, while a separate, newly launched price-insensitive sector ETF (DRAM) independently chose the winning stock (Micron). A calibrated mechanical simulation with no fundamentals or sentiment reproduces roughly 60% of the volatility-regime change.
- channels:
  - (c) collateral channel — dealer total-return-swap capacity limits push funds toward buying call options, forcing dealer delta-hedging that amplifies "vol-up, spot-up" melt-ups; the SOXS/SOXL pair-decay short trade is margin/collateral-constrained exactly when volatility (its payoff driver) rises.
  - (d) market structure and flows — the core subject: daily leveraged-ETF rebalancing mechanics, "flow elasticity" of holders, a new sector ETF's price-insensitive creation flow, single-stock levered wrapper launch dynamics, and spillover into Korean-listed shares via Korea's own new leveraged products.
  - (e) fundamentals — explicitly partly conceded: "the memory cycle is genuinely extraordinary" and "fundamentals wrote the script," but he argues flows ("the flows performed it") determined the size, timing, volatility and choice of winning stock.
  - P — central: decomposes what actually set semiconductor-sector and single-stock prices this quarter, attributing roughly 40% of the price move and roughly 60% of the change in "the tape's character" (volatility/persistence) to measurable mechanical flows.
- mechanism claims:
  - Leveraged ETFs must trade daily to hold a constant leverage multiple; required rebalance size scales as L(L-1) x assets x return (coefficient 6 for a 3x fund), and inverse/bear funds trade in the same momentum direction as bull funds rather than offsetting them, so the whole levered complex is structurally pro-cyclical.
  - Volatility drag on a levered fund's growth rate scales with L^2 x variance/2, so a 3x fund's breakeven return rises from ~14.4%/year at the SOX's 20-year volatility (31%) to ~54%/year at this spring's 60% volatility.
  - For roughly a decade this pro-cyclical rebalancing was offset by holders' own profit-skimming (negative "flow elasticity" — redeeming shares as the fund rises, adding as it falls), so the levered complex's net market footprint was historically small or negative.
  - In April 2026 that skimming behavior weakened abruptly (measured via SOXL's elasticity), flipping the complex from net seller-into-rallies to net buyer, injecting a large net momentum flow into the tape within weeks and materially raising realized volatility and the frequency of >3% trading days.
  - A separately launched sector ETF (Roundhill's DRAM, launched April 2, 2026) generated "return-decoupled" demand — buying regardless of the day's price direction, concentrated almost entirely in three stocks (Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron) — which he argues is what selected Micron as the quarter's biggest winner, while Nvidia's older, still-skimming wrapper and zero DRAM weight left it out of the squeeze.
  - Single-stock leveraged wrapper ETFs' holder behavior is predicted almost entirely by launch date: pre-2026 wrappers show profit-skimming (negative elasticity); 2026-launched wrappers show zero/positive elasticity ("no recycling, all reinforcement") because their holders have no embedded gains to trim yet.
  - A wave of new single-stock wrapper launches (dominated by one issuer, "Corgi") reflects the ETF industry's fee-driven supply economics ("what ticker might catch flow") rather than investor demand; most gather negligible assets.
  - Rapid fund asset growth strains dealer swap-capacity limits under ISDA agreements, pushing funds toward buying call options instead, which forces dealers to hedge by buying more of the underlying stock as it rises — a mechanical channel for melt-ups.
  - The SOXL/SOXS "pair-decay harvest" trade (shorting both legs to collect volatility decay) depends on continuous fresh capital flowing into the structurally money-losing bear fund, is collateral/margin-constrained precisely when volatility (its profit driver) rises, and can be forced to cover (buy) into a melt-up — a stabilizer that itself fails under stress.
  - Roughly half of DRAM's exposure lands on Korea-listed Samsung/SK Hynix; Korea's own newly launched domestic single-stock leveraged ETFs (retail-dominated) coincided with an unprecedented number of KOSPI circuit breakers and sidecars in 2026, illustrating a US-listed product exporting volatility to a market with no jurisdiction over it.
  - A mechanical agent-based simulation (fitted only on pre-April data, no sentiment/valuation) reproduces about 60% of the post-April volatility increase; a counterfactual using the old, higher-skimming elasticity turns the actual +60% April-to-late-July rally into a modeled +35% rally, implying roughly two-fifths of the move was flow-amplification.
  - Three of his own hypotheses failed: no stock-level cross-sectional link between wrapper AUM/volume and idiosyncratic volatility increase versus a six-stock control group; no swollen closing-auction pattern in Micron (DRAM's buying is transmitted via swaps/dealers spread through the day, not stamped at the close); and the model leaves an unexplained weekly mean-reversion gap he speculates (unconfirmed) may be trend-following CTAs — which he calls "the best argument against the strongest version of my own thesis."
  - Explicitly concedes the model explains "transmission and magnitude, not ignition": it does not explain why holders' skimming behavior changed in April, only what followed mechanically once it did.

## 2026-08-02 — Maid in Japan
- message_id: 19fc29b73a07ca97 | kind: essay | text: full
- thesis: Long-term government bond yields are rising together across the US, Japan, Germany, Britain and Australia not primarily because of each country's local fiscal story, but because two large, return-insensitive buyers — central banks (winding down QE-era duration absorption) and Japanese life insurers (historically pushed abroad by yield-curve control) — are stepping back at the same time. Japanese insurers in particular are pulling back because domestic JGB yields now clear their yen liabilities without the cost of hedging foreign bonds, thinning the dependable buyer base for long-duration debt everywhere.
- channels:
  - (a) direct money creation — central-bank balance sheets are one of the "common forces": Fed reserve growth is now in short bills not duration, ECB reinvestment has stopped, and BOJ purchases are on a shrinking schedule.
  - (d) market structure and flows — the core mechanism is a change in the marginal buyer/"foreign flows" for long-duration government debt (Japanese insurers) rather than a change in fundamentals.
  - (e) fundamentals — presented as rival explanations he weighs and partly discounts: local fiscal stories, general risk/uncertainty premium, term premium (cites the NY Fed's ACM model), inflation expectations, and an oil-price shock.
  - (c) collateral channel — lighter touch: notes hedge funds often hold long Treasuries "with borrowed money," and that currency-hedge rolls (forwards/swaps) are the financing mechanism connecting Japanese and US bond demand.
  - F — explicit: names "the AI borrowing boom — a flood of corporate debt funding data centers" as a genuine common demand shock competing with governments for the same capital pool, to be developed further in a companion essay.
  - P — bond prices/yields across five developed markets are the direct subject; he also flags a follow-on essay on what this means for who buys US assets, including equities, next.
- mechanism claims:
  - Uses a "musical chairs" metaphor: central banks, regulation and Japan's own policy choices "arrange the chairs" (bond supply and the pool of must-hold buyers) even though the final price is set by an unmanaged scramble — so "markets set rates" is true but incomplete as a description.
  - The degree of cross-country synchronization in the 2026 bond selloff (five countries at generational-extreme yields at once) is, in his view, too large to be explained by each country's distinct local story; a shared component is required.
  - Japanese insurers' foreign-bond economics: after currency hedging, a yen-based buyer of Treasuries earns roughly Japanese short rates plus the US yield curve's slope, not the headline US yield — so a Bank of Japan rate hike makes dollar-hedging cheaper, not more expensive, contradicting the popular "hedging costs are driving Japan away" story.
  - The actual driver is that domestic JGB yields have risen enough to cover Japanese insurers' yen liabilities outright, without hedging cost or currency risk, so foreign bonds must now offer substantially more than before to be worth holding — analogous to how higher US rates triggered US pension de-risking into bonds in 2022-23.
  - This withdrawal shows up mainly as insurers not reinvesting maturing bonds and not participating in new issuance, rather than as outright selling — which is why visible "selling" data looks modest even as yields reprice in anticipation of future auctions.
  - Symmetric hedge effect: the same currency-hedge arithmetic that now penalizes Japanese buyers of Treasuries rewards non-Japanese buyers of JGBs, pulling in "carry" money that cushions Japan's repricing without reversing it, and can decouple currency moves (a weak yen) from the underlying hedged-bond-flow story.
  - Frames this as "the regime, not the trade": three decades of low global long rates rested partly on Japan's financial repression pushing its institutions into foreign bond markets, functioning as a hidden subsidy to G7 deficit financing; that subsidy is ending as BOJ normalizes and JGBs again offer competitive yields.
  - Proposes five falsifiable tests of the thesis (lead-lag around Japan-specific news; geographic/maturity concentration matching Japan's historical ownership footprint; insurers' published semi-annual allocation plans; rolling foreign buying of Japan's longest bonds; and a two-way policy experiment involving BOJ yield-capping or US Treasury buybacks/issuance cuts), each "checkable within quarters."
  - Explicitly reports one early test result against himself: reporting on a key date (May 18) shows Japanese yields following the prior day's Treasury move rather than leading it, which he calls a "mixed" result for his own thesis.

- thesis: Green announces he is leaving Simplify Asset Management to found and run (as CEO/CIO) Tier1 Alpha Asset Management, a new "passive-aware" investment firm built to convert his decade of passive-investing/market-structure research into investable products that measure continuous, security-level mechanical fund flows (not just discrete index-rebalance events) and tilt modestly toward where measured passive demand exceeds available liquidity. - mechanism claims:
  - Argues passive/mechanical investing simplified individual investment decisions (lower fees, better diversification) while degrading the market's collective functions (price discovery, capital allocation, shock absorption) — an externality no individual household internalizes.
  - Reiterates that benchmark-relative active underperformance is, in his view, partly a consequence of mechanical flows rather than purely a skill/competition story, citing Unterberg's paper as vindicating a hypothesis he made a decade earlier.
  - Describes a "second-order" effect: as passive flows become more predictable, sophisticated capital can profit more from anticipating what mechanical buyers/sellers must do than from fundamental analysis — so arbitrage capital may trade with a flow-driven distortion (front-running it) rather than against it, amplifying rather than correcting the mispricing.
  - Tier1 Alpha's approach extends index-rebalance arbitrage logic (citing Lasse Pedersen) to continuous, small, persistent daily flows into passive vehicles, aggregated across "thousands of ETFs" and "thousands of individual stocks."
  - Flagship product design: holds essentially the same securities as a leading large-cap benchmark, with a modest, systematic tilt toward stocks where measured passive-demand-to-liquidity mismatch is greatest, built to fit within institutional tracking-error budgets so it can sit in a portfolio's core rather than as a satellite allocation.
  - Argues incumbents with the greatest ability to commercialize passive-impact research also have the greatest institutional interest in not questioning the passive/benchmark complex, which is why (he says) "not one firm was built to incorporate these insights" despite a large academic literature.
  - Frames the firm's durability as resting on flows originating outside any trade (payroll rules, benchmark mandates, statutory retirement structures) that persist even once publicly known, analogous to public index-methodology-based index arbitrage; distinguishes this explicitly from illegal front-running of non-public customer orders.
  - Uses Vanguard's history as a cautionary parallel: credits Bogle's 1977 no-load conversion as the source of Vanguard's edge, but criticizes Vanguard's own later rent-seeking — a 2003-2023 patent on the ETF-as-share-class structure, and a 2020 institutional target-date fund migration that pushed large taxable capital-gains distributions onto remaining retail investors — as a model of what a dominant, scaled incumbent can become, and pre-commits Tier1 to specific anti-rent commitments ("The Covenant": no shelf fees, placement fees, or platform-access tolls).

## 2026-08-16 — Anchors Aweigh, My Boys
- message_id: 1a00a58af9bb2e5c | kind: essay | text: full
- thesis: Opens with a brief Tier1 Alpha business update (processing speed, upcoming ETF registrations, and notice that he is pausing market writing for five weeks for a society-focused book series), then delivers Part II of the Japan/bond-market argument: with Japan's insurers and central banks stepping back (Part I), none of America's own liability-driven buyers — corporate pensions, public pensions, or the insurance/reinsurance complex — can replace them, leaving price-insensitive 401(k) bond-index flows and a bills-only Fed as the main buyers of long-duration debt. He argues this buyer-composition shift, not an AI-driven "capital shortage," best explains rising long yields, citing hyperscaler credit stress as evidence against the popular AI-crowding-out story.
- channels:
  - (a) direct money creation — the Fed ended balance-sheet runoff and is growing it again only via short-term bills, not duration; Treasury is financing its funding gap almost entirely with bills rather than long bonds.
  - (b) shadow banking — the insurance/reinsurance complex that inherited pension liabilities is increasingly PE-owned, backs annuities with private credit, and cedes risk to offshore affiliated reinsurers, redirecting liability-driven capital away from government bonds.
  - (c) collateral channel — hyperscaler corporate debt has functioned as a near-Treasury-substitute safe asset; its credit stress (widening CDS, avoidance of long maturities) is used as the diagnostic; mortgage-market convexity forces duration-hedgers to buy into rate rallies, a hedging feedback loop.
  - (d) market structure and flows — the core mechanism: market-cap-weighted bond-index flows (401(k)/target-date contributions) buy "by formula" regardless of term premium, and their DV01 contribution shrinks exactly when the government most needs long-duration buyers.
  - (e) fundamentals — the rival "AI capital shortage lifting the return on capital" explanation for high yields is examined and rejected using credit-market evidence; he also weighs inflation-expectations vs. term-premium decompositions.
  - F — explicit and central: directly rebuts the consensus view that AI data-center borrowing is crowding out the bond market, using hyperscaler bond issuance and CDS data as the test.
  - P — direct: the entire piece is about what sets US (and global) long-term bond prices/yields and the term premium.
- mechanism claims:
  - The term premium is framed as the price charged by "the marginal buyer" for absorbing the residual: new issuance minus Japan's exiting bid minus the Fed minus what price-insensitive index formulas buy automatically.
  - Bond-index funds are market-value-weighted, so a bond's index weight (and DV01 contribution) shrinks as its price falls — meaning the automatic 401(k) bid mechanically supplies the least interest-rate-risk absorption exactly when the government's cheap, high-duration long paper most needs buyers.
  - Illustrates with real-bond DV01 arithmetic: ultra-low-coupon pandemic-era 30-year Treasuries (e.g., the 1.25% of 2050, trading near 47 cents) carry extreme duration density (~$1,800 of DV01 per $1mn market value) despite tiny index weight; bonds trading below 75 cents are about 35% of the long-bond sleeve's market value but about 41% of its risk.
  - Surveys three domestic pools that might have replaced Japan as a liability-driven buyer and argues none can: corporate pension plans already de-risked into bonds in 2022 when rising rates pushed funded ratios over 100% and largely finished by 2023; public pensions (a larger ~$6 trillion pool) discount liabilities at an assumed ~6.8% return rather than market yields, so higher rates create no incentive to shift, and they are additionally trapped holding illiquid, appraisal-priced private equity/credit/venture allocations; the insurance/reinsurance complex that inherited transferred pension liabilities is increasingly PE-owned and redirected into private credit and offshore affiliated reinsurance rather than government bonds.
  - Proposes, explicitly as "a hypothesis, not a conclusion," that this now-departed consortium of price-indifferent buyers (Japan, corporate pensions, QE) may have artificially deepened the 2022-23 yield-curve inversion, meaning the recession the inversion predicted may never have been truly signaled by growth expectations.
  - Estimates Japan's historically missing annual bid at roughly $150-250 billion of long-dated foreign sovereign purchases (about $180-300 million of DV01 per basis point per year), explicitly "with error bars," and argues the resulting term-premium adjustment is non-linear: it can "coil" quietly while retirement inflows outrun new supply, then "gap" suddenly (citing May 18 and July 29, 2026 as gap events).
  - Notes target-date fund rebalancing, a normal stabilizer, fails exactly when stocks and bonds fall together (as on July 29, 2026), because the return-spread that drives the rebalancing trade disappears.
  - Describes a parallel mortgage-market "barbell": trillions in pandemic-era sub-3% mortgages are rate-insensitive, while roughly $5 trillion originated at higher rates could see large-scale refinancing and duration compression if long rates fall meaningfully, forcing MBS hedgers to buy into the rally — a convexity "ratchet" that could amplify a rate decline.
  - Central rebuttal of the "AI is crowding out the bond market" consensus story: hyperscaler debt has functioned as a quasi-Treasury-substitute (citing Lira Mota's research), but this privilege is fading — record hyperscaler bond issuance is being met with widening CDS spreads and buyer avoidance of long maturities, which he interprets as "a shortage of buyers willing to own duration," not a shortage of safe assets — evidence he says undercuts a fundamentals-driven capital-shortage story and instead supports his buyer-composition account.
  - Frames current Fed/Treasury policy as "the worst possible pairing": Treasury finances its funding gap almost entirely with bills that fall below bond-index funds' one-year eligibility floor (so its most reliable automatic buyer structurally cannot absorb this issuance), while the Fed faces pressure to treat what looks like a term-premium shock as though it were an inflation-expectations shock.
  - Speculates (explicitly labeled as unconfirmed personal intuition) that the US Treasury's joint early-August yen intervention with Japan may be intended to discourage US investors from rotating into yen-denominated assets by increasing currency volatility.
  - Proposes a falsifiable test of his own thesis: if long-dated inflation compensation (breakevens, inflation swaps) stays contained, that supports a term-premium/flow explanation over an inflation-fear explanation; if they rise, the traditional inflation-fear reading regains support.
