## 2026-08-30 — The Vibecession Was Real
- message_id: 1a052a002f28e715 | kind: essay | text: full
- thesis: Green argues the gap between strong macro aggregates and dismal household sentiment (the "Vibecession") is real information, not consumer irrationality or partisan bias, because national accounts, surveys, and balance sheets measure different things — dollars, people, and net worth respectively — in an economy that has become "K-shaped."   - P — indirect but central: the essay's distributional argument turns on asset-price appreciation (housing, equities) enriching incumbent owners while raising the entry price for new buyers/savers.
  - F, I — not addressed in this post.
- mechanism claims:
  - Opens with editorializing (not a market mechanism) that Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole turn partly reflects a leadership/authority dynamic with a skeptical, technically-expert FOMC room.
  - Distinguishes inflation (rate of change, "speedometer") from the price level (cumulative change, "odometer"): returning inflation to 2% does not undo the accumulated price-level rise households already absorbed.
  - "K-shaped economy": an identical average change can conceal opposite paths for different households, because the same rate or asset-price move (e.g., higher rates, rising home prices) benefits some balance sheets and hurts others, and each period's outcome resets the next period's starting point.
  - "Dollars vote differently than people": national spending/GDP data are dollar-weighted, so resilient aggregate consumption can coexist with widespread reported household distress, because a wealthier minority contributes disproportionately to spending.
  - Older/incumbent households' wealth reflects not just savings discipline but a multi-decade regime of falling discount rates and growing passive retirement demand (DB-to-DC pension shift), which he ties to the Gabaix-Koijen "inelastic markets" caution against assuming price-insensitive demand is harmlessly absorbed.
  - Mortgage rate "lock-in": a large share of homeowners hold sub-4% fixed mortgages from the low-rate era, which keeps them from selling (suppressing housing supply/turnover) while new buyers face both higher prices and higher financing costs — illustrated with a hypothetical monthly-payment comparison.
  - On inflation's causes, partially disputes Warsh's "sits squarely with the central bank" framing, citing academic decompositions attributing the pandemic-era surge to a shifting mix of supply shocks, commodity prices, and firms exploiting broken price reference points to reset prices permanently upward (illustrated via a GM inventory-management example).
  - Argues monetary restraint cannot create physical supply (housing, electricity, resilient supply chains) and, applied to scarcity-driven inflation, mainly reallocates who can afford scarce goods rather than making them less scarce.
  - Calls for supplementing CPI/PCE with distributional and balance-sheet measures (e.g., a household cost-of-living index, survey data, balance-sheet data) rather than treating any single aggregate as authoritative.

## 2026-09-06 — The Quiet Turning
- message_id: 1a0774d8b2512f12 | kind: essay | text: full
- thesis: Green advances a hypothesis, not a claim of victory, that AI-assisted verification is sharply lowering the cost of interrogating claims and reconstructing relationships/networks, which over time could raise the reputational and behavioral cost of brazen dishonesty ("grift") even though current fraud-loss statistics have not yet turned. He frames the last decade as one where enforcement capacity eroded until openness about wrongdoing itself became a status signal, and grounds the argument in the economics of costly information and fraud.
- channels: not applicable — this essay is about epistemics, trust, verification, and fraud/AI, not about money creation, shadow banking, collateral, market structure/flows, or price fundamentals. None of (a)-(f) meaningfully apply, and it is not about P, F, or I; the only market-adjacent aside is his personal disclosure that he is currently launching an investment firm (Tier1 Alpha Asset Management), which is a business note, not an argument about asset-price or funding channels. Flagging this explicitly since it is a departure from the other posts in this batch.
- mechanism claims:
  - Opens with an anecdote: a young entrepreneur advises him to inflate his firm's "one terabyte" proprietary flows database to "a hundred terabytes" for pitch impact; Green refuses, calling it a lie rather than ordinary pitchcraft, and reflects that commercial culture (fundraising narratives, résumé inflation, engagement metrics) taught younger generations that exaggeration is normal and rewarded.
  - Central historical claim: over the past decade, enforcement capacity (inspectors general, newsrooms, compliance offices, independent analysts) was "defunded, captured, exhausted, and reassigned," so brazen wrongdoing increasingly signals untouchability rather than risking exposure.
  - Draws on information economics (Grossman-Stiglitz: information is costly to gather, so markets/norms cannot be perfectly self-policing) and Akerlof-Shiller's "Phishing for Phools" (profitable exploitable weaknesses attract organized exploitation once monitors disappear) to explain why brazen corruption can persist even when evidence is public.
  - Distinguishes concealed fraud (survives because victims can't cheaply verify) from brazen corruption (survives because everyone can see it and no penalty follows); argues cheaper information mainly fixes the first problem, and helps the second only if exposure starts imposing real costs again.
  - Uses the 2009 film "The Invention of Lying" as a parable for trust-saturated institutions (no one develops a "verification reflex" until someone exploits it), and frames blockchain ("don't trust, verify") as society's response once institutional trust was depleted — but argues blockchain only validates narrow, pre-defined claims, whereas AI instead cheapens the labor of cross-referencing messy real-world evidence (filings, court records, biographies).
  - Cites an investigative journalist's AI-assisted document analysis (Nick Nemeth on insurance executive Dan Towriss) as a model of careful fact-vs-allegation verification.
  - Argues the deterrence channel is reputational/network-based: cheaper reconstruction of a person's associations (photos, endorsements, board seats) could make investors, brands, and donors more cautious about association with bad actors, though he expects the effect to be uneven since "reputation is tribal."
  - Explicitly weighs the opposing dynamic: the same AI tools that cheapen verification also cheapen large-scale personalized scamming (synthetic identities, testimonials, voices), so aggregate fraud can keep rising even as individual warning quality improves, if attacker scale/incentives currently outrun checkers.
