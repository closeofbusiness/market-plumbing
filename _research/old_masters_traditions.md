## Schumpeter: credit creation as the monetary complement of innovation (Theory of Economic Development 1911/1934; Business Cycles 1939; Das Wesen des Geldes, written c.1930-35, pub. 1970; History of Economic Analysis 1954)

**Core mechanism.** In the circular flow every purchase is financed out of receipts from a prior sale and all factors are already employed; there is no idle fund waiting to be lent. Development — carrying out a new combination — requires command over factors *before* any corresponding output or receipt exists, so the entrepreneur must first become a debtor, and the banker supplies not intermediated savings but a newly created deposit: "credit is essentially the creation of purchasing power for the purpose of transferring it to the entrepreneur, but not simply the transfer of existing purchasing power" (TED 1934, p.107). That new claim bids specific factors away from their accustomed uses; the resulting rise in those factors' prices ("credit inflation") is the transmission mechanism, and the real loss imposed on incumbent claimants to the same factors is the forced saving (TED p.109, where Schumpeter credits the term to Mises). If the innovation succeeds, repayment out of entrepreneurial profit destroys the deposits and the price effect reverses (Schumpeter's "autodeflation"); if it does not, the claims remain as debts against output that never arrived, and the secondary wave of speculative credit that followed the primary one liquidates into debt deflation (Business Cycles 1939, pp.152-53). The searchable signature is therefore: a rise in newly created claims-to-resources, and in the relative price of the specific factors the innovation needs, with no prior rise in the saving rate and no necessary movement in broad money.

**On investment without prior saving.** Directly: yes, and Schumpeter is the clearest statement of how. Investment exceeds prior saving whenever some balance sheet is willing to create a claim on resources in advance of the output that will validate it. The bank is the paradigm case because its liability circulates as money, but the general form is a *claim*, not a deposit — which is why Schumpeter's late work (HEA p.686) recommends treating finance as a clearing system in which money payments are a special case. Ex post, S will equal I in the national accounts; that identity is satisfied by the incomes the investment itself generates, not by any prior fund, and it tells you nothing about the financing. Applied to the AI cycle, the honest split is this: about half the global data-centre capex through 2028 is projected to come from hyperscaler operating cash flow (~$1.4tn of ~$2.9tn, Morgan Stanley via SFA) — that is prior corporate saving and it is the circular flow, not development in Schumpeter's sense. The external half is the Schumpeterian part, and it is arriving on a datable schedule: incremental debt went from 9% of capex in FY24 to 32% LTM mid-2026 (FactSet); aggregate free cash flow across the five hyperscalers crosses zero around Q3 2026 (Epoch AI, from XBRL). Within that external half, corporate bonds (>$100bn gross in 2025, BIS QR Box A) and private credit funds (closed-end, >$200bn outstanding to AI-related firms, BIS Bulletin 120) transfer existing deposits rather than create new ones — they are Wicksellian intermediation of pre-existing balances. Genuine deposit creation enters through the bank lines that fund those vehicles and funds, visible in H.8 loans to non-depository financial institutions ($2,016.1bn, 5 Aug 2026, subject to a 2026 reclassification break). Alphabet's $80bn June 2026 equity raise, including a $10bn private placement with Berkshire, is the exact opposite case: it mobilises hoarded balances, and Schumpeter would classify it as dishoarding, not credit creation. So the correct answer for the operator is not 'the AI trade is money creation' but 'the AI trade is claim creation, of which money creation is a minority component, and the share is rising on a measurable schedule.'

**On invisibility to aggregates.** Schumpeter's answer is that the monetary aggregate is the wrong instrument, and he says so explicitly: it is not clear that the useful method is to start from coin and add credit; better to treat capitalist finance as a clearing system that cancels claims and carries differences forward, so that money payments are a special case of no fundamental importance (HEA 1954, p.686). Purchasing power that commands resources but never appears in M1-M3 takes several concrete forms in this cycle, and each is separately measurable. First, off-balance-sheet SPV and JV debt raised by private placement and serviced by lease cash flows, with the hyperscaler holding a minority stake and providing offtake commitments and guarantees — BIS QR March 2026 Box A calls these obligations 'economically akin to debt' and names the practice shadow borrowing. Second, capacity offtake and unconditional purchase obligations, which are a forward command over compute and power with no financing line at all. Third, vendor arrangements — supplier equity stakes, backstops and guarantees — which transfer a supplier's own market capitalisation into a customer's spending capacity without any bank involvement. Fourth, intra-group credit and prepayments. Fifth, the leverage embedded inside the private credit funds and insurers holding the paper, which is bank credit at one remove and shows up in H.8's NDFI line rather than in any measure of corporate borrowing. None of these expand M2. All of them expand the volume of claims outstanding against a given flow of current output, which is Schumpeter's actual variable. The operationally useful reformulation for a structurer is: stop asking whether money grew and start measuring newly created claims-to-resources per unit of current output, aggregating SPV debt, lease and purchase commitments, guarantees, and bank commitments to NBFIs, and accept that this quantity is only recoverable from filings and supervisory data, never from the monetary statistics.

**On the illusion of wealth.** Schumpeter is weakest here and should not be stretched. He has a theory of factor prices under credit inflation, not a theory of asset valuation, and the honest statement is that the paper-versus-realisable-value gap has to be imported from Fisher, Minsky, and the inelastic-markets literature rather than derived from him. What he does contribute is three things. First, the asymmetry: created deposits are destroyed on repayment, so a boom that never showed up in the aggregates on the way up can still produce contraction on the way down — his autodeflation. Second, the recognition that the credit which follows the innovation is not the same as the credit which funded it; the secondary wave finances speculation and, unchecked, ends in insolvency and debt deflation (Business Cycles 1939, pp.152-53). The circular arrangements around Nvidia — supplier guarantees, equity stakes in customers and in the infrastructure companies serving them — are secondary-wave objects in his taxonomy, not primary financing of a new combination. Third, and most usable, the observation that the validity of the created claim depends entirely on the innovation delivering output that the market pays for; until then the claim is a bet, and the collateral behind it is the very asset the bet built. The measurable version of that today is not equity multiples but depreciation: reported useful lives of 4-6 years against an annual product cadence, with Goldman's sensitivity putting cumulative 2026-31 depreciation at ~$3tn on a five-year life versus ~$4tn on three years. That $1tn is unrealised write-down sitting inside a footnote assumption, and it is the closest thing in the accounts to a quantified illusion. A second, sharper indicator comes from BIS Bulletin 120: private-credit spreads to AI borrowers (6.2pp) are statistically indistinguishable from spreads to everyone else (6.1pp), with similar secured shares and maturities, while equity valuations imply outsized returns. Either the lenders are wrong or the equity holders are; the two markets cannot both be right, and that disagreement is priced, published, and trackable.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) Why investment can surge with no change in the saving rate and no acceleration in M2: the financing constraint is the willingness of some balance sheet to create a claim, not the prior existence of a fund. Borio and Disyatat's saving-versus-financing distinction (BIS WP 346) is Schumpeter 1911 restated in flow-of-funds language, and the mainstream S=I identity is silent on exactly the question the operator is asking. (2) Why the price effect appears in specific factor prices — PJM capacity at $329.17/MW-day, HBM and DRAM contract prices, electrical-contractor wages, transformer and turbine lead times — rather than in the CPI: newly created purchasing power is directed at a narrow set of inputs, so the inflation is relative, not general, and a headline-index-based test of "is there too much money" will always return no. (3) Why the boom is lumpy and clustered rather than a smooth response to a lower cost of capital: innovation arrives in clusters and the credit expansion is its monetary shadow, not an independent impulse. (4) Why the downswing is not symmetric with the upswing: repayment destroys the deposits that were created (autodeflation), so the same aggregate that never rose on the way up can fall on the way down, while the collateral being liquidated is the very asset the credit built. Standard neoclassical investment theory, which treats the marginal product of capital against a market interest rate, cannot generate any of these; it also has no way of saying who pays for the reallocation in real time, which is precisely what forced saving names.

**Ideology flagged.** Several parts, and they should be quarantined from the mechanism. (1) The entrepreneur as an exogenous, heroic, quasi-aristocratic type is sociology, not economics; it is unfalsifiable because any observed investment surge can be relabelled a new combination after the fact. (2) The three-cycle Kondratieff-Juglar-Kitchin schema in Business Cycles is imposed on the data rather than derived from it; Kuznets' 1940 AER review is the canonical demolition and it has never been answered. (3) The claim that the credit machine is *designed* to serve the productive apparatus and to punish other uses (BC p.153) is a functionalist assertion. The credit-allocation literature (Bezemer et al. 2023; Cecchetti-Kharroubi BIS WP 490) shows credit systematically flows to pledgeable collateral, which is the opposite of what innovation finance requires. Schumpeter's escape clause — that the design can be altered — makes the proposition unfalsifiable as stated. (4) Forced saving is presented as legitimate because development follows. That is a normative judgement wearing analytical clothes; Schumpeter supplies no welfare criterion, no compensation principle, and no account of what happens if the innovation fails and the transfer is never validated by output. (5) 'Creation out of nothing' is true of one bank's ledger and misleading about system constraints — reserves, capital and liquidity requirements, and the borrower's obligation to settle in something the bank cannot itself manufacture. The popular-Austrian and popular-MMT uses of the phrase both drop the constraint; Schumpeter did not, but his rhetoric invites the error. (6) The upswing/repayment/autodeflation cycle assumes loans are repaid out of innovation profit. There is no Schumpeterian theory of rollover, of debt that is refinanced rather than extinguished, or of the valuation of the assets pledged against it. Minsky (his student) and Fisher supply that; Schumpeter does not, and reading him as if he does is where the tradition slides into prophecy. (7) Most importantly for this project: Schumpeter has no theory of asset prices. He cannot deliver the 'illusion of wealth' result on his own resources, and any account that attributes it to him is importing it from elsewhere.

### Analysis

**1. What the structure actually says, and where.**

Schumpeter's architecture is two-part by construction. Chapter I of the *Theory of Economic Development* describes the circular flow, where output is financed out of the proceeds of last period's output; Chapter II introduces development as the discontinuous carrying out of new combinations, and Chapter III ("Credit and Capital") supplies the monetary complement. The load-bearing claims are narrow and precise. Credit is the creation of purchasing power, not the transfer of it (TED 1934, p.107). The entrepreneur is "the typical debtor in capitalist society" and becomes a debtor by necessity, not by accident (p.102). The banker is not an intermediary of the commodity purchasing power but a producer of it, and is therefore "the ephor of the exchange economy" (p.74), authorising new combinations in society's name. Schumpeter distinguishes "normal" credit — certificates for production already performed — from the "abnormal" credit that finances future goods (p.101). The essential function of credit is to let the entrepreneur withdraw producers' goods from their previous employments by exercising demand for them.

That withdrawal is the whole point, and it is where forced saving enters. Schumpeter took the term from Mises (TED p.109) and shares the mechanism with Hayek's 1932 QJE reconstruction of the doctrine's lineage from Bentham and Thornton: credit not backed by voluntary abstinence is spent on factors, factor prices rise, and incumbent claimants on those factors consume less in real terms than they intended. Schumpeter differs from the Austrians in valuation, not mechanics — for him this is the normal, productive way capitalism reallocates, not a distortion of an intertemporal price.

Late Schumpeter makes the methodological point that matters most for the operator's question. In *History of Economic Analysis* (p.686) he argues that starting from coin and treating credit as a substitute for it is analytically backwards; it may be preferable to treat capitalist finance as a clearing system that cancels claims and debts and carries forward differences, so that money payments are a special case of no fundamental importance — "a credit theory of money is possibly preferable to a monetary theory of credit." He faults Thornton through Mill for discovering credit structure but failing to build a systematic credit theory of money (p.687), and says credit creation "brings out the peculiar mechanism of saving and investment" characteristic of full capitalism (p.1080). The unfinished *Das Wesen des Geldes* (drafted c.1925-35, published 1970 ed. F.K. Mann) develops money as an institution and a social accounting system with banking as the central agency. This is the part of the tradition that speaks directly to "invisible to M1-M3": Schumpeter's own answer is that asking whether a monetary aggregate moved is asking the wrong question, because the analytically primary object is the volume of newly created claims on the social product, of which bank deposits are one legal form among several.

**2. What modern work has confirmed, refined, and refuted.**

*Confirmed, on mechanics.* The single-bank accounting Schumpeter asserted is now the official central-bank description: McLeay, Radia and Thomas, "Money creation in the modern economy," Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin 2014 Q1; Jakab and Kumhof, BoE Staff WP 529 (2015), "Banks are not intermediaries of loanable funds"; Werner (International Review of Financial Analysis 36, 2014) supplied the first direct empirical test on a live loan. Borio and Disyatat (BIS WP 346, 2011) generalised Schumpeter's distinction into the modern saving-versus-financing argument: gross financial flows bear no arithmetic relation to national-accounts saving, and the pre-crisis excess was one of financing elasticity, not of saving.

*Confirmed, on growth.* King and Levine (QJE 1993) titled their paper "Finance and Growth: Schumpeter Might Be Right," and the finance-growth literature ran on that premise for fifteen years.

*Refuted, or at least badly damaged, on allocation.* Schumpeter asserted in *Business Cycles* (p.153) that the credit machine is designed to serve the improvement of the productive apparatus and to punish other uses — while conceding the design can be altered. It has been. Bezemer, Ryan-Collins, van Lerven and Zhang (*Socio-Economic Review* 21(1), 2023, pp.437ff) document that across 17 advanced economies, 1973-2005, private credit rose from about 60% to about 100% of GDP while the share going to non-financial firms fell from about 60% to about 40%; later work shows the decline continued. Cecchetti and Kharroubi (BIS WP 490, 2015) give the mechanism: credit flows to projects that are easy to pledge as collateral, and pledgeability is negatively correlated with productivity; financial expansion disproportionately harms R&D-intensive, intangible-asset industries. Borio, Kharroubi, Upper and Zampolli (BIS WP 534, 2016) show credit booms shift labour into low-productivity-growth sectors, construction especially, and that the misallocation cost is realised mainly if a crisis follows. Arcand, Berkes and Panizza add the threshold result. The net effect is that Schumpeter's *monetary* claim survived and his *selection* claim did not.

**3. Does the AI capex cycle fit the structure?**

Partially, and the split is informative.

On the funding source, the honest answer is that until very recently this boom was the circular flow financing itself. BIS Bulletin 120 (Aldasoro, Doerr and Rees, 7 January 2026) states it plainly: the AI firms — Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle — historically carried less debt than other US firms and funded investment out of operating cash flow, and the shift to debt is happening now because the scale has outgrown internal funds. Morgan Stanley's decomposition (via SFA Research, 23 July 2026) puts roughly $2.9tn of global data-centre capex through 2028, about $1.4tn from hyperscaler cash flow and about $1.5tn external. Epoch AI's XBRL-based series has aggregate operating cash flow growing about 23% a year against cash capex about 70%, crossing to zero aggregate free cash flow around Q3 2026. FactSet has incremental annual debt rising from 9% of capex in FY24 to 32% LTM by mid-2026. So the Schumpeterian moment is arriving, on a datable schedule, rather than having been the cause all along.

On what is being created, the fit is better than the money numbers suggest — but only under Schumpeter's own credit-theory-of-money framing. BIS QR March 2026, Box A (Eren, Krohn, Todorov) documents gross hyperscaler bond issuance topping $100bn in 2025, mostly over five years, plus a parallel structure in which a JV or SPE owns the asset, is capitalised by a sponsor consortium, raises debt by private placement, and is serviced by lease cash flows, with the hyperscaler holding a minority stake and providing offtake commitments and guarantees. BIS calls this "shadow borrowing": economically debt, largely off balance sheet. Bonds and private credit do not create money — they transfer existing deposits. But they do create *claims on resources ahead of output*, which is Schumpeter's actual criterion. Private credit direct lending to AI-related firms went from near zero to over $200bn outstanding, about 8% of the market, with over $40bn originated in 2025 (BIS Bulletin 120, PitchBook data). Data-centre ABS and CMBS outstanding went from $4bn in 2020 to $61bn YTD 2026 (Barclays via SFA).

Bank money creation does enter, but at one remove: H.8 loans to non-depository financial institutions stood at $2,016.1bn on 5 August 2026, +20.4% y/y, against total bank credit +6.5% and C&I +9.5% — though a 2026 reporting reclassification breaks that series and the growth rate should not be taken clean. BIS notes explicitly that banks supply funding lines to the SPVs. This is Schumpeter's banker intermediated through a fund.

On forced saving, the incidence is identifiable and partly foreign. PJM capacity prices went from $28.92/MW-day (2024/25) to $329.17 (2026/27) with 2027/28 at $333.44, and IEEFA attributes 63% of the 2025/26 increase to data centres, about $9.3bn recovered from customers. That is forced saving with an address. But the largest single real input is imported: BIS footnotes that data-centre GDP contributions may be overstated because equipment is imported, and the Fed's July 2026 FEDS Note observes that net exports of computers and peripherals offset much of the gross investment. A large part of the resource transfer is borne by foreign fab and memory capacity, not by US households.

Two things do not fit. First, Schumpeter's model has no asset-price theory at all; his price effects are factor prices. He cannot speak to the paper-versus-realisable-value gap except indirectly. Second, real interest rates have risen — the 10-year TIPS auctioned at 2.438% in July 2026, the highest at auction since 2008 — which is what a loanable-funds crowding-out story predicts and what pure credit creation would not require. That is confounded by fiscal deficits and the 2026 inflation shock, but it is the honest counter-evidence.

## Wicksell and the cumulative process — natural rate vs market rate, the pure credit economy, and its BIS descendant (Borio, White, Disyatat) on excess elasticity and the financial cycle

**Core mechanism.** When banks lend below the rate that the newly created capital is expected to yield, entrepreneurs earn a surplus profit that does not come out of anyone's saving; they bid for factors, the price level rises, and because the higher prices restore the same real margin, the gap persists and the process repeats period after period. Wicksell's key structural claim is that in a "pure credit" system there is no reserve or specie drain to stop it, so the process is limited only by the banks' own rate-setting: it is a ball on a frictionless plane, not a ball in a bowl. The money stock in this process is endogenous and passive — in Wicksell's own worked arithmetic deposits and loans grow exactly in step with prices (K → 1.01K → 1.02K) and net to zero at each year-end — so money growth tracking nominal income is the *signature* of a cumulative process, not evidence against one. The extension this project needs: Wicksell defined the price level to exclude securities and land deliberately (Interest and Prices, ch. 2, pp. 16–17), so when the created purchasing power lands on asset prices instead of goods prices, his index is blind by construction, and the goods-market feedback that made his process converge to a finite rate of increase (output prices and input prices rise together) is replaced by a collateral feedback that is self-reinforcing rather than self-limiting.

**On investment without prior saving.** Directly: it can, and Wicksell showed the mechanism in 1898 without needing any modern apparatus. In ch. 8 (pp. 104–105) the banks extend credit against which entrepreneurs draw cheques; those cheques become wages and rents, are spent, and return to the banks as deposits; at the end of the production period the entrepreneur repays out of the product. No pre-existing pool of saving is drawn on at any point in that circuit. The saving that "funds" the investment is created ex post — partly by the income the investment itself generates, partly by forced saving as fixed-money-income recipients see their real claims cut (ch. 7B, p. 94 and the qualifications after p. 148). The ex post identity S=I always holds and is therefore silent on who financed what.

The modern statement is Borio and Disyatat, BIS WP 346 (2011), pp. 7–9, and Borio WP 395 (2012), pp. 12–13: financing is a gross cash-flow concept — access to purchasing power in an accepted settlement medium — while saving is income not consumed. Expenditures require financing, not saving. During booms the change in the credit stock exceeds income, and therefore exceeds saving by a much larger margin.

Applied to the AI trade, three financing routes require no rise in the saving rate and no sale of other assets:

1. **Bank credit creation.** A bank loan creates a deposit. This is the only route that adds to M2, and it is the route that is currently growing fastest in the least-monitored place: bank loans to nondepository financial institutions (Fed H.8) went from $546bn in July 2019 to $2,006bn in July 2026, +19.8% y/y against +6.2% for total bank credit. Banks fund the private credit funds and the SPV sponsors; the funds on-lend; the purchasing power reaches the equipment vendor.

2. **Non-bank credit.** Private credit funds lending to AI borrowers (>$200bn outstanding, from near zero; originations >$40bn in 2025 per BIS Bulletin 120) do **not** create deposits. They transfer existing deposits from fund investors to borrowers. This matters and should not be blurred: a boom intermediated by closed-end private credit funds is invisible to money aggregates *in principle*, not merely in practice, because no money is created. It shows up as velocity, not quantity. Only where banks fund those vehicles does creation enter.

3. **Commitments that are not recorded as credit at all.** BIS QR March 2026 Box A: long-term operating leases and capacity offtake agreements substitute multi-year operating expense for upfront capex, keeping the associated debt off the hyperscaler's balance sheet at the SPV level. These are economically debt-like obligations that appear in no credit aggregate.

The empirical marker that the transition has occurred is in BIS Bulletin 120: free cash flows of the five AI hyperscalers have begun to lag capex in absolute amounts. That is the moment the boom stops being funded out of prior corporate saving and starts being funded out of created and intermediated purchasing power.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** Wicksell's answer is structural, not a list of loopholes: in the pure credit economy the money stock is an endogenous residual whose growth tracks nominal spending by construction, so no aggregate can identify a gap. He also anticipated the velocity substitute — the necessary quantity of money can be "infinitely small" with virtually infinite velocity (ch. 6B, p. 65).

Four specific, checkable respects in which purchasing power sits outside M1–M3 today:

1. **Institutional money-fund shares.** These were in M3, which the Fed discontinued in March 2006. Total US money-fund assets were $8,289.6bn at 2026Q1 (Financial Accounts), against $3,047.2bn of retail money funds inside M2 (June 2026). Roughly $5.2tn of money-like balances sits outside every published US aggregate. That figure is a stock and must not be added to M2 (different money-holding sector definitions) — it is a statement about coverage, not a corrected aggregate.

2. **Secured funding turnover.** SOFR volume was $2.9–3.1tn per business day in mid-August 2026 (NY Fed). This is a **gross flow of settlement, not money and not new purchasing power** — mostly overnight rollover of the same collateral. It is the empirical content of Wicksell's velocity point, and it is the reason a $23.2tn money stock can support asset-market gross financing of an entirely different order of magnitude. Do not annualise it and do not compare it to a stock.

3. **Non-bank credit that never creates a deposit.** Private credit fund lending reallocates existing deposits. A boom financed this way cannot show in M2 even in principle. The transmission back into money creation is bank lending to those funds, which is the H.8 NDFI series above — and which, being lending to *financial* institutions, is also excluded from the BIS credit-to-GDP gap for the private non-financial sector.

4. **Obligations not classified as credit.** SPV/JV debt with hyperscaler minority stakes, operating leases, capacity offtake commitments, and contingent guarantees (BIS QR March 2026, Box A). Not in M, not in credit-to-GDP, disclosed only in issuer footnotes.

The honest caveat: I have not decomposed how much of the H.8 NDFI growth is AI-related, and I do not know. It funds mortgage REITs, consumer finance, BDCs and insurers as well. The claim is that the channel is large, fast-growing and structurally excluded from both standard measurement systems — not that it has been traced to data centres.

**On the illusion of wealth.** Wicksell has no theory of this and should not be made to supply one — his system contains no asset market and his index deliberately excludes securities. The tradition acquires the concept only through its BIS descendant, and there it is quantified.

The operative object is the **leverage gap** of Juselius, Borio, Disyatat and Drehmann (BIS WP 569, 2016): the deviation of credit-to-GDP from its cointegrating level given the aggregate asset price index, with a long-run elasticity of one. Their finding (p. 12) is the operator's intuition stated as econometrics: during booms asset prices run ahead of credit, so the leverage gap goes *negative* even as credit-to-GDP rises above trend — "this also makes borrowers look deceptively solid in the boom phase." The illusion is not that the valuations are fake; it is that the collateral ratio is flattering because the denominator has been marked up faster than the numerator. Unit elasticity means the relation is mean-reverting: the gap must close, and it can close either by credit rising to meet asset prices or by asset prices falling to meet credit. Their impulse responses put the cost of the second route at roughly 3pp of private expenditure permanently for a −10% gap; the mid-2000s peak reached −20%, which they estimate explains about half of post-crisis output losses.

The second BIS quantity is the **debt-service gap**: 1pp off the average lending rate supports 5.5pp more debt relative to income in the long run (WP 569, cointegrating vector, coefficient 5.54***). Run backwards, this is the collateral-call mechanism in the operator's question. It also explains why the bust is slow rather than instant: the constraint binds at refinancing, not on demand. With private credit maturities at 4.7 years and hyperscaler bond issuance concentrated beyond five years (BIS QR March 2026, Box A), the AI-specific repricing date is 2030–2031, not now.

Two disciplines. First, Gabaix–Koijen inelasticity ($1 of flow moving $5 of market cap, symmetric) and the leverage gap are *different objects on different measurement bases* — one is a flow-to-valuation multiplier, the other a stock-to-stock cointegrating relation. They must not be composed into a single number. Second, Wicksell's own model contains no collapse at all (ch. 9B, p. 147): close the rate gap and the system rests in equilibrium at permanently higher prices. The Wicksellian null for the AI trade is therefore "valuations settle at a permanently higher level and nothing breaks," and the burden is on the collapse story to supply the extra mechanism — which is collateral and refinancing, i.e. the BIS extension, not Wicksell.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Three things. (1) Why an asset boom can run for years with money aggregates growing at nominal-GDP pace. In Wicksell's own worked example the money stock grows exactly in step with prices and nets to zero at each period-end, because credit is created and extinguished within the cycle of production. Money is the passive by-product; the interest gap is the driver. Under this reading the operator's observation that M2 is +5.5% y/y against nominal GDP +6.5% (2026Q2) is not evidence against a cumulative process — it is precisely what one looks like from the money side, with the small shortfall showing up as rising velocity. Mainstream monetary analysis treats M growth ≈ NGDP growth as an all-clear; Wicksell treats it as uninformative. (2) Why the CPI can stay quiet. Wicksell excluded securities and sites from the price index by choice (ch. 2, pp. 16–17). Borio and Lowe (2002, pp. 20–21) then give the positive account of why, under credible inflation targeting plus a favourable supply shock, the same excess financing shows up in asset markets rather than goods markets — and why that combination removes the policy trigger that would otherwise close the gap. The neo-Wicksellian mainstream cannot generate this because it uses inflation to identify the gap; a quiet CPI mechanically implies no gap. That is an identification failure, not a refutation. (3) Why saving rates need not rise and other assets need not be sold. Borio and Disyatat's saving/financing distinction (BIS WP 346, 2011, pp. 7–9) is a straightforward accounting correction that the loanable-funds framework — including Woodford's — assumes away by construction, because in a pure real model there is no difference between the two.

### Analysis

**1. What Wicksell actually says, at page level.**

The natural rate is defined three ways in a single paragraph of *Interest and Prices* (Kahn trans., 1936), ch. 8, p. 102: the loan rate that is "neutral" with respect to commodity prices; the rate that would be set by supply and demand if no money were used and all lending were in real capital goods; and the current value of the natural rate of interest on capital. These coincide only in equilibrium. On pp. 103–104 he gives the third an operational upper limit: the amount by which the total product exceeds the wages and rents that had to be paid out — i.e. the expected yield on newly created capital. That is the definition worth carrying into an AI capex analysis, because it is the only one with an observable proxy.

The cumulative mechanism is ch. 7B, pp. 94–95. A lower loan rate raises prices; producers pay more for inputs but receive more for output, "he finds himself in precisely the same situation as before"; so the same gap generates the same increment again. "Prices will continually rise higher and higher" (p. 95). Ch. 9B, pp. 145–148, gives the arithmetic. Two points there are decisive for this project. First, after two years borrowings and deposits both stand at 1.02K while prices are up 2% — the money stock grows *exactly* with nominal magnitudes, and at each year-end "all the banking accounts once again show zero balances" (p. 146). Second, p. 147: if the banks then raise the loan rate to the natural rate, prices stop rising but do not fall — "everything is in equilibrium, at a higher level of money prices, wages, and rents." **Wicksell's cumulative process is a permanent level shift, not a cycle. There is no crash in Wicksell.** Any argument that invokes "Wicksellian" to mean "must end in collapse" is importing Fisher, Hayek or Minsky and should be marked as such.

The pure credit economy is ch. 6C (pp. 68–70) and the framing question at p. 80: is bank power "unlimited, so that in a pure credit economy they could bring about any desired rise or fall in prices by pursuing a uniform policy with regard to the rate of interest?" Under convertibility the answer is no — the external drain binds. Without it, nothing does. Wicksell also anticipates velocity as a substitute for quantity: with bills circulating, the required money "can be supposed to be infinitely small, and its (virtual) velocity of circulation to be infinitely great" (ch. 6B, p. 65).

**2. The two places Wicksell himself blocks the asset extension.**

Ch. 2, pp. 16–17: he argues the price index should cover objects of direct consumption only, and says purchasing power is unchanged "even though the prices of securities or sites have risen in the meantime, on account of a fall in the rate of interest." That is a measurement choice, not a finding. And ch. 7B, pp. 97–98: he describes "speculation proper," where goods are bought only to be resold, "the time element... ceases to be of any great significance," prices rise almost without limit *despite dearer credit*, and the reversal "may rapidly drag down prices even below their normal level." Then: he sets it aside. So Wicksell saw the asset channel, named its distinguishing features correctly, and excluded it from the model. Claiming he predicted asset bubbles is reading in.

**3. Woodford's reconstruction, and why it cannot see the operator's phenomenon.**

Woodford (*Interest and Prices*, 2003) rebuilds the natural rate as the flexible-price equilibrium real rate and works in the cashless limit. The critical line, at p. 238: it is only with sticky prices that the Wicksellian distinction can be introduced, "as the discrepancy between the two arises only as a consequence of a failure of prices to adjust sufficiently rapidly" (quoted in Borio et al., BIS WP 685, fn. 32). This is an identification restriction, not a result. In the neo-Wicksellian framework the entire welfare cost of a rate gap is relative-price dispersion; there are no banks, no balance sheets, no collateral. Inflation is then used *to identify the gap* — Laubach–Williams infers a negative gap from falling inflation. BIS WP 685 (pp. 12–13) states the problem exactly: the maintained hypothesis that inflation signals slack "ends up having a decisive influence on the end-result." So a quiescent CPI does not tell you the gap is closed; in the standard apparatus it is *defined* to tell you that. Boianovsky and Trautwein (JHET 28(2), 2006) make the parallel history-of-thought point: what Woodford drops relative to Wicksell is banks, intermediation, and asset markets.

**4. The BIS descendant, and its numbers.**

Borio and Disyatat (BIS WP 346, 2011, pp. 7–9) supply the missing accounting: "Expenditures require financing, not saving"; saving is "not a wall, but a 'hole' in aggregate spending"; banks "create additional purchasing power in the form of deposits through the act of extending credit." Excess elasticity (p. 24) is defined as the degree to which monetary and financial regimes fail to constrain credit creation. Borio (WP 395, 2012) gives the empirics: financial cycle ≈ 16 years average across seven industrial countries since the 1960s, nearly 20 years for cycles peaking after 1998; peaks coincide with crises; recessions in the contraction phase are ~50% deeper.

Borio and Lowe (WP 114, 2002, pp. 20–21) is the direct answer to "boom with quiescent CPI." Four channels: post-disinflation optimism; a positive supply shock that lifts asset prices while depressing unit labour costs; credibility that anchors expectations and desensitises inflation to demand; and the resulting absence of any policy trigger. Their conclusion, in their words, is that latent inflationary pressures manifest in financial imbalances rather than goods prices. White (WP 205, 2006, pp. 7–8) supplies the template case: 1920s America — rapid technological innovation, rising productivity, rising equity and real estate prices, strong fixed investment, and essentially stable-to-falling consumer prices.

Juselius, Borio, Disyatat and Drehmann (WP 569, 2016) make it measurable. Two cointegrating relations on US data 1985Q1–2015Q1: unit long-run elasticity of credit-to-GDP with respect to the aggregate asset price index (the *leverage gap*), and 5.5 percentage points of extra debt serviceable per 1pp lower lending rate (the *debt-service gap*). Their finance-neutral natural rate falls from 4% to 0.6% over thirty years — higher and flatter than Laubach–Williams — and market rates sit below it for essentially the whole sample. Their most useful sentence for this project is p. 12: during booms asset prices run ahead of credit, so the leverage gap *declines*, and "this also makes borrowers look deceptively solid in the boom phase." That is the operator's illusion of wealth, as a constructible series, with a sign convention. At the mid-2000s peak it reached −20%; a −10% gap costs ~3pp of private expenditure permanently.

**5. Where the tradition fails on this particular boom.**

Borio himself (WP 395, p. 2): "equity prices can be a distraction." The financial cycle is credit and *property*. Jordà, Schularick and Taylor (*Leveraged Bubbles*, JME 2015, Table 4) go further: in the crisis logit, housing-bubble × credit loads 0.54*** (full sample) and 0.55* (post-WW2), while equity-bubble × credit loads 0.18 and −0.07, both insignificant. A credit-fuelled equity bubble does not significantly raise crisis probability. The output cost is real but second-order: Table 5 gives −13.1pp cumulative real GDP per capita over five years for equity + high credit versus −29.6pp for housing + high credit.

And the live indicators are green. BIS: US credit-to-GDP gap −11.54pp at 2025Q4 against a Borio–Lowe threshold of +4pp; US private non-financial debt service ratio 14.1% and drifting down from 14.6% in 2024Q3. Fed Z.1: nonfinancial corporate debt 45.4% of GDP in 2026Q1, down from 48.9% in 2023Q1. On its own apparatus, the BIS tradition says there is no financial boom in the United States.

**6. Why the apparatus may nonetheless be looking in the wrong place.**

Three measured facts cut the other way. (i) Fed H.8: bank loans to nondepository financial institutions have gone from $546bn (July 2019) to $2,005bn (July 2026), +19.8% y/y against +6.2% for total bank credit, rising from 4.0% to 10.1% of all US bank credit. This is deposit-creating bank lending that is *definitionally excluded* from a credit gap measured on the private **non-financial** sector. (ii) BIS Quarterly Review March 2026, Box A (Eren, Krohn, Todorov): hyperscalers now finance data centres through JV/SPV vehicles in which they hold minority stakes, with long-term operating leases, capacity offtake commitments and guarantees — "shadow borrowing," debt held by private credit funds and insurers, banks providing the funding lines. Offtake commitments are not credit in any national accounting sense. (iii) BIS Bulletin 120 (Aldasoro, Doerr, Rees, Jan 2026): free cash flow has begun to lag capex; private credit to AI has gone from near zero to >$200bn outstanding and from <1% to ~8% of private credit loan volumes.

Bulletin 120 also states the Wicksellian gap in the cleanest available form: private credit spreads to AI borrowers are 6.2pp versus 6.1pp for non-AI, maturities 4.7 versus 4.8 years, secured 46% versus 48% — lenders price AI capital as *average* risk while equity prices it as extraordinary. Either lenders are underpricing the risk or equity is overpricing the cash flows. In Wicksell's own third definition, that wedge between the equity-implied yield on newly created capital and the marginal cost of financing it *is* the natural-rate/market-rate gap for this boom, and it is quotable daily.

## Mises, Hayek and Austrian capital theory — commodity credit vs circulation credit, the intertemporal capital structure, and the subsistence-fund constraint (Böhm-Bawerk, Mises, Hayek, Lachmann; modern operationalisation via Cachanosky–Lewin duration and the BIS saving/financing distinction).

**Core mechanism.** Credit extended without any prior act of abstention (Mises's *Zirkulationskredit*, circulation credit, as against *Sachkredit*, commodity credit) adds nominal purchasing power without subtracting it from anyone else, so it pushes the market rate of interest below the rate that would clear genuine ex-ante saving against ex-ante investment. Because a lower discount rate raises the present value of distant cash flows more than near ones, the additional purchasing power is preferentially bid into long-duration, "higher-order" capital projects — the capital structure lengthens. But since nobody actually consumed less, the complementary real inputs (Böhm-Bawerk's subsistence fund; in modern terms power, transformers, turbines, HBM, skilled labour) required to *complete* those projects at the planned scale were never released, so a subset is discovered mid-construction to be unfinishable — Mises's master builder who sizes the foundations for four storeys with bricks for two. The loss is incurred at the moment of misallocation during the boom and merely *revealed* at the bust, and it is largely unrecoverable because capital goods are heterogeneous and multiply specific (Lachmann), so the realisable value of a half-built, purpose-specific combination falls far below its book value. The observable signature is therefore not in the price level or in monetary aggregates but in relative prices across the production structure and in the *duration* composition of investment and its financing.

**On investment without prior saving.** Directly: it can, and Mises's whole point is that this is precisely what circulation credit does. Where commodity credit transfers command over resources from someone who abstained, circulation credit — the issuance of a claim payable on demand at no sacrifice to the issuer — creates nominal purchasing power that nobody gave up. Investment then exceeds voluntary saving ex ante, and the accounts are forced into balance ex post by *forced saving*: rising prices of consumption goods deny households the consumption they intended, and the resources are diverted to the longer projects. The identity S≡I is never violated; what is violated is the correspondence between the intertemporal preferences of savers and the intertemporal shape of the capital stock being built. Borio and Disyatat state the same thing from the BIS: saving is an ex-post national-accounts residual, "the true constraint on expenditures is not saving, but financing," and banks "grant nominal purchasing power without reducing it for other agents." Applied to the AI buildout: until roughly 2024 this was overwhelmingly commodity credit — hyperscaler retained earnings, genuine prior abstention by shareholders forgoing distributions. The *marginal* dollar has shifted. BIS Bulletin 120 finds free cash flow now lagging capex in absolute terms, over $100bn of hyperscaler bond issuance in 2025, private credit to AI borrowers going from near zero to >$200bn, and a shadow-borrowing layer of JVs and SPVs the BIS Quarterly Review says keeps the debt off the sponsors' balance sheets. So the disciplined answer to the operator is: yes, and the quantity to track is not total funding but the *share of incremental* funding that is circulation credit — computed as (capex plus lease and SPV obligations) minus operating cash flow, firm by firm, quarter by quarter. Two honest caveats. First, some of this is not money creation at all but the reallocation of existing saving through non-bank intermediaries — a private credit fund lending out locked-up institutional capital is intermediation, not issuance; only the part financed by money-like or bank-backstopped claims is Misesian fiduciary media, and separating the two is the hard empirical work. Second, if the natural rate genuinely fell, the lengthening is warranted and there is no distortion to find — and Sraffa's objection means you cannot settle that by observation of the rate alone.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** Mises's criterion for fiduciary media is functional, not statistical: any claim that confers command over goods without a matching sacrifice by the issuer. That definition is indifferent to whether the claim is captured by M1–M3, which is why the tradition is comfortable with the operator's central observation rather than embarrassed by it. Three channels are invisible to the monetary aggregates but live under the Misesian definition. First, money-like claims outside the deposit perimeter: Sunderam (*RFS* 2015) shows ABCP was priced by investors as a money substitute; the same logic extends to repo, MMF shares and, arguably, the near-cash treatment of certain private-credit fund claims. Second, the credit layer that never touches a deposit at all — the BIS Quarterly Review (March 2026) "shadow borrowing" structure, where an SPV raises debt from private-credit funds and insurers, banks provide funding lines behind it, and the hyperscaler holds a minority stake plus an offtake commitment. The purchasing power reaching the datacentre contractor is real and is not measured as money creation anywhere. Third, the disclosure gap itself: the BIS reports (via Moody's) roughly $662bn of signed-but-not-yet-commenced datacentre lease commitments sitting off balance sheets — flagged as unverified against Moody's directly. The BIS's own formulation is the one to keep: leverage does not disappear by being out of sight. Crucially, the tradition also tells you the aggregate you *should* be watching is not a monetary one. Borio and Disyatat, at p. 21 of BIS WP 346, cite Hayek (1933) for the proposition that a market-natural gap may show up in the relative price of investment against consumption goods rather than in inflation — which is why an M2 growth rate at nominal-GDP pace and a CPI at target are, on this account, uninformative about whether the distortion exists. The correct instruments are relative prices across stages of processing and the duration composition of investment and its financing, not any monetary quantity.

**On the illusion of wealth.** The tradition's answer is definitional and then structural. Definitionally, from Böhm-Bawerk through Mises: capital is not a physical stock but a valuation — the discounted present value of an expected stream of future services. There is no independent physical measure to fall back on; Hayek conceded exactly this in 1941 when he abandoned the technical period of production. So a fall in valuation is not the loss of something that previously existed in another form. It is the correction of an expectation. Structurally, and this is where the tradition earns its keep on the operator's question, Lachmann (*Capital and Its Structure*, 1956) adds heterogeneity and *multiple specificity*: capital goods are useful in a limited number of combinations, complementarity is of the essence of capital use, and when expectations are disappointed the combinations must be regrouped at a loss. Redeployment value is therefore structurally below discounted-expectation value, and the gap is widest exactly where the asset is most purpose-built. This gives the operator's collateral intuition a mechanism rather than a mood. A gigawatt-scale, single-tenant, purpose-configured campus in rural Louisiana has an extremely thin secondary market; its going-concern value is a function of one counterparty's offtake, and its liquidation value is close to the shell plus the grid interconnect. So the sequence he describes is not merely a price decline. It is: expected cash flows are revised or the discount rate normalises → the discounted-expectation valuation falls → but the *realisable* value falls further and faster because the asset is specific → collateral coverage on facilities secured against that asset breaks → and where a sponsor has written a residual value guarantee (Meta's 16-year RVG on Hyperion is the disclosed example), the decline converts into a cash call on an investment-grade balance sheet, which is the transmission from a private-credit mark to a public-market event. Two disciplines on this. First, the price-multiplier part of the story belongs to inelastic-markets work (Gabaix–Koijen), not to the Austrians; the Austrian addition is the specificity wedge below the market price, and it should be claimed narrowly. Second, "illusion" overstates it: the valuation was a real forecast, and if the cash flows arrive it was correct. What is *not* an illusion in any state of the world is the real resource consumption — the concrete, the copper, the turbines, the electricians — which happened, is unrecoverable, and appears in no valuation at all.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) Why aggregates can be silent while a distortion is real: the tradition's claim, endorsed at BIS WP 346 p. 21, is that a market-natural gap need not appear in inflation or M2 at all — it appears in the *relative* price of long-dated capital goods against consumption goods. A quantity-theoretic or inflation-targeting frame has no place to record this, so the operator's M2 observation is not evidence of absence. (2) Why the *composition* of investment matters independently of its level: mainstream investment models are largely one-good, so a dollar of datacentre and a dollar of inventory are the same dollar. Austrian capital theory says the duration and specificity of the dollar determine both its sensitivity to the discount rate and its recoverability, which is exactly what Gopinath et al. (2017) measured as rising dispersion in the return to capital with no matching labour-side dispersion. (3) Why the loss is incurred in the boom, not the bust: standard accounts treat the crash as the shock. Here the resource misdirection is the loss; the crash is the accounting entry. This reframes the collateral question — the collateral was already impaired at origination. (4) Why paper wealth and realisable wealth diverge asymmetrically: capital value is discounted expected service flow, but capital goods are multiply specific (Lachmann 1956), so the redeployment value of a purpose-built combination is a fraction of its discounted-expectation value. The mainstream inelastic-markets result (Gabaix–Koijen) gives the price multiplier; the Austrian addition is that for specific real assets the *realisable* value falls below even the reduced market price, because the second-hand market is thin by construction.

**Modern descendants.** Claudio Borio and Piti Disyatat (BIS) — the saving-versus-financing distinction (BIS WP 346, 2011) and 'excess financial elasticity' (BIS WP 457, 2014); the closest institutional restatement of Mises's commodity/circulation credit, with data, and explicitly citing Hayek (1933) for the relative-price signature; Borio, Kharroubi, Upper and Zampolli, BIS WP 534 (2015) — credit booms cause labour misallocation toward low-productivity-growth sectors, with the damage crystallising when a crisis follows; the best-identified empirical malinvestment result anywhere; Iñaki Aldasoro, Sebastian Doerr and Daniel Rees, BIS Bulletin 120 (Jan 2026) 'Financing the AI boom: from cash flows to debt' — the direct measurement of the shift from retained earnings to debt in the AI cohort; BIS Quarterly Review March 2026, 'Financing the AI infrastructure boom: on- and off-balance sheet borrowing', and BIS Annual Economic Report 2026 ch. I on circular financing and multiply pledged assets; Gita Gopinath, Sebnem Kalemli-Özcan, Loukas Karabarbounis and Carolina Villegas-Sánchez, QJE 2017 — rising dispersion of the return to capital (but not labour) in Spain as real rates fell; capital misallocation identified at firm level; Nicolas Cachanosky and Peter Lewin — the Macaulay-duration operationalisation of roundaboutness (Review of Political Economy 2014; JHET 2018; Cambridge Elements, Austrian Capital Theory, 2019); the reason this tradition is testable at all today. Contested by Fillieule, Review of Austrian Economics 2024; Roger Garrison, Time and Money: The Macroeconomics of Capital Structure (2001) — the canonical modern formal statement of capital-based macro; theoretical rather than empirical; Adi Sunderam, RFS 2015, 'Money creation and the shadow banking system' — the empirical case that money-like claims are created outside the aggregates; the modern extension of Mises's fiduciary media; Lawrence H. White and George Selgin (free banking) — the internal dissent holding that credit expansion accommodating money demand is not distortionary; White's work on Hayek and Robbins in the 1930s documents the school's own retreat from liquidationism; Ricardo Reis, Ryan Banerjee and Boris Hofmann (zombie-firm and low-rate-misallocation literature) — the mainstream channel closest to malinvestment, with better identification and none of the capital-structure apparatus; The negative-result descendants that must be cited alongside: Robert Lester and Jonathan Wolff, Review of Austrian Economics 2013; Christian Gehrke, Economica 2003 on the Ricardo effect

**Ideology flagged.** Six things, named. (1) **Praxeological a priorism.** Mises (*Human Action* ch. II) holds the theory is deduced from the action axiom and cannot be refuted by history. That is a licence to ignore every test in the field above, and much of the Mises Institute literature uses it as one. Treat the theory as a conjecture, not a theorem; a school that cannot lose an argument to data is not doing empirical work. (2) **A central variable with no agreed measure.** Hayek conceded in 1941 (p. 145) that the period of production cannot be aggregated into a technical datum; Sraffa (1932) showed there is no single natural rate out of equilibrium. The Cachanosky–Lewin duration rehabilitation is a genuine repair but is itself contested (Fillieule, *RAE* 2024). Any claim that "the structure lengthened" without stating the metric is unfalsifiable framing. (3) **Liquidationism.** The policy conclusion that the bust must be allowed to run is a prior, not a result, and Hayek himself abandoned it: in the 1970s he stated he would no longer maintain, as he had in the early 1930s, that a short deflation might be desirable, and that the secondary deflation had no steering function. Do not carry the polemic along with the mechanism. (4) **The central bank as unique culprit.** Nothing in the commodity/circulation credit distinction requires a central bank; endogenous private credit creation, and the shadow-money channel Sunderam documents, produce fiduciary media without one. Free bankers within the school (Selgin, White) go further and hold that credit expansion accommodating money demand is not distortionary at all — which means "credit grew" is not by itself the marker the popular literature treats it as. (5) **"Malinvestment" as a moralised counterfactual.** The word presupposes we know what the undistorted capital structure would have been. We do not. Ex post, a failed long-gestation project and a failed bet on technology look identical; the tradition supplies no test that separates them. (6) **The gold standard / hard-money remedy** is an axiological commitment, and the recurrent hyperinflation forecasting after 2008 was a clean, public empirical failure. None of that touches the capital-structure insight, and it should be firewalled from it.

### Analysis

**1. The distinction, stated precisely.**

Mises's *Theory of Money and Credit* (1912; Batson trans. 1934), Part III ch. 1, separates two things ordinary banking language conflates. In **commodity credit**, the transaction "imposes a sacrifice on that party who performs his part of the bargain before the other does — the forgoing of immediate power of disposal over the exchanged good." The bank lends its own funds or funds placed at its disposal; someone somewhere has abstained. In **circulation credit**, "the gain of the party who receives before he pays is balanced by no sacrifice on the part of the other party": the lender confers the loan by issuing claims payable on demand, and "the granting of credit is bound up with no economic sacrifice." The instrument is a *fiduciary medium* — a money-substitute not covered by money.

The criterion is therefore **not** the legal form of the instrument, and not whether it lands in M1–M3. It is whether the purchasing power handed to the borrower was withdrawn from someone else. This matters enormously for the operator's question, because the modern versions of fiduciary media are not banknotes. Sunderam (*RFS* 2015, 28(4):939–977) shows investors priced ABCP as a money-like claim alongside deposits and T-bills; the shadow system supplied money services outside the aggregates. Mises's criterion classifies such claims correctly where M2 does not. This is an extension of Mises, but a faithful one.

Independently, and from the opposite ideological direction, Borio and Disyatat reach the same operative distinction. BIS WP 346 (2011), pp. 7–8: "The true constraint on expenditures is not saving, but financing." Saving is a national-accounts residual, "not a wall, but a 'hole' in aggregate spending." Banks "can grant nominal purchasing power without reducing it for other agents." Their Annex is explicitly Wicksellian, and — decisively for this project — at p. 21 they note that while Wicksell expected the market-natural gap to show up in inflation, "Hayek (1933) argued that the distortion would be reflected in relative prices, in this case between consumer and investment goods." That is a BIS endorsement of *where to look* when the aggregates are silent.

**2. Hayek's transmission and its measurement failure.**

*Prices and Production* (1931) supplies the mechanism: new credit enters at the early stages, forced saving diverts real resources from consumption-goods to investment-goods production, and the resulting intertemporal disequilibrium — excess demand for consumption now, excess planned supply later — reverses when consumption-goods prices rise and switch incentives back toward late stages. The Ricardo effect (*Profits, Interest and Investment* 1939; *Economica* 1942) is the reversal engine: rising real product prices / falling real wages make direct labour more attractive than machinery, shortening the structure abruptly.

Both the measurement and the reversal engine are in poor shape. Sraffa (*EJ* 1932, 42:42–53) argued that outside equilibrium there is no single natural rate — there are as many own-rates as commodities — which undermines the "market rate below *the* natural rate" formulation at its root. Kaldor's "concertina effect" (*Economica* 1942, 9:359–382) attacked the Ricardo effect's dynamics; Gehrke (*Economica* 2003) concludes the machinery-substitution effect is not caused by a rising real wage and holds only in an "extremely special case." Most damaging, Hayek conceded the core measurement point himself. *The Pure Theory of Capital* (1941), p. 145: there is no way the variety of technical waiting periods "can be combined into an aggregate or average which can be regarded as a technical datum," and under different conditions the same technique would appear longer or shorter (see Steele, "Hayek's *The Pure Theory of Capital*," Elgar Companion to Hayekian Economics, 2006). The theory's central variable had no agreed measure for seventy years.

**3. Empirical record — state it plainly.**

It is weak. The most careful test, Lester and Wolff (*Review of Austrian Economics* 2013, 26(4):433–461) — published in the Austrians' *own* journal, using stage-of-process data rather than aggregates — finds a positive monetary shock *decreases* early- and late-stage production relative to middle stages, the opposite of the prediction, and concludes the mechanisms are unsupported or second-order. Luther and Cohen (Advances in Austrian Economics, 2016) contest the construction of the measures rather than rescuing the result. Supportive work exists — Keeler (*RAE* 2001), Mulligan (2006) on term structure and expenditure composition, Bismans and Mougeot (*RAE* 2009, country-mixed), Young (*RAE* 2012) finding roundabout industries' prices rose more into the subprime episode — but it is thinner and less adversarial. Popular-Austrian post-2008 hyperinflation forecasting was simply wrong.

What has changed is measurability. Cachanosky and Lewin (*Review of Political Economy* 2014; *JHET* 2018) identify Böhm-Bawerk's average period of production with Macaulay duration — Hicks's 1939 average period and Macaulay's 1938 duration are the same object. That converts an unmeasurable metaphysical variable into a number any structurer can compute from a cash-flow schedule. Fillieule's rejoinder (*RAE* 2024, "Pointlessness of average period of production") shows the identification is contested. But the operational upside is large, and it is what makes the field below non-vacuous.

The strongest modern confirmations come from outside the school. BIS WP 534 (Borio, Kharroubi, Upper, Zampolli, 2015): credit booms reduce productivity growth by pulling labour into lower-productivity-growth sectors, and the damage is far larger when a crisis follows — misallocation begets misallocation. Gopinath, Kalemli-Özcan, Karabarbounis and Villegas-Sánchez (*QJE* 2017, 132(4):1915–1967): in Spain 1999–2012, as real rates fell, the *dispersion of the return to capital* rose sharply while the dispersion of the return to labour was stable — capital misallocation, precisely located, with no matching labour-market distortion.

**4. Is the AI datacentre buildout the Austrian structure?**

Partly, and the honest answer distinguishes level from margin. BIS Bulletin 120 (Aldasoro, Doerr, Rees, 7 Jan 2026): US IT investment reached 5% of GDP by mid-2025, above the 2000 dot-com peak; data centres plus IT manufacturing facilities ≈1% of GDP; AI investment contributed ~0.4pp to GDP growth over three years, and total IT investment "almost half of GDP growth in recent quarters." Historically these firms funded capex from operating cash flow — that is genuine commodity credit, prior abstention by shareholders forgoing distributions. But "free cash flows have recently lagged capital expenditures in absolute amounts," and financing is shifting to debt, with private credit to AI-related borrowers rising from near zero to over $200bn (from <1% to ~8% of outstanding direct loans; $40bn originated in 2025 vs ~$3bn in 2010). Hyperscaler bond issuance topped $100bn in 2025. The BIS Quarterly Review (March 2026) names the off-balance-sheet layer "shadow borrowing." The BIS 2026 Annual Economic Report flags circular financing — chipmakers taking equity in labs that commit to buying chips — and warns of "the same asset being pledged multiple times."

The Meta/Blue Owl Hyperion JV is the clean specimen (Meta press release, 21 Oct 2025): ~$27bn total development cost, Blue Owl 80% / Meta 20%, ~$7bn cash in, Meta taking a ~$3bn distribution, debt raised privately to PIMCO and others — and an **initial operating lease term of four years** with a **residual value guarantee for the first sixteen years**. Against BIS's finding that AI private-credit maturities average 4.7 years, this is the Böhm-Bawerk problem in modern dress: the liability structure is roughly five years long; the asset's payback is measured in decades; and the residual value guarantee is a written put on the collateral, retained by the sponsor while the debt sits off its balance sheet. That is the transmission channel from "valuations fall" to "collateral calls" that the operator intuits.

The physical subsistence-fund test is live. ERCOT's large-load interconnection queue exceeds 438,000 MW of proposed demand, ~90% data centres (ERCOT Senate Business & Commerce update, 2026) — a multiple of the system's peak load. Transformer, switchgear and turbine lead times are the binding constraint, not GPUs. Mises's bricks are visible and countable.

**5. The steelman.**

Five counter-arguments deserve weight. (i) The buildout may be genuine lengthening: hyperscaler retained earnings are real abstention, and the marginal debt share, while rising fast, is still a minority of funding. (ii) If r* genuinely fell for demographic and productivity reasons, the lengthening is *warranted*, and Sraffa's point means we cannot cleanly identify the gap. (iii) Lester–Wolff's stage-of-process evidence runs against the theory. (iv) Free bankers (Selgin, White) hold that credit expansion accommodating a rise in money demand is not distortionary, so credit growth per se is not the marker — which weakens the school's own most-used indicator. (v) BIS Bulletin 120's own perspective chart: at ~1% of GDP the AI investment boom is *smaller* than the 1990s US tech boom (half the size) and less than a fifth the size of Japan's 1980s commercial property boom or Australia's 2000s mining boom. On the tradition's own logic, the malinvestment is real but the macro loss may be modest — though BIS notes the largest post-boom GDP slowdown historically followed dot-com, the smallest boom.

## Cantillon effects and Thornton's money/credit distinction — non-neutrality by point of entry, and why a consumer price index is the wrong instrument for detecting it

**Core mechanism.** New purchasing power enters the economy at identifiable points, and the identity of the first receivers determines which prices move, because agents differ systematically in what they buy with an incremental unit. Cantillon states the neutrality condition explicitly and negatively (Essai, Part II ch. VII): a doubling of money doubles all prices only if the people offering the new money in the market are the same people who offered the old, which he says is almost never true. Thornton adds that the relevant object is not the stock of any nameable instrument but the flow of payments it effects (Paper Credit, ch. III, pp. 44-47), that the medium is a continuum with no natural boundary, and that only the *acceleration* of credit creation produces real effects, not its level (ch. X, p. 291). Where the injection lands among agents with near-zero marginal propensity to consume goods — securities portfolios rather than transaction accounts — the price response appears in the discount rate applied to future cash flows and not in the price of current output. Cantillon describes exactly this case in Part III ch. VIII: notes issued to buy public stock do not disturb the circulation because they are used for buying and selling stock, are not spent on household needs, and are not converted into coin.

**On investment without prior saving.** Thornton answers this directly and it is the most useful thing the tradition has for the operator. Paper Credit ch. XI, pp. 263-64: an excessive issue that raises the cost of goods but not the price of labour produces "some augmentation of stock", because the labourer is forced by necessity to consume fewer articles while exercising the same industry, supplemented by a similar defalcation of the revenue of the unproductive members of society. Investment exceeds prior *voluntary* saving because credit creation finances a bid for real resources already in use, the bid raises their price, and whoever holds a fixed nominal claim abstains involuntarily. Cantillon says the same thing in Part II ch. VI without the label: increased expenditure by mine workers "diminishes of necessity the share" of those who did not participate first. Hayek traced the doctrine to Bentham, Thornton, Ricardo, Malthus and Mill in the QJE (47, 1932). Two disciplines must be applied. First, Thornton himself blocks the strong version: bona fide capital cannot be suddenly and materially increased by any emission of paper (ch. X, p. 289). Credit creation redistributes command over the existing resource stock; it does not conjure the stock. Second, forced saving only bites at full employment — Bentham said so explicitly — and for most of 2009-2019 the US had a negative output gap, so the extra real resources came from idle capacity and nobody was forced to abstain. That is the Keynesian answer and for that period it is the right one. The mechanism becomes live again precisely when the economy is at or near full employment, which is where the AI capex cycle is being financed. For that cycle the operative question is not "where is the money" but "which real resource is being bid away, and who is losing it": electric power, grid interconnection queues, skilled construction labour, advanced-node wafer and HBM capacity. The Cantillon prediction is that those relative prices move first and hard, and they are moving — PPI industrial electric power is up 15.0% since January 2024 against roughly 3.5% y/y headline CPI. That is forced saving in progress, visible as relative-price dispersion rather than as an aggregate.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** Thornton settles this in 1802 and the settlement has not been improved on. Against Smith he argues (ch. III, pp. 44-47) that bills of exchange, exchequer bills and India bonds all perform payment work; that the interest-bearing part of the medium turns over far more slowly than the non-interest-bearing part; and that the relevant magnitude is not the quantity of the thing capable of circulating but the quantity of actual circulation. There is no natural boundary at which an instrument stops being money — the boundary is chosen by the statistician, and the economy routinely migrates across it. M1-M3 are cuts through a continuum, and the continuum's outer layers (repo, MMF shares, bills used as collateral, committed credit lines, dealer balance sheet capacity) do the marginal payment and financing work in exactly the segment where asset purchases clear. Two further reasons aggregates go blind. First, QE is an asset swap: a Treasury becomes a reserve plus a deposit, changing the composition of private-sector portfolios without changing net private financial wealth. The price effect is therefore not a money effect at all in the aggregate-quantity sense; it is a duration- and convexity-supply effect operating through a small set of constrained intermediaries — which is why the measured magnitude (7.87bp per $100bn, decaying to 3.13bp within a year, per Selgrad) is modest and transient rather than proportional to any stock. Second, and this is the accounting discipline that matters most here: a valuation is not a quantity of money and can never appear in one. Between 2009Q1 and 2021Q4 US household and non-profit net worth rose $92.4tn while M2 rose about $13.2tn; those two numbers are on different measurement bases and must never be netted, differenced or compared as though the first were financed by the second. The gap is not missing money. It is the arithmetic of applying a lower discount rate to an existing set of claims. Thornton's footnote at p. 263 supplies the last piece: because payments were concentrated in a few London bankers, new paper reached prices faster than new guineas would have. Concentration of the receiving sector is itself a determinant of transmission speed and destination — and today's receiving sector is the most concentrated it has ever been.

**On the illusion of wealth.** The tradition supports the operator's intuition but requires one correction and one sharpening. Cantillon (Part III ch. VII) calls bank-created purchasing power a fictitious and imaginary abundance that raises the price of land and labour and then vanishes at the first gust; in ch. VIII he says outright that if panic drove noteholders to demand silver, the bomb would burst. Thornton (ch. XI) gives the flow version: the trader who sells three months after buying pockets the general price rise as apparent profit and confounds it with the return on his trade — a holding gain booked as earnings. The correction: "if someone actually pulled money out, valuations would collapse" is not quite the right statement, because in aggregate nobody can pull money out — every seller has a buyer, and the stock of securities and the stock of deposits are both conserved through the transaction. What is not conserved is the PRICE at which the marginal trade clears, and that is the Gabaix-Koijen result this project already holds: roughly $5 of market capitalisation per $1 of net flow, symmetric on the way down. The correct formulation is therefore about the price elasticity of the marginal clearing trade, not about the existence of exit liquidity. The sharpening comes from Greenwald, Leombroni, Lustig and Van Nieuwerburgh. Heterogeneity in portfolio revaluation explains essentially the whole rise in the top-10% financial wealth share since the 1980s (113% of the Gini rise), but total wealth — financial plus human, the quantity relevant to lifetime consumption — rises far less, and the top-1% total-wealth share actually falls. Their normative exercise is decisive for the operator: they compute the financial wealth each household would need to keep its consumption plan unchanged, and find that to preserve pre-shock plans, financial wealth inequality should have *declined* (top-10% share falling 1.5pp) over the sample. So the gains were not merely paper — households born in the 2000s lose more than 8% of lifetime consumption from the rate decline — but neither were they new wealth. A discount-rate-driven revaluation is a transfer from future purchasers of the asset to present holders, recognised today at full value and settled later by whoever must buy in at the higher price. Fagereng, Holm, Moll and Natvik close the loop on the funding side: net-of-capital-gains saving rates are roughly flat across the wealth distribution, and "saving by holding" explains up to 80% of Norway's rise in wealth-to-income from about 4 to about 7. Aggregate wealth can therefore grow rapidly without anyone saving more and without money creation — which is precisely the operator's puzzle, and the resolution is that the wealth was never created, it was re-dated. The collateral risk he identifies is real and follows directly: collateral is marked at the revalued price, but the revaluation is a discount-rate artefact that can reverse without any change in underlying cash flows, and the margin call arrives against a real liability.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) The 2009-2019 versus 2020-2022 asymmetry: the same institution expanding its balance sheet by comparable amounts produced 1.84%/yr CPI in the first episode and a 8.98% y/y peak in the second. A quantity-theoretic or a pure discount-rate account has nothing to say about the difference; a point-of-entry account predicts it, because the first receivers changed from securities portfolios to transaction deposits. (2) The composition of rebalancing. Selgrad's finding that funds move over 60% of QE proceeds into corporate bonds of *issuers whose bonds they already hold* is not an aggregate-money fact and not a discount-rate fact; it is a fact about who received and what was adjacent to their existing book. (3) Why credit growth has such different macro consequences depending on what it finances — the Jordà-Schularick-Taylor mortgage-share result and the Bezemer et al. debt-shift result are invisible to any framework in which credit is a homogeneous aggregate. (4) The persistent decay of QE effects. Thornton's proposition that only progressive augmentation gives relief, and that the level of the stock does nothing once prices adjust, is confirmed by Selgrad's 7.87 → 4.75 → 3.13bp decay path. Standard treatments regress on the stock of holdings; the tradition says regress on the flow, and the flow specification is the one that fits.

**Ideology flagged.** Several things, named plainly. (1) The internet-Austrian version treats "Cantillon effect" as evidence of deliberate transfer to bankers. Cantillon himself describes a mechanism, not a conspiracy; his own conspiracy passage (Part III ch. VIII, the minister who leans on the Bank's directors to buy stock) is offered without a shred of evidence, however prescient it reads. Motive is not a mechanism, and no distributional result requires one. (2) "The inflation is there, it just went into assets" is unfalsifiable as usually stated. Asset prices and consumer prices are not commensurable; you cannot add or average them, and a claim that money "went into" assets is empty until it names whose spending, on what, at whose expense. Cantillon's own text is stricter than his followers: he specifies the sequence and the losers by name. (3) The hyperinflation prior attached to this tradition has been wrong continuously since 2009 and should be discarded, not rescheduled. (4) Cantillon's own priors are mercantilist — he equates the comparative wealth of states with the quantity of money they hold, and closes with a moralising cycle in which luxury inevitably destroys a rich state. That is fatalism, not analysis, and none of the mechanism depends on it. (5) Thornton's policy conclusions are inseparable from convertibility and the usury laws; his analytics survive the gold standard, his prescriptions do not. His forced-saving passage is also explicitly normative — he calls it hardship and injustice in the same sentence in which he concedes the capital formation is real. (6) On the modern side, the hazard is statistic-shopping. Over 2009Q1-2021Q4 the top 1% net worth share rose 3.5pp and the bottom 50% share rose from 0.7% to 2.4%; the losers were the 50th-99th percentiles. Reporting only the first is advocacy. (7) Finally, the tradition's own strongest modern rival is not a rebuttal from the right or left but Coibion, Gorodnichenko, Kueng and Silvia's finding that the segmentation channel is quantitatively small for US consumption inequality — a result the popular version of this tradition simply does not engage with.

### Analysis

**1. What Cantillon actually says, and the neutrality condition.**

The Essai (posthumous 1755, written c. 1730) sets out the mechanism in Part II chs. VI-VII. New silver from a mine accrues first to the mine proprietor, the undertaker, the smelters, refiners and workers; they raise household consumption of meat, wine, cloth, furnishings; that bids up those specific goods; farmers respond and gain; the losers are named precisely — landowners locked into leases, domestic servants, and fixed wage-earners, who must cut expenditure. Cantillon's decisive analytical move is in ch. VII, where he denies proportionality: doubling money does not double prices; "in England the price of meat might be tripled while the price of corn went up only one fourth." He states the condition under which proportionality *would* hold — that those who now offer two ounces are the same people who previously offered one — and observes it is hardly ever the case. That is a falsifiable statement about distribution, not a metaphysics of money.

Cantillon then writes what is, read literally, the 1730s statement of quantitative easing (Part III ch. VIII). A minister who wants to lower the interest rate and raise the price of public stock arranges for the Bank to issue notes without backing and to buy stock with them; the price rises; sellers, holding idle notes and reading the rise as a signal, buy back higher; others follow the Bank's agents. His conclusion is the one this project needs: the excess notes do not upset the circulation, because being used for buying and selling stock they do not serve for household expenses and are not changed into silver. And then the closing sentence, which is the operator's question exactly: if panic drove holders to demand silver, the bomb would burst. In ch. VII he calls this an abundance of fictitious and imaginary money that raises land and labour prices but vanishes at the first gust.

**2. Thornton's three contributions.**

Thornton's *Paper Credit* (1802) supplies what Cantillon lacks. First, the money/credit continuum: against Adam Smith he argues (ch. III, pp. 44-47) that bills of exchange, exchequer bills and India bonds all do payment work; that interest-bearing instruments turn over far more slowly than non-interest-bearing ones (100l. of country notes might effect a payment every three days, a 100l. bill one every nine); and that what matters is "the quantity not of the thing which circulates" but of actual circulation. Any monetary aggregate is a chosen cut through a continuum, and the cut moves.

Second, forced saving. In ch. XI (pp. 263-64) he grants that an excessive issue that lifts the cost of goods but not the price of labour will produce "some augmentation of stock", because the labourer is forced by necessity to consume fewer articles while exercising the same industry — supplemented by a similar defalcation of the revenue of the unproductive members of society. He immediately calls this hardship and injustice. In the same chapter he notes the trader who sells three months after buying pockets the general price rise as apparent profit and confounds it with the return on his trade — an inventory-holding gain mistaken for earnings, which is the flow analogue of the operator's illusion of wealth. And critically: when the medium stops increasing, the extra profit ends.

Third, in ch. X (pp. 287-291), the priority claim usually given to Wicksell: the demand for discounts is governed by the gap between the discount rate and the current rate of mercantile profit; because the usury laws cap the Bank at 5%, borrowers "obtain their loans too cheap. That which they obtain too cheap they demand in too great quantity." He then blocks the obvious objection: bona fide capital cannot be suddenly increased by any emission of paper, so once prices have fully adjusted the temptation to borrow is exactly as before. Therefore it is the progressive augmentation of bank paper, not the magnitude of its existing amount, that gives the relief. That is the sharpest statement in the whole tradition of the project's own rule that stock is not flow. A footnote on the same page adds the portfolio-rebalancing channel: London bankers, being few and concentrated holders of Bank paper, are much less disposed to sit on a doubled quantity of it than the public is to sit on doubled guineas — so concentrating the payment business in few hands changes the *speed* with which new medium reaches prices.

**3. The 2009-2021 answer.**

Who received first: the Fed bought Treasuries and agency MBS from primary dealers, and the marginal sellers in the Financial Accounts were the household sector — which in Z.1 includes domestic hedge funds — and broker-dealers, not commercial banks (Carpenter, Demiralp, Ihrig, Klee, *JBF* 52, 2015; confirmed and extended in the Board's June 2024 FEDS Note on QT). What they bought: Selgrad (NYU, 2023) constructs a security-level unexpected-purchase shock and finds mutual funds rebalance over 60% of proceeds from QE-induced Treasury sales into corporate bonds of similar maturity, disproportionately of issuers whose bonds they already hold; $100bn of purchases lowers a typical corporate bond yield by 7.87bp on impact, decaying to 4.75bp at three months and 3.13bp at twelve. Note the decay — Thornton's acceleration result, measured.

Why the goods market never saw it: three verifiable reasons, none requiring conspiracy. (i) The recipients have essentially no propensity to spend an asset swap on goods; the best-identified stock-wealth MPC is 3.2 cents per dollar per year (Chodorow-Reich, Nenov, Simsek, *AER* 111(5), 2021), and it applies to realised local wealth, not to a portfolio reshuffle. (ii) The largest asset on the household balance sheet enters CPI as a rental flow, not a capital value: Case-Shiller national HPI rose 103.9% from Jan-2012 to Oct-2021 while CPI owners' equivalent rent rose 32.4% — a 71-point wedge, by construction, in the index component carrying roughly a quarter of the CPI weight. (iii) Credit was created against existing assets rather than against production: the mortgage share of bank lending across 17 advanced economies roughly doubled from about 30% in 1900 to about 60% today (Jordà, Schularick, Taylor, *Economic Policy* 31/85, 2016), and Bezemer, Ryan-Collins, van Lerven and Zhang (*Socio-Economic Review* 21/1, 2023) tie the fall in the non-financial-business share of credit to the retreat of credit policy.

The measured contrast is the tradition's best evidence, and it is a natural experiment. Fed assets went from $905bn (3 Sep 2008) to $4.45tn (Oct 2014); over 2009Q1-2019Q4, M2 grew 5.8%/yr, CPI 1.84%/yr, and household net worth 6.4%/yr ($59.2tn to $115.7tn). Then in 2020-21 the injection point moved: M2 rose 26.8% in the twelve months to Feb-2021, but this time through Treasury transfers into deposit accounts of high-MPC households, and CPI peaked at 8.98% y/y in June 2022. Same central bank, similar balance-sheet expansion, opposite price response — differing in who received first. The honest caveat is that this experiment is confounded: Bernanke and Blanchard's decomposition attributes much of the 2021 surge to shocks to prices given wages (energy, food, shortages), with labour-market tightness mattering later.

**4. What the modern literature confirms and what it refutes.**

Confirmed: the incidence is monotone in income. Andersen, Johannesen, Jørgensen and Peydró (*Journal of Finance* 78/5, 2023), on Danish administrative data 1987-2014 with the currency peg as the identifying instrument, find a 1pp policy-rate cut raises disposable income by under 0.5% at the bottom, ~1.5% at the median and ~5% at the top. Confirmed on the wealth side: Fed Distributional Financial Accounts show the top 1% share of corporate equities and mutual fund shares rising from 46.5% (2009Q1) to 52.0% (2019Q4), so an equity revaluation lands roughly half on one percent of households by portfolio composition alone.

Refuted, or at least sharply bounded: Coibion, Gorodnichenko, Kueng and Silvia (*JME* 88, 2017; NBER WP 18170) name the Cantillon channel by its modern label — financial segmentation, after Williamson (2009) and Ledoit (2009) — and conclude that in US CEX data its counterfactual sign for consumption inequality after contractionary shocks is wrong, implying these channels are quantitatively small relative to earnings heterogeneity and savings redistribution. That verdict applies to conventional policy, to consumption rather than wealth, and to a pre-ZLB sample; it does not touch the asset-valuation version. Gmeiner (*Kyklos* 78/2, 2025) builds quintile-specific CPIs for 1990-2022 and finds an inflationary effect on the lowest quintile from Fed purchases of government debt but not other assets — supportive, but ARMAX time-series identification is weak and I would not lean on it.

**5. Steelman of the counter-argument.**

Nothing above requires money. Let the real rate fall for demographic or safe-asset-demand reasons; every long-duration claim reprices as P = D/(r-g); the observed asset boom is the correct relative-price response and CPI is silent because CPI prices current consumption while asset prices price future consumption, and their ratio *is* the interest rate — which lowering was the announced objective. On this reading "the inflation went into assets" is a category error, since you cannot deflate a bond and a loaf with the same numéraire. The counter has real force, and Greenwald, Leombroni, Lustig and Van Nieuwerburgh (NBER 28613, rev. 2023) supply its strongest version: heterogeneity in portfolio revaluation alone accounts for essentially the entire rise in the top-10% financial wealth share since the 1980s (113% of the Gini rise), with no money-order-of-arrival needed.

Where the counter is incomplete: it is indifferent to who borrows and who receives, and therefore cannot generate the composition facts — 60% of proceeds into corporate bonds of already-held issuers, mortgage share doubling, the 2009-19 versus 2020-22 CPI asymmetry. A frictionless discount-rate story also predicts a flow-to-price elasticity near unity, not the roughly five-to-one this project has already established from Gabaix and Koijen. The five-to-one *is* the segmentation Cantillon and Thornton posited; it is the price of the point of entry.

## Fictitious capital: Marx (Capital Vol. III, Chs. 25, 29–30), Hilferding (Finance Capital, 1910, Ch. 7), and the modern Marxian finance literature (Durand 2017; Palludeto & Rossi, CJE 2022; Rotta 2018; Lapavitsas 2013), with Bichler & Nitzan's capital-as-power critique as the internal adversary.

**Core mechanism.** A title to a future income stream is priced by capitalising that income at a market discount rate (Marx, Vol. III Ch. 29: every periodic income is capitalised at the average rate of interest). The title then circulates as a commodity with its own price dynamics, so its value moves for two independent reasons — a change in the expected income, and a change in the capitalisation rate — the second of which alters no underlying productive capital whatsoever. Critically, the title is a *duplicate*: the capital exists once, in the enterprise, and again as a marketable claim, so the sum of claim values and asset values overstates society's wealth even when every individual claim is correctly priced. The distinctive addition beyond discounting is Hilferding's: when the same cash flow is repackaged from illiquid operating capital (capitalised at the profit rate) into liquid marketable titles (capitalised at the interest rate plus a risk premium), the arithmetic difference is realised as a once-only income — promoter's profit — accruing to the issuer, and this is a determinate quantity, not a residual. Fictitious capital therefore funds nothing directly; it functions as *collateral* and as the *allocation signal* that directs real labour and means of production toward the capitalised activity, and it can only be validated ex post by surplus actually produced.

**On investment without prior saving.** The tradition's answer is that the question is malformed, and it says so explicitly. Durand (2017, p. 50): "the central problem fictitious capital poses is not — as the Austrian school approach has it — the existence of prior savings sufficient to allow the creation of supplementary capital." Credit money "largely results from an ex nihilo creation, in that it is an advance on a future revenue and essentially does not come from previously saved funds" (p. 51); it constitutes, in Lipietz's phrase that Durand adopts, the a-priori canonisation of private labour as social labour — production and wages are pre-validated by money before commodities are sold. Marx's Ch. 25 quotation of Gilbart makes the same point mechanically: the same £1,000 deposited at A is reissued and becomes a deposit at B, indefinitely. So investment does not wait on saving; saving is generated ex post by the investment, and the fiction is in the *validation*, not the funding.

But three qualifications, and the operator should hold all three.

First, **fictitious capital itself funds nothing.** A re-rating creates no money. Primary issuance draws on money-capital that already exists; it reallocates rather than creates. The only genuine money-creation channel running through valuation is *collateral*: a higher capitalised value of titles expands the borrowing base, and bank (or bank-like) credit extended against that base is new purchasing power. Hilferding's promoter's profit is a transfer from share buyers to promoters at the moment of issue, not creation — though it converts a future income into unconditional present purchasing power for the promoter, which is why it matters for who gets to command real resources now.

Second, Durand gives an exhaustive and testable list of where net inflow into an asset market can come from (p. 93): other, less-favoured asset markets; a decline in bank savings; an increase in overall savings; imported capital; or an increase in bank credit devoted to financial operations. Given the project's established finding that the savings rate is not up and other assets are not being liquidated en masse, that list narrows hard to imported capital and credit extended for financial operations — including credit extended outside the deposit-creating banking system, which is where the NBFI +9.4% figure belongs.

Third, and this is the tradition's real contribution to the operator's puzzle: **the funding constraint and the resource constraint are different constraints, and only the second binds.** Money can be created; fab capacity, grid interconnects, transformers, land and skilled engineers cannot. Palludeto & Rossi's §5 is the mechanism: fictitious capital allocates social labour, because prices formed in financial markets "become a benchmark for the production process." The AI capex boom therefore need not be funded out of prior saving at all — it needs real resources withdrawn from other uses, and the observable is a real reallocation (electricity, construction, semiconductor capacity, engineering labour), not a monetary aggregate. Looking for the boom in M2 is looking in the wrong ledger.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** Five distinct reasons purchasing power escapes M1–M3, only some of which are Marxian.

(1) **A correction the operator should have.** There is no US M3 to be invisible to: the Board ceased publishing M3 on 23 March 2006, along with large-denomination time deposits, repurchase agreements and Eurodollars, on the stated grounds that M3 conveyed no information beyond M2. The components mostly remain published individually; what was discontinued was the *aggregation*. So "does not show in M1–M3" should be restated as: the US has not maintained a broad aggregate for twenty years, and institutional money funds, repo and Eurodollars are outside M2 by construction.

(2) **Holding gains are excluded from income and saving by accounting design.** In SNA 2008 and in NIPA, revaluations are recorded in the revaluation account and never enter production, income or capital accounts. A ten-trillion-dollar rise in market capitalisation therefore produces exactly zero measured saving and zero measured money, while producing real collateral capacity. This is not a Marxian insight but it is the accounting fact that makes the Marxian point unavoidable, and it is the single cleanest answer to the operator's question.

(3) **Deposit reuse rather than deposit creation.** Marx's Ch. 25 Gilbart quotation is the mechanism: the same deposit is redeployed through successive balance sheets. Non-bank credit — private credit funds, securitisation vehicles, insurance-affiliated lenders — transfers purchasing power by re-lending existing deposits rather than creating new ones. The quantity of money is unchanged; the quantity of credit and the intensity of its use are not. This is a velocity-and-intermediation-chain phenomenon that a stock measure cannot see, and it is where the established NBFI +9.4% / narrow measure +12% to $76.3tn figures belong.

(4) **Promoter's profit is invisible in the national accounts.** Hilferding's once-only gain from re-capitalising an unchanged cash flow appears in the flow of funds as a financial transaction, never as value added. When a founder sells a secondary tranche at a 50× markup, real command over resources is transferred and no aggregate registers it as income.

(5) **Circular and vendor-financed transactions register as revenue on both sides.** Marx's accommodation-paper problem: a bill drawn to retire another is indistinguishable from trade paper. Where a chip vendor takes equity in a customer who then buys chips, revenue and asset values are both created without external cash entering the system — visible in supplier-finance and related-party disclosures, invisible in monetary aggregates.

The Kalecki channel the project has already established belongs alongside these as a sixth: a profit flow that appears in no monetary aggregate.

**On the illusion of wealth.** This is where the tradition names the operator's intuition most directly, and also where it should be most carefully constrained.

The direct statement is Marx, Vol. III p. 597: the capital "does not exist twice over," once as the share and again as the enterprise; it exists once, and the share is a pro-rata title to surplus value the enterprise is *to* realise. And Ch. 29 on the state debt: "as soon as the promissory notes become unsaleable, the illusion of this capital disappears." Marx also anticipates the collateral-call dynamic exactly: in a crisis securities fall "for two reasons: first, because the rate of interest rises, and secondly, because they are thrown on the market in large quantities" — the discount-rate shock and the forced-sale shock compound, and the resulting depreciation "serves as a potent means of centralising fortunes."

But the illusion is *not* that any individual price is wrong. That is the crucial refinement, and it is the point at which the tradition becomes usable rather than polemical. Each claim may be correctly priced given expectations. What is illusory is that the aggregate is realisable at the marked price. Durand states it precisely (p. 93): capital gains are "a zero-sum game among the various participants on the market"; the fiction becomes real for a seller only when a buyer takes it over. The mark-to-market value of the entire non-traded stock is an imputation from the marginal trade — Durand calls this the paradox of liquidity (p. 54), and Palludeto & Rossi quote Keynes (GT p. 135) making the same point: the exchange gives the individual, though not the community, the chance to revise. Gabaix & Koijen's ~5× multiplier, already established in this project, is the modern quantification of exactly this gap, and it is symmetric.

So the operationally correct statement for a structurer is: paper valuation exceeds realisable value by roughly the inverse of market elasticity, and the fiction is load-bearing precisely because it functions as collateral while it holds. The collateral function is why the collapse is not merely a repricing — it converts a valuation change into a funding event, which is the mechanism behind the operator's collateral-call intuition.

Two honest counterweights. Durand himself insists (p. 83) that financial profits "are not themselves fictitious" — paid in cash, they carry full purchasing power; only their *origin* is a transfer rather than a creation of value. And the strongest objection, from Bichler & Nitzan (2024), is that the whole illusion framing presupposes a measurable "real" wealth against which paper is judged excessive, which the Cambridge capital controversy says does not exist independently of prices. If that objection holds, the correct statement is not that valuations are illusory relative to some real magnitude, but the weaker and still-useful one: valuations are not simultaneously realisable, and the multiplier that separates marked value from realisable value is the measurable object.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) **Promoter's profit as an income category with a determinate size.** Mainstream finance has no name for the systematic gain that arises purely from re-capitalising an unchanged cash flow at the interest rate instead of the profit rate; it scatters the same quantity across "IPO underpricing," "liquidity premium," and "paper wealth." Hilferding gives it a formula and insists it is once-only, which correctly predicts that promoter income is front-loaded and non-recurring even where the business is sound. (2) **Why aggregate valuation and aggregate accumulation can diverge for decades without either being an error.** Mainstream q-theory treats high q as an investment signal and is embarrassed when q rises and investment does not; Durand's Piketty critique (ρ up, K/Y flat) treats the divergence as the normal operation of a system where titles are priced in a market that is only loosely coupled to the reproduction of means of production. (3) **The double-capitalisation of a single future income along a supply chain as systematic rather than anomalous.** Marx's Ch. 25 treatment of accommodation paper — where a bill is drawn to retire another and no outsider can tell it from bona fide trade paper — is the direct ancestor of vendor-financed and circular revenue in a capex chain, and the tradition predicts that this is indistinguishable from real transactions *ex ante* and only separable *ex post*. (4) **The separation of the funding question from the real-resource question,** which the mainstream funding debate collapses into one. It also correctly identifies that the operative link from valuation to spending is the *collateral* channel, not a money-supply channel — which is exactly why M2 can grow at nominal GDP pace while the asset complex re-rates.

Where it does **not** beat the mainstream, plainly: it offers no theory of the *level* of valuation (Marx's formula is R/i, i.e. the mainstream's), no timing, no maintained empirical index, and its signature prediction of a rising financial drain on non-financial firms is contradicted by Durand's own Figure 30 outside the United States.

**Observable implications.**
- **Internal-funds gap for non-financial corporates.** Fed Z.1 table F.103 (nonfinancial corporate business) — capital expenditures versus internal funds, i.e. the 'financing gap' line. Durand's Fig. 24 measures the same idea as NFC fixed capital formation as a share of gross operating surplus, and his claim is that it fell across the rich world after 1980 ('profits without accumulation'). The AI capex boom should be the first large reversal. Concrete test: is US NFC capex now exceeding internal funds, and by how much? If the gap is small, external finance is not the marginal funder and the fictitious-capital linkage is weak; if it is large and widening, the tradition's channel is live and you can name the instruments filling it.
- **Foley's measurable version of the gap.** Fed Z.1 table B.103: market value of corporate equities outstanding divided by net worth at market value (the Fed's Tobin's q proxy; FRED mnemonic MVEONWMVBSNNCB — verify before use, I could not reach FRED from this session). Report both the ratio and the dollar difference between equity market value and the replacement cost of the underlying capital. This *is* Foley's fictitious capital (CJE-cited: Foley 1986 p. 115, 1991 p. 116) and the mainstream already publishes it. Compare the current level against the 2000 and 2021 peaks and the postwar mean.
- **Promoter's profit, computed directly.** For each large private AI company: (latest post-money valuation × founder/early-investor ownership) minus cumulative capital actually contributed to the enterprise. This is Hilferding's 900,000-mark residual and Durand's $104.6bn Facebook calculation (pp. 93–94), applied to OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Databricks, CoreWeave pre-IPO. Also compute it in flow terms as realised secondary-sale proceeds by insiders — that fraction is the part of the fiction already converted into unconditional purchasing power, and it is the only part that has actually funded anything.
- **Interest-rate versus cash-flow decomposition of the AI re-rating.** Marx Ch. 29 is explicit that the price of titles moves inversely with the rate of interest 'without any change in the value of the actual capital.' Decompose the change in aggregate market capitalisation of the AI complex since 2023 into (a) realised earnings growth, (b) forward-earnings revision, (c) multiple/discount-rate change. The share attributable to (c) is the part Marx would call movement of the title independent of the underlying capital. This is a clean, standard, fully mainstream computation that operationalises the old claim exactly.
- **Chain double-counting test.** Sum the enterprise values of the AI supply chain (chip designer, foundry, hyperscaler cloud arms, neocloud/GPU lessors, model labs, power/data-centre REITs) and divide by consolidated *end-customer* AI revenue — revenue paid by parties outside the chain. Marx's point that 'the capital does not exist twice over' predicts that the same future income stream is being capitalised at several nodes. A rising ratio of chain EV to external end-revenue is the measurable form of the duplication claim.
- **Accommodation-paper analogue: circular and vendor financing.** Under US GAAP ASU 2022-04, supplier finance programme obligations have been a required disclosure since fiscal 2023; IAS 7/IFRS 7 amendments impose the analogue. Pull these, plus related-party revenue disclosures, plus vendor equity stakes taken in customers (e.g. chip vendor equity investments in model labs and neoclouds), plus prepayments and take-or-pay capacity commitments in 10-K contractual-obligations tables. Marx's Ch. 25 warning is that bona fide and accommodation paper are indistinguishable ex ante, so the measurable object is the *ratio* of vendor-financed or related-party revenue to total revenue and its trend, not a judgement on any single deal.
- **The collateral channel, which is the tradition's actual funding mechanism.** Track: (a) securities-based lending and margin debt; (b) issuance of ABS and private-credit facilities collateralised by GPUs, data-centre leases, or contracted compute revenue (SIFMA / Finsight ABS issuance by collateral type — GPU-backed and data-centre ABS is a new and separately identifiable category); (c) net lending by NBFIs to the sector. The prediction is that money creation appears here and in bank lending against re-rated collateral, not in a re-rating of equity per se.
- **Depreciation-life divergence as an auditable test of the fiction.** Hyperscaler 10-K disclosed useful lives for servers and network equipment (several extended from ~3 to ~5–6 years) versus observed replacement cadence and residual values in the secondary GPU market. If assumed life exceeds economic life, reported earnings are overstated, and every capitalised valuation built on those earnings is overstated by the discounted value of the shortfall. This is the single most concrete place where 'capitalised claim exceeds underlying accumulation' becomes an accounting quantity rather than a metaphor.
- **Rotta's knowledge-rent share, extended.** Reproduce Rotta (2018) with current BEA data: net income of 'knowledge and information rents' (software, data management, R&D, advertising, publishing, recorded media) as a share of net unproductive income — 7.9% in 1947 to 17.4% in 2011 — and BEA intellectual property products investment as a share of private nonresidential fixed investment. On this framework AI IP is a zero-reproduction-cost commodity whose return is a rent on surplus produced elsewhere, so the prediction is that IPP's share keeps rising while the measured return to productive capital does not.
- **Durand's components, used separately, never summed.** BIS credit-to-non-financial-private-sector series, general government debt, and World Bank GFDD stock-market capitalisation, each as % of GDP, for the US and the eleven-country group. Durand's mean rises 151% (1979) to 358% (2014) of GDP for the sum; Japan hits 485% in 2014. Recompute the three separately to 2026. Do not report the sum: it adds face-value liabilities to market-value equity and double-counts every leveraged issuer.
- **Aggregate-realisability test in a drawdown (the strongest ex-post test).** The tradition's core claim is Durand's 'paradox of liquidity' (p. 54): individually liquid, collectively not. In the next drawdown, measure the ratio of aggregate market-capitalisation decline to cumulative net dollar outflow. Gabaix–Koijen's ~5x is the mainstream estimate; the fictitious-capital claim is that this multiplier is the quantitative content of 'illusion', and that it rises when a larger share of the float is pledged as collateral. Measure the multiplier conditional on pledged-collateral intensity.

**Ideology flagged.** Six things, named plainly. (1) **The word "fictitious" is loaded rhetoric attached to neutral arithmetic.** Marx's Ch. 29 mechanism — capitalise the income at the going rate — is the Williams/Gordon dividend discount model. The alarm is not in the mathematics; it is in the adjective. Practitioners should take the arithmetic and discard the adjective. (2) **The labour theory of value premise is a classification convention, not a measurement.** "Only productive labour creates value; all financial and knowledge income is a transfer" is definitionally true within the framework and not independently testable outside it. Rotta's productive/unproductive split, on which his striking 13.4%→53.6% series rests, is a decision about which NAICS industries to label unproductive; the numbers are only as robust as that decision, and classifying all software and R&D as unproductive is a substantive commitment most economists would reject. (3) **The crisis claim is unfalsifiable as usually stated.** Durand (p. 55): over-accumulation of fictitious capital "will inexorably lead to crisis" — with no horizon, no threshold, and no counterfactual. Three decades of a rising ratio punctuated by recoveries is consistent with the thesis and with its negation. (4) **The duplication claim is partly an accounting confusion dressed as a discovery.** "The capital does not exist twice over" is correct, and it is precisely what consolidation of national and sectoral balance sheets already does: intra-sector claims net out. Presenting standard consolidation as an unmasking is rhetorical. (5) **Teleology and moralism.** Titles like *How Finance is Appropriating Our Future*, and categories like Carcanholo & Sabadini's "parasitic speculative capital," encode the conclusion in the vocabulary. Durand's own Figure 30 (p. 138) shows no rising net financial drain on non-financial corporations outside the US, contradicting the drain thesis his framing invites — which is to his credit and to the framing's discredit. (6) **The tradition does not agree with itself on whether the concept is even about a measurable gap.** Foley, Harvey and Carcanholo say yes (and are therefore measurable); Palludeto & Rossi (*CJE* 2022) and Milios say the gap reading is a misreading and the category is about form and fetish, not magnitude. A school that cannot agree whether its central concept denotes a quantity cannot claim that quantity as evidence. Bichler & Nitzan's objection is fatal if taken seriously: measuring the divergence of financial from real capital requires a magnitude for real capital that the Cambridge capital controversy says does not exist independently of prices — so Tobin's q may be comparing a market value to another market value in disguise.

### Analysis

**1. The primary texts, precisely.**

Marx introduces fictitious capital in Vol. III Ch. 25 through bills of exchange, and develops it in Ch. 29 ("Component Parts of Bank Capital"). The mechanism in Ch. 29 is exactly the discounted-present-value formula: an income of £100 at 5% "becomes" a capital of £2,000. Marx's phrase is "capitalised income, that is, the income calculated on the basis of a fictitious capital." He then draws three consequences the mainstream states less sharply. First, price and nominal value diverge: "their market-value is determined differently from their nominal value, without any change in the value of the actual capital" — the price "rises and falls inversely as the rate of interest." Second, duplication: the capital "does not exist twice over," once as the share and again as the plant; it exists only as the plant, the share being a pro-rata title to surplus value the plant will realise (Vol. III, Penguin p. 597; quoted by Durand 2017, p. 53). Third, the state debt is the pure case: the capital was consumed, so the paper is a claim on tax receipts and nothing else — and "as soon as the promissory notes become unsaleable, the illusion of this capital disappears."

Ch. 30 supplies the separation the operator needs: "not every augmentation of loanable money-capital indicates a real accumulation of capital." Money-capital and real capital accumulate on different, sometimes inverse, schedules. Ch. 25 supplies the circularity mechanism: quoting Leatham, Marx notes it is impossible to distinguish real transactions from "accommodation paper, that is, where one bill of exchange is drawn to take up another running," and quoting Gilbart, that "the same £1,000 which you deposit at A today may be reissued tomorrow, and form a deposit at B." Both are analytically live for the AI complex.

**2. Hilferding is where the tradition earns its keep.**

Finance Capital Ch. 7 contains the only genuinely non-mainstream *quantity* in this literature, and the numbers are exact. Take an enterprise with 1,000,000 marks of capital, average profit 15%, so 150,000 marks of annual profit; the prevailing interest rate is 5%. Capitalised at 5%, that income "will have a price of 3,000,000 marks." Hilferding then deducts ~20,000 marks of administration and adds a 2% risk premium, so 130,000 marks is distributed at a 7% dividend yield, giving a share price of roughly 1,900,000 marks. The difference over the 1,000,000 actually invested — about 900,000 marks — is promoter's profit, which "arises only from the conversion of profit-bearing into interest-bearing capital." He is emphatic that this is a *once-only* income, paid to the issuing bank as a lump sum, and that it is a legitimate economic category, not a swindle. He is equally emphatic that the shareholder is a money-capitalist, not an industrial capitalist, and that this is only possible because the stock exchange lets him "recover his capital at any time by selling his shares."

Durand (2017, pp. 93–94) computes a modern instance: Facebook's May 2012 IPO, 421m shares at $38 (15% of the company, $16bn), total capitalisation $106bn against a 2006 Businessweek valuation of ~$2bn; founder's profit on the tranche sold ≈ $15.7bn, on the whole re-rated stock ≈ $104.6bn. The identical calculation is available today for every large private AI company from disclosed round post-money valuations and cumulative capital contributed.

**3. What the modern literature has confirmed, refined and refuted.**

Durand's *Fictitious Capital* (Verso 2017) is the only sustained attempt at measurement. His index (Fig. 11, p. 64) sums three "basic forms" from Marx — credit to the non-financial private sector, government debt, and stock-market capitalisation — each as a share of GDP. The eleven-rich-country mean rises from 151% of GDP in 1979 to 358% in 2014; Japan peaks at 485% in 2014; US stock-market capitalisation goes from 40% of GDP in 1975 to 146% in 2014 (Fig. 10, p. 62). His interpretive claim is disciplined: over three decades "the quantity of value validated in anticipation of future valorisation processes has constantly increased relative to the quantity of wealth actually produced."

**This index has an accounting defect the operator must not inherit.** It adds face-value debt liabilities to market-value equity, and it double-counts every leveraged firm — the corporate bond appears in private credit and the residual equity of the same enterprise appears in market capitalisation. It is a violation of exactly the "never sum across measurement bases" rule. Use the components; do not use the sum.

Durand's more defensible empirical move is Chapter 4's Piketty critique: Piketty's wealth-to-income ratio ρ rises sharply while corporate capital-output ratio K/Y at book value stagnates or falls (citing Piketty & Zucman's own appendix figures A133 and A71). That divergence — capitalised wealth up, capital intensity flat — is the real, checkable, non-trivial phenomenon the tradition names.

Durand is also honest against his own side. On the origin of capital gains (p. 93) he concedes they are "a zero-sum game among the various participants on the market," positive in aggregate only if the asset market attracts additional money-capital, from one of five sources: other asset markets, a decline in bank savings, an increase in overall savings, imported capital, or an increase in bank credit devoted to financial operations. That is a complete and testable decomposition of the operator's funding puzzle. And on the alleged financial drain (Fig. 30, p. 138), he reports that net financial payments as a share of non-financial corporations' gross operating surplus show no rising trend outside the US — "the financial sector makes no net drain on the resources of non-financial firms" — refuting a central claim of the financialisation literature.

Palludeto & Rossi (*CJE* 46(3), 2022, pp. 545–560) go further and attack the operator's intuition head-on. They argue that the widespread reading of fictitious capital as a *measurable detachment* between market price and the value of real capital — Foley (1986, p. 115; 1991, p. 116), Harvey (1982, p. 95), Carcanholo & Sabadini's "Type 2" — is a misreading. For them the category is defined by three attributes: an associated future income, a secondary market, and "real nonexistence"; it is fictitious not as imaginary detachment but because it reifies production relations (citing Milios 2019, p. 123). On this reading a government bond is "neither more nor less fictitious" than a share, and there is no gap to measure. Their positive contribution (§5) is the mechanism that actually matters here: fictitious capital *allocates social labour* — financial-market prices become the benchmark against which every branch of production is compared, so "the instability typical of those markets... becomes inherent to the process of allocating social labour." Their footnote 17 lists the precedents: railway mania, 1920s radio and autos, 1990s US fibre optics, 2000s real estate.

Rotta (2018, PERI/Greenwich) supplies the one series that speaks directly to AI. Classifying knowledge production as unproductive because reproduction cost tends to zero, he measures "knowledge and information rents" (advertising, pharma, software, data management, R&D, publishing, recorded media) rising from 7.9% to 17.4% of net unproductive income between 1947 and 2011, with finance and knowledge-rents jointly nearly doubling from 21.9% to 40.5%. Gross unproductive income relative to value generated in productive activity rises from 13.4% (1948) to 53.6% (2009). On this framework, AI model weights are the limiting case of a zero-reproduction-cost knowledge-commodity, and their capitalised value is a rent claim on surplus produced elsewhere rather than new value.

**4. The internal adversaries, which are strong.**

Sotiropoulos, Milios & Lapatsioras (*Demystifying Finance*, Routledge 2013) reject from inside Marxism the reading of finance as parasitic rent or illusion; they treat derivative markets as efficient pricing and disciplining of capitalist risk, "unthinkable in the absence of derivatives." Lapavitsas (*Profiting Without Producing*, 2013, p. 29) denies there is anything inherently fictitious about loan capital at all. Bichler & Nitzan ("The Mismatch Thesis," 2024) are the sharpest: the claim that financial and real capital diverge presupposes that real capital has an independently measurable magnitude, which the Cambridge capital controversy denies; they call the convention "baseless if not fraudulent," and note that the same authors who say real accumulation drives financial accumulation present data showing the two moving in opposite directions.

**5. Verdict on measurability.** Mostly the framing, with two genuine exceptions. Foley's version of the gap is measurable and the mainstream already measures it under another name (Tobin's q, market-to-book). Hilferding's promoter's profit is measurable and the mainstream does *not* have it as an income category — it books the same quantity as IPO underpricing, liquidity premium, or unrealised founder wealth, and never as a determinate transfer whose size is set by the spread between two capitalisation rates. That is the tradition's one real analytical export.

## Soddy, Fisher, and the wealth-versus-debt asymmetry (virtual wealth; debt-deflation; the nominal-claim / real-collateral divergence)

**Core mechanism.** Nominal claims and the assets that back them are governed by different laws and marked on different bases: a debt contract fixes a number that accrues at a contractual rate regardless of the state of the world, while the collateral behind it is a marginal market price applied to an inframarginal stock, and the physical asset underneath that price depreciates and obsolesces. Soddy's contribution is the accounting insight that the community's real "virtual wealth" — the goods it permanently forgoes in order to hold money-claims instead — is finite and slow-moving, while the issuance of claims against it is bounded only by arithmetic; so the claim/thing ratio can grow without any aggregate showing strain. Fisher's contribution is the dynamics of the unwinding: because the nominal debt is rigid and the collateral value is state-contingent, an attempt by many debtors to reduce leverage simultaneously lowers the value of what they are selling faster than it lowers what they owe, so that in his phrase the more debtors pay, the more they owe (Fisher 1933, pp. 344-45). The mechanism is therefore distributional, not aggregate: it works through *who* holds the loss and how levered they are, not through how large the loss is, which is why it is invisible to representative-agent macro and to M1-M3. To look for it, measure the gap between the tenor and rigidity of claims and the life and price-elasticity of the collateral, and measure the quantity of marked-to-market valuation that has been pledged.

**On investment without prior saving.** Directly: this tradition says the question contains a false premise, and then gives a real answer underneath it. The false premise is that investment needs prior saving. Ex post, S is identically I; nobody has to have abstained first. What must exist before investment is *finance* — a means of payment — and Soddy's insight is that the means of payment is itself a claim on the community's virtual wealth, created by a balance-sheet expansion rather than by anyone going without. Fisher's 100% Money exists precisely because he accepted that banks create the medium when they lend. The Bank of England restated this in 2014. So the correct question is not "where did the saving come from" but "who created the claim, and what did the claim displace." For the AI trade there are four distinguishable sources and they have very different fragility properties. (i) Hyperscaler operating cash flow: this genuinely is prior corporate saving, it is large, and it is the least fragile — no claim is created. (ii) Public bond issuance: 2025 hyperscaler gross issuance topped $100bn, mostly at maturities beyond five years (BIS QR, March 2026). This creates a rigid nominal claim against a depreciating asset — Soddy's asymmetry, on balance sheet and visible. (iii) Off-balance-sheet SPV/JV debt held by private credit funds, with the hyperscaler taking a minority stake and committing to long-term leases or capacity offtake: the BIS's own word is "shadow borrowing", obligations economically akin to debt that reside outside the corporate balance sheet. This is where the tradition earns its keep — the claim exists, it is rigid, it is collateralised by equipment with a 5-6 year disclosed life, and no monetary or corporate-leverage aggregate captures it. (iv) Kalecki: at a 6%+ deficit with full employment, the government deficit *is* corporate profit, so a large part of what appears as "internally funded from cash flow" is investment funded by the public sector's dissaving — a flow that appears in no monetary aggregate. Put together: investment is exceeding measured private saving not because money is being created at pace inside M2 (it is not; +5.5% y/y), but because (a) the profit flow is being manufactured fiscally, (b) the marginal claim is being created outside the perimeter that M2 measures, and (c) marked-up collateral is being monetised through pledging — margin loans +30.5% y/y — which supplies purchasing power without creating a single deposit. Note the counter-fact that cuts the other way and should be stated: 2026Q1 was the first quarter since 2021Q1 in which US nonfinancial corporates were net *issuers* of equity (+$124bn SAAR, Z.1). To the extent the trade is equity-funded, it is funded by a genuine transfer of existing purchasing power, not by claim creation at all.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** Soddy's V = M/P is the right starting point restated operationally: what matters is the stock of claims that can be spent at par, on demand, without a price concession. M1-M3 measure a legal and institutional subset of that, defined by who issues the liability rather than by whether it functions as purchasing power. Four categories sit outside. First, at-par redeemable claims excluded by definition: M2 counts retail money funds only ($3,047.2bn, RMFSL, June 2026) while total MMF financial assets are $8,289.6bn (Z.1, 2026Q1) — different bases and dates, so do not net them, but several trillion of on-demand claims are outside the number the operator is being shown. Add repo claims, Treasury bills held explicitly as cash equivalents, and stablecoin float. Second, *contingent* purchasing power: committed undrawn credit lines and revolvers are spendable on demand and appear in no aggregate until drawn. Third — and this is the specifically Soddy-Fisher point — *pledging capacity*. When a portfolio re-rates upward, the holder acquires the ability to borrow against it at par. No money was created anywhere; the community's virtual wealth is unchanged; only the claims on it grew. Yet the holder can now spend. Broker-dealer receivables due from customers rose from $476.9bn to $622.2bn between 2025Q1 and 2026Q1 (+30.5%) while M2 grew 5.5%: that is purchasing power manufactured out of a price, and it is recorded as an asset of a dealer rather than as money. The symmetric point is that it is destroyed by a price fall, with no monetary contraction to mark it. Fourth, obligations that function as spending commitments without being debt: long-term data-centre leases and capacity offtakes inside SPVs — the BIS's "shadow borrowing". Two disciplines apply. Fisher's own identity MV = PT means a rise in V is observationally equivalent to a rise in M, and this project has already established that collateral velocity is flat for two to three years and that the SFTDS evidence runs against the liquidity-windfall hypothesis — so the velocity channel specifically is not carrying the load, and I will not claim it is. And bank credit is growing faster than bank money (loans and leases +7.2% y/y versus M2 +5.5%), which is itself the observable: the wedge between credit creation and deposit creation is exactly the space in which the aggregate stops being informative.

**On the illusion of wealth.** Soddy's cleanest surviving contribution, with the polemic removed, is that aggregate market valuation is a *price*, not a *fund*. Market capitalisation is the last marginal transaction price multiplied by the entire inframarginal stock, and it is realisable at that price only for the marginal holder. The gap between paper valuation and realisable value is therefore not a measurement error to be corrected — it is what the number means. This is the same object Gabaix and Koijen quantify from the flow side (~$1 of net flow moving ~$5 of capitalisation, symmetric on the way down): if the multiplier is five, then four-fifths of the capitalisation was never money and never could be. The scale: US household net worth $182,979,889mn = $183.0tn (Z.1, 2026Q1), 574% of GDP; nonfinancial corporate equities at market value $69.5tn; against M2 of $23.2tn. Roughly eight units of claimed wealth per unit of the medium in which realisation would have to occur. Say plainly what that does and does not establish. It does not establish fragility: the ratio was 615% of GDP in 2021Q3 and nothing broke. What Fisher adds is the step that converts an illusion into an obligation, and it is the operator's own step: the illusion becomes dangerous only where it has been *pledged*. An unpledged overvaluation deflates and hurts sentiment; a pledged overvaluation deflates and triggers a call for cash against collateral that is worth less at the moment the cash is demanded, while the nominal claim is unchanged — Fisher's paradox, that the more debtors pay the more they owe, translated from the goods price level into the collateral price. The measurable version of "illusion of wealth" is therefore not the market-cap-to-money ratio at all. It is: what fraction of market capitalisation has been pledged, at what haircut, to lenders who will re-mark it? That fraction is the part of the illusion that has been converted into a hard, dated, nominal obligation. On the public data it is small at the margin-loan level (0.90% of nonfinancial corporate equity market value, below the 0.95% of 2021Q4) and unmeasured everywhere else — securities-based lending, NAV loans, portfolio-margined derivative exposures, and bilateral repo. The honest conclusion is that the operator's mechanism is correct in structure and unmeasured in magnitude, and that the unmeasured part is exactly the part a FIG structurer at a dealer can see and the public data cannot.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) Why quiet aggregates and a violent margin are consistent. Fisher's mechanism is distributional — it operates through which balance sheets hold the loss and how levered they are — so it is structurally invisible to representative-agent models and to any monetary aggregate. Von Peter (BIS WP176) says this outright: the representative agent and log-linearisation are ill-suited to this territory, which is why formal work on debt-deflation is scarce. (2) Why the trigger can be trivially small relative to the outcome. Brunnermeier-Pedersen note subprime losses of a few hundred billion, about 5% of US equity market capitalisation, alongside more than $8tn of equity losses. A wealth-effect or fundamentals framework cannot generate that ratio; a framework in which the losses land on the levered price-setters can. (3) Why the nominal/real distinction is not a netting identity. Standard accounts treat a debt and its collateral as two sides that cancel in aggregate. Fisher's whole point is that one side is rigid and the other is state-contingent and marked on a different basis, so they do not cancel in the state of the world where it matters — and the operator is right that this is exactly the collateral-call mechanism. (4) Why depreciation policy is a monetary variable. Soddy's asymmetry says that if the useful-life estimate is wrong, the equity cushion supporting the debt was never there; a change in an accounting estimate is then a change in the real quantity of loss-absorbing capital in the system, which appears in no financial-conditions index. Mainstream finance treats useful life as an accounting convention; here it is the location of the future writedown.

### Analysis

**1. Soddy, stripped.** Frederick Soddy's usable idea is a definition, not a prophecy. In *Wealth, Virtual Wealth and Debt* (1926) he defines virtual wealth as the aggregate of exchangeable goods and services the community continuously and permanently goes without in order to hold money instead (Daly, *HOPE* 12(4), 1980, pp. 469-88, reconstructs this as V = M/P: the real value of the money stock is pinned by the community's willingness to abstain). Two consequences follow that are analytically clean. First, money is not wealth but a *claim* on the community's wealth — from the community's side a debt, a negative quantity. Second, the size of that claim stock is set by issuance, while V is set by real behaviour; the two are independent, and their divergence must show up somewhere, in P or, once we admit assets, in asset prices.

Soddy then adds the asymmetry he is remembered for: debt obeys the mathematics of compound interest, real wealth obeys thermodynamics and decays (*Cartesian Economics*, 1922). Discard the rhetoric; keep the structure. The defensible core is narrower than Soddy's version and is not a physical law at all: nominal claims are rigid and accrue; the collateral behind them is state-contingent and physically depreciates; and the two are recorded on different measurement bases, so netting them is the error. Soddy's genuine contribution is the epistemic one — the standing confusion of a claim with a thing.

**2. Fisher, precisely.** Fisher's 1933 *Econometrica* paper names two dominant factors, over-indebtedness and deflation, and runs a nine-link chain from a state of over-indebtedness: (1) debt liquidation leading to distress selling and (2) contraction of deposit currency as loans are repaid, plus a slowing of velocity, causing (3) a fall in the price level — "a swelling of the dollar"; (4) a still greater fall in business net worths, precipitating bankruptcies; (5) a fall in profits; (6) reduced output, trade and employment; (7) pessimism and loss of confidence; (8) hoarding, slowing velocity further; and (9) disturbance of interest rates — nominal rates falling while real rates rise (Fisher 1933, p. 342 for links 1-3; reproduced with pagination in von Peter, BIS WP No. 176, 2005, §1.1). The famous passage is the stability condition: if over-indebtedness is great enough, liquidation defeats itself, because the fall in the number of dollars owed is slower than the rise in the value of each dollar owed; the boat does not right itself but capsizes (Fisher 1933, pp. 344-45).

Note what Fisher does and does not give the operator. He gives the exact asymmetry the operator described: nominal obligation fixed, real value of the thing that discharges it variable, and a self-defeating collective exit. He does *not* give a theory of the boom — over-indebtedness is asserted as a starting state — and his transmission runs through the *goods* price level via a quantity-theory link (loan repayment destroys deposits, deposits are money, money determines P). In a fiat system with an elastic central-bank balance sheet and no goods deflation, that specific link is broken. Minsky's amendment (1963, 1982; von Peter §1.2) substitutes *asset* prices for the price level: distress selling lowers asset prices directly, which causes losses at maturing debts, which reinforces distress selling. That is the version that applies. Running Fisher's 1933 chain unamended in 2026 is crankery.

Fisher's *100% Money* (1935) is a reform programme, not a mechanism, and belongs here only for the money/credit distinction it forces: what banks create when they lend is a means of payment, so command over real resources can precede any abstention. The Bank of England stated the same thing plainly (McLeay, Radia & Thomas, *QB* 2014 Q1).

**3. The chain, with links attributed.** Formalising the operator's sentence:

- L0 *Finance precedes saving.* Ex post S ≡ I always; what must exist before investment is a means of payment, not prior abstention. DOCUMENTED (BoE 2014; Werner 2014). Soddy's framing: the community lends its own virtual wealth to a private borrower.
- L1 *Claims are rigid and accrue.* Definitional.
- L2 *Collateral depreciates faster than the claim amortises.* Soddy's asymmetry, made measurable as (useful life) − (financing tenor). OPERATOR'S/MY INFERENCE as applied to AI.
- L3 *Valuation is a marginal price on an inframarginal stock.* Soddy's epistemic point; quantified by inelastic markets (Gabaix-Koijen ~5×). DOCUMENTED.
- L4 *The valuation is pledged.* Margin loans, repo, securities-based and NAV lending, GPU- and data-centre-backed ABS/CMBS convert an unrealisable valuation into realised cash. This is the step that turns Soddy's "illusion" into a hard obligation. DOCUMENTED and measurable.
- L5 *Exit moves price more than flow.* DOCUMENTED (established elsewhere in this project).
- L6 *Price fall → net worth fall → forced sale, at a discount, because the high-valuation bidders are similarly constrained.* Fisher links 1 and 4; Shleifer-Vishny (1992; *JEP* 2011). Measured discounts: 10-20% on distressed-airline aircraft (Pulvino 1998); ~27% on foreclosed homes (Campbell-Giglio-Pathak); convertibles normally mispriced under 2% trading at 10% discounts in 2008 (Mitchell-Pulvino). DOCUMENTED.
- L7 *Haircuts widen endogenously.* Geanakoplos: supply and demand set the margin as well as the rate, and "scary" bad news — news that raises volatility *and* disagreement — tightens it; his Ellington series runs ~20% margins, spiking to 40% in 1998, ~10% in 2005-07, over 40% in late 2007, 70%+ by 2009Q2. Brunnermeier-Pedersen separate the *margin spiral* (margins rise in illiquidity, tightening funding) from the *loss spiral* (losses on existing positions force further sales) and show the two reinforce each other superadditively. THEORY DOCUMENTED; EMPIRICS WEAKEST HERE (see below).
- L8 *The loss lands on the natural buyers.* Geanakoplos: the price-setting leveraged optimists are wiped out and the asset migrates to pessimists; BP note subprime losses of a few hundred billion, ~5% of equity market cap, coincided with >$8tn of equity losses, because the losses sat on levered maturity-mismatched balance sheets. DOCUMENTED.
- L9 *Nominal debt unchanged; real burden up.* Fisher, with Minsky's substitution of collateral price for goods price.

**4. The steelman.** Two findings cut against the operator. First, von Peter's BIS model — built explicitly to reproduce Fisher's features — finds debt-deflation *stable* even under large shocks, high indebtedness and widespread default, because agents without prior debt or losses borrow to exploit the expected price recovery; it turns unstable only when agents actively contain indebtedness (margin requirements are his named example) or when intermediation is impaired. Collapse is not automatic; the binding condition is the presence and funding of unlevered natural buyers. Second, the empirical case for L7 — the operator's key link — is contested. Krishnamurthy, Nagel & Orlov (*JF* 2014) find repo against private collateral was small, that MMF-to-dealer haircuts rose far less than the bilateral haircuts Gorton-Metrick report, that the contraction was concentrated in ABCP, and that 2008 looks more like a dealer credit crunch than a depositor run. Gorton-Metrick's rejoinder is that over half the repo market is simply unobserved. So the haircut spiral is the least-measured link in a chain that depends on it.

**5. Where this earns its place today.** Bank money is not the story: M2 $23.16tn June 2026, +5.5% y/y, roughly nominal GDP pace. But bank *credit* is running faster — loans and leases at all commercial banks $13.956tn on 5 Aug 2026 versus $13.015tn a year earlier, +7.2% — and the pledged-valuation channel is running much faster still: Z.1 broker-dealer receivables due from customers (margin loans) $622.2bn in 2026Q1 against $476.9bn in 2025Q1, +30.5%. That is the Fisher-relevant series and it is compounding at roughly six times M2. Against that, honesty requires the ratio: those margin loans are 0.90% of nonfinancial corporate equity market value ($69.5tn, Z.1 2026Q1), versus 0.95% at the 2021Q4 peak. The *level* is unremarkable; the *rate of change* is not. And the price of the asymmetry is being ignored: ICE BofA US HY OAS was 2.73% on 19 Aug 2026, inside the lowest ~13% of daily observations of the trailing three years.

The Soddy point applies most sharply to depreciation. AI infrastructure is financed with instruments whose tenor exceeds the disclosed useful life of the equipment — hyperscalers report 5-6 year server/network lives, against bonds mostly beyond five years (BIS *QR* March 2026) and leases far longer — and useful life under GAAP is an entity-specific management estimate, so an extension raises reported profit with no change in cash. That is Soddy's confusion of a claim with a thing, occurring inside an audited accounting policy. The BIS's own term for the SPV/JV structures — minority stake, long-term lease or capacity offtake, debt held by private credit funds — is "shadow borrowing": obligations economically akin to debt residing outside the corporate balance sheet.

## Gurley & Shaw, Tobin, Kaldor — non-bank near-money, portfolio balance, and endogenous money

**Core mechanism.** Three linked mechanisms, one composite claim. (1) Gurley & Shaw (1956, 1960): any intermediary — not only a bank — can issue an "indirect" liability that is a close substitute for money against a claim on real investment, so the set of instruments performing money services is an economic variable, not a definitional constant; a fixed-boundary aggregate therefore mismeasures liquidity by construction whenever the composition of the claim structure changes. (2) Tobin (1963, 1969): asset prices are set by market-clearing across the whole balance-sheet matrix of the economy (rows = assets, columns = sectors, each sector's demands functions of the return vector subject to a net-worth adding-up constraint); a shift in any sector's portfolio-demand function, with no change in any money stock, raises the market valuation of existing capital relative to its replacement cost (q), and it is q, not money, that calls forth investment. Because the own-rate on money is fixed, adjustment to any supply change must run entirely through other assets' prices. (3) Kaldor (1970, 1982) and the Post-Keynesians: the quantity of credit is demand-determined at a policy-set price, loans create deposits, and the aggregate is therefore an outcome of the boom, not a cause of it or a constraint on it. Joint prediction: an investment boom can be financed against a flat monetary aggregate provided the *moneyness* of the existing claim stock rises, portfolio-demand functions shift, or non-bank intermediaries expand indirect debt — and the observable signatures sit in user-cost-weighted liquidity, in q, and in the composition of the safe-asset stock, never in M2.

**On investment without prior saving.** The three thinkers do not agree, and the disagreement is the useful part. Gurley & Shaw explicitly DENY that intermediation creates saving: intermediation raises the ratio of gross financial assets to wealth, economises on money holdings and raises velocity, but it reallocates a given saving flow rather than manufacturing purchasing power. Tobin is more deflationary still — the New View kills the widow's cruse, and in his 1969 accounting, internally generated financial assets sum to zero across the economy, so intermediation adds liquidity, not wealth. But Tobin then supplies the answer by another route: financial events raise q, the market valuation of capital relative to replacement cost, and investment responds to q. Ex ante no prior saving is required, because the investment spending generates the income out of which the matching saving is made; ex post the identity holds trivially. Tobin's channel is a valuation channel, not a funding channel — and that distinction is the crux for your question. Kaldor and the Post-Keynesians assert the funding version directly: loans create deposits, Keynes's 1937 finance motive makes credit a revolving fund logically prior to and independent of saving, and the constraints are lender risk appetite, borrower creditworthiness, bank capital, and — Tobin's condition, which the structuralists concede and the horizontalists do not — someone's willingness to hold the resulting liability at the offered rate. APPLIED TO THE AI TRADE, the honest answer is mostly Tobin, not Kaldor. Van Nieuwerburgh's decomposition puts the buildout at roughly 60% equity, and of the 40% debt about 70% is private credit funds drawing committed capital from insurers, pensions and sovereigns — reallocation of an existing saving flow, not deposit creation. The genuine endogenous-money channel is narrow: construction lending, subscription and NAV lines, warehouse facilities. Consistent with that, commercial paper — the classic private money-manufacturing instrument — shrank 2.4% y/y to June 2026. So the tradition licenses the general claim that investment need not wait on prior saving, but for this specific boom the dominant identified mechanism is a portfolio-preference shift toward longer-duration, less liquid, higher-risk claims at unchanged aggregate saving. That is precisely why savings rates are flat, nothing was sold, and the aggregates show nothing.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** The argument is correct and can be pushed a long way, but not as far as it usually is. What is genuinely outside M2 today: institutional money-fund shares, large time deposits, repo and eurodollars (all dropped when the Fed discontinued M3 in March 2006), T-bills held by non-banks, stablecoins (~$308bn, ~99.5% dollar-denominated, August 2026), semi-liquid fund shares (~$535bn of evergreen vehicles at end-2025), and ~$660bn of unrecognised hyperscaler lease commitments. US money-fund assets alone are ~$7.93tn against an M2 of ~$23.2tn, and only the retail slice is inside M2. The Fed's own November 2024 FEDS note concedes it does not know where stablecoins belong; the 2026 H.6 change netting IRA and Keogh balances out of M2 shows the line is administrative. So "the money boundary is arbitrary" is not a rhetorical claim — it is the Fed's stated position. BUT the argument stops short of what you need it to prove. First, the measured divergence is modest: Divisia M4 grew 6.8% y/y in June 2026 against simple-sum M2 at 5.4%, a gap of about 140bp, and only ~40bp of that is T-bill-supply driven. Directionally right, quantitatively small. Second, the near-money that IS growing is not the classic shadow variety: commercial paper contributed nothing, and the acceleration is dominated by demand deposits (unweighted +25.6% y/y, 27.0% Divisia weight, 6.1 of the 6.8pp) — a composition shift WITHIN M2 toward the highest-moneyness component, which simple summation cannot see because it adds a demand deposit and a savings balance one-for-one. That is Barnett's critique validated, and it is the single most interesting series in this whole exercise, subject to first verifying it is not an artefact of the Feb-2021 or 2026 H.6 methodology changes. Third, the strongest safe-asset evidence cuts the other way: the safe-asset share has been ~31.1% of total US assets with s.d. 2.3% since 1952, and after 2008 production moved BACK toward bank deposits (27% of safe financial debt pre-crisis, 35% by 2020). Where the argument becomes unfalsifiable: the moment "near-money" means any claim redeemable near par, the statement "hidden money was created" is true in every credit cycle by construction. Two disciplines keep it honest — Barnett's (weight by measured user cost, so moneyness is an estimated price, not a definition) and Gorton's (moneyness is observable as zero haircut and absent price discovery). Anything not tied to one of those is not a claim, it is a mood.

**On the illusion of wealth.** This tradition has the cleanest formal statement of your intuition, and it is Tobin's. In his 1969 accounting matrix, internally generated financial assets sum to zero across the economy; only outside money and real capital are net. Gurley & Shaw's inside/outside distinction is the same point. It follows as an accounting matter, not a forecast, that the aggregate private sector cannot realise its posted valuations: realisation is available to an individual holder and definitionally unavailable to all holders at once. Tobin's q is the measure of the gap — market valuation over replacement cost — and Tobin's conclusion is that financial events work by moving q, while the valuation itself creates no net wealth. Gabaix & Koijen's inelastic-markets estimate (~$1 of net equity flow moving ~$5 of market capitalisation, symmetric downward) is the modern quantification of exactly this, and is best read as Tobin portfolio balance made empirical: imperfect substitutability plus a wealth adding-up constraint yields a finite, estimable price impact of aggregate exit. THE COLLATERAL POINT IS MEASURABLE IN THIS CYCLE. Meta's end-2025 leverage was 25% against book equity and 4% against market equity — a market-to-book near 6x. The Hyperion/Beignet structure sits at roughly 90% LTV ($27bn debt on a $30bn asset), is rated A+ one notch below Meta, and is underwritten by a residual value guarantee whose value is the guarantor's capacity to make lenders whole. That capacity is assessed against the market number. Moody's counts ~$970bn of hyperscaler lease commitments with ~$660bn unrecognised on balance sheet — GAAP recognises neither the future lease obligations nor the RVG, though one of the two must materialise. A reversion of hyperscaler valuations toward book would raise measured leverage roughly sixfold at precisely the moment the collateral (single-tenant campuses, GPUs with short economic lives and obsolescence risk) is impaired and the RVG is called. Data-centre REIT betas have already moved from ~0.5 to ~1, so these assets no longer diversify the shock that would trigger the call. THE SMALL-SCALE LIVE DEMONSTRATION IS ALREADY RUNNING. Non-traded BDCs and interval funds promise par-ish quarterly redemption against level-3 assets — Gurley-Shaw near-money in its purest current form, invisible to every aggregate. Requests went from 1.6% of NAV in Q3 2025 to 4.8% in Q4 2025 to roughly 10% of NAV at several large funds in Q2 2026, against 5% caps; gates closed. In Gorton's terms the claims flipped from information-insensitive to information-sensitive, and at that moment they stopped being money. That is your scenario, at ~$535bn scale, observable in EDGAR tender filings today.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) Why stable simple-sum M2 is uninformative: Divisia weights components by user cost, so a composition shift toward high-moneyness balances raises measured liquidity services with no change in the sum. June 2026 shows this live — demand deposits +25.6% y/y contributing 6.1 of DM4's 6.8pp, while simple-sum M2 grew 5.4%. Nothing in the mainstream framework generates or looks for that wedge. (2) Why asset prices moved so far under ZIRP with no aggregate response: Tobin 1969 — when money's own rate is pinned, the entire adjustment to any supply or preference change must run through other assets' prices. The mechanism requires no money creation at all, only imperfect substitutability plus a fixed own-rate. (3) Why the money/credit boundary keeps moving and why that is endogenous rather than accidental: intermediaries manufacture near-money wherever a convenience premium exists (Sunderam, Krishnamurthy–Vissing-Jorgensen), so regulating one issuer relocates production rather than reducing it — the constancy of the safe-asset share at ~31% (s.d. 2.3%) against a 2.5x rise in assets/GDP is the evidence. (4) Why leverage measured against market equity understates fragility: the tradition insists inside claims net to zero across the private sector, so an aggregate exit at posted valuations is not a possibility the accounts admit. IMPORTANT COUNTER, stated plainly: the mainstream New Keynesian core does not predict an M2 boom either — it has no balance sheets and no role for aggregates. So "M2 is flat" refutes monetarism, which few now defend; it is not by itself a point against the modern consensus. This tradition's advantage is that it tells you where else to look, not that it wins an argument the mainstream is having.

### Analysis

**1. Gurley & Shaw: the shadow-banking question, answered in 1956.**

The 1956 *Journal of Finance* paper (11(2): 257–276) and *Money in a Theory of Finance* (Brookings, 1960) make an accounting point before they make an economic one. Gurley & Shaw split securities into *primary* debt (directly funding real investment) and *indirect* debt (pass-through claims issued by intermediaries), and money into *inside* (financing private primary debt, netting to zero across the private sector) and *outside* (net). Their economic claim follows: "each kind of non-monetary intermediary can borrow, go into debt, issue its own characteristic obligations — in short, it can create credit, though not in monetary form." Banks differ from savings institutions only in that they run the payment mechanism and carry reserve requirements — a regulatory, not an ontological, distinction. Two of their observations still bind: the money/non-money line is institutional, and the two banking functions are "at least incompatible," since a payments utility cannot afford a shadow of insolvency while an intermediary in a growing economy must take risk.

The strong Gurley-Shaw/Radcliffe corollary — that NBFI growth would neuter monetary control — was substantially wrong in the event. The 1960s–70s Regulation Q disintermediation episodes went the other way: when rates rose, the public pulled funds *out* of intermediaries and bought primary securities directly, and central banks retained control of the short rate throughout. That failure should be conceded up front; the measurement critique survived, the monetary-impotence thesis did not.

**2. Tobin: the New View is a constraint, not a licence.**

Tobin's "Commercial Banks as Creators of 'Money'" (CFDP 159 / CFP 205, 1963) is routinely misread as friendly to "banks print money." It is the opposite. Banks have no widow's cruse. Deposit expansion is bounded by the public's portfolio preferences and by the marginal cost of attracting liabilities against the marginal return on assets; the scale of the banking system is a profit-maximising outcome, not a multiple of reserves. Tobin and Brainard (*AER* 53(2): 383–400, 1963) formalised the intermediary case. Palley's survey cites exactly this against the horizontalists: agents have limited demands for the liabilities banks offer, so banks must pay more to place them, and loan rates rise as they do. Tobin's objection is the binding constraint on any "credit creates itself" story and it has never been answered.

Tobin (1969, *JMCB* 1(1): 15–29) supplies the mechanism the operator actually described. Sectors hold portfolios of imperfectly substitutable assets; each row of the matrix clears; internally generated financial assets sum to zero. Because money's own rate is fixed, Tobin's key line is: when supply changes and the own rate cannot rise, "the whole adjustment must take place through reductions in other rates or increases in prices of other assets." That is ZIRP, written in 1969. And his conclusion is the answer to "why doesn't M2 show it": financial events affect demand principally by moving the valuation of physical assets relative to replacement cost, and there is "no reason to think that the impact will be captured in any single" monetary stock or market interest rate. Crucially, changes in portfolio *preferences* — not supplies — do the same work. An expectations shift about AI returns is a shift in the asset-demand functions; it moves q with no monetary event at all.

**3. Kaldor and the horizontalist/structuralist split.**

Kaldor's "The New Monetarism" (*Lloyds Bank Review*, 1970) reversed Friedman's causation: money is endogenous, running from Y to M. Moore's *Horizontalists and Verticalists* (1988) pushed this to a horizontal credit-supply curve at the policy rate with automatic central-bank accommodation. Pollin (1991, *JPKE* 13(3): 366–96) named the alternative: structuralists hold that banks' own asset and liability management, not central-bank passivity, does the accommodating, and that the supply curve slopes up. Palley sides with the structuralists and generalises usefully — with the decline of traditional banking, the object is "endogenous *finance*," not endogenous money, and the relevant agents are all institutions that can create highly liquid liabilities. That is Gurley & Shaw reappearing inside Post-Keynesianism, and it is where the two halves of this tradition join.

Official doctrine has since conceded the direction of causation (Bank of England *Quarterly Bulletin* 2014 Q1; Bundesbank *Monthly Report*, April 2017): loans create deposits, the multiplier is not a description of practice, and banks are constrained by profitability, capital and regulation. What was conceded is causation, not quantity. "Loans create deposits" is silent on *how much*, and horizontalism's remaining unfalsifiable residue is the assertion that the central bank has no choice.

**4. How far the "invisible money" argument goes — and where it stops.**

The modern safe-asset literature is the empirical wing of Gurley & Shaw. Gorton & Pennacchi (1990) reframed intermediation as the production of information-insensitive debt. Krishnamurthy & Vissing-Jorgensen (*JPE* 2012) priced it: Treasury convenience lowered yields by 73bp on average, 1926–2008, and their 2015 *JFE* paper (US data 1875–2014) shows government safe debt crowds out privately issued short-term debt. Sunderam (*RFS* 2015) tested whether shadow-bank paper was actually treated as money and found up to roughly half of mid-2000s ABCP growth attributable to money demand. Nagel (*QJE* 2016) shows near-money liquidity premia track the short rate.

But the same literature imposes the discipline. Gorton, Lewellen & Metrick (*AER* 2012, NBER w17777) find the safe-asset share of total US assets constant near 33% (high estimate 33.2%, low 31.0%) from 1952 to 2011Q3, with an economically negligible trend, while total assets went from 4× to 10× GDP. The Fed's 2020 update (FEDS note, Nov 2020) puts it at mean 31.1%, s.d. 2.3%, 1952–2020. Composition rotated violently — bank deposits fell from ~80% of safe financial debt in the 1950s–60s to 70% in 1978 to 27% pre-crisis — but *reversed after 2008*, back to 35% by 2020. On its own preferred metric, the tradition's strong claim has recently been running the wrong way.

The current readings are similarly modest. CFS Divisia (June 2026): DM4 +6.8% y/y, DM4− (excluding Treasuries) +6.4%, DM3 +6.5%, DM2 +6.1%, DM1 +6.6% — against year-earlier readings of 3.9/4.1/4.0/3.6/4.0. Fed H.6 simple-sum M2 for the same month: +5.4%. The DM4-versus-M2 gap is roughly 140bp. That is a real, signed, correctly-directed divergence. It is not a boom, and only ~40bp of the broad-liquidity acceleration is T-bill-supply driven. Notably, commercial paper — the classic shadow-money instrument — contributed essentially nothing (weight 0.9%, unweighted −2.4% y/y). The single striking item is demand deposits: unweighted +25.6% y/y with a 27.0% Divisia weight, contributing 6.1 of the 6.8 percentage points, while other liquid deposits fell. That figure appears identically in H.6 and in the CFS release, but it must be checked against the Feb-2021 H.6 restructuring and the 2026 IRA/Keogh netting change before being treated as economic. If it is economic, it is precisely Barnett's point: the *moneyness* of a barely-changed stock rose, and simple summation cannot see it.

**5. Application to the AI trade.**

Van Nieuwerburgh's JEP draft (March 2026) gives the funding decomposition: ~$2.9tn of investment 2025–28, more than half external, roughly 60/40 equity/debt, and within the debt about $800bn private credit (~70%), $200bn corporate bonds (17%), $150bn structured (13%), with asset-level LTVs of 70–80%. Private credit funds draw committed capital from insurers, pensions and sovereigns — reallocation of an existing saving flow, not deposit creation. So the Kaldorian channel is present but narrow (construction lending, subscription and NAV lines, warehouse facilities); the *dominant* identified channel is Tobin's: a shift in portfolio-demand functions toward longer-duration, less liquid, higher-risk claims at unchanged saving.

What the tradition does identify is a safe-asset factory. Hyperion/Beignet: a $30bn asset with $27bn of debt (90% LTV), rated A+, the largest single investment-grade corporate issuance in US history (October 2025), at 6.58% — at least 100bp above Meta's unsecured cost, over $5bn of extra interest, bought for balance-sheet optics. Moody's counts ~$970bn of hyperscaler lease commitments, ~$660bn unrecognised on balance sheet. That is Gurley-Shaw indirect debt at scale, appearing in no monetary aggregate and no credit aggregate. And the information-insensitivity is manufactured — by an IG tenant and a residual value guarantee — which is exactly Gorton's condition for a claim to become collateral.

## Minsky and Godley — the financial instability hypothesis, the two-price system, and stock-flow-consistent sectoral accounting

**Core mechanism.** Capitalism runs two price levels with different determinants. Output prices are a markup on unit costs anchored by money wages; capital-asset prices are the capitalisation of expected quasi-rents net of carrying cost plus a liquidity premium (Keynes GT ch. 17: q − c + l + a). A fall in the marginal liquidity premium — which is what financial innovation in position-making instruments does — raises the demand price of capital assets relative to their supply price with no effect whatever on output prices and no requirement that any monetary aggregate change. Godley's accounting then constrains what can follow: every sector's net lending sums to zero, but the revaluation term (NKG) in ΔNetWorth = NetLending + NKG + OtherVolumeChanges is unconstrained in size, is never a transaction, and can therefore grow without limit relative to saving. The two claims fit together: Minsky supplies the reason the price of capital assets moves independently of output prices, Godley supplies the accounting that says the resulting wealth was never anyone's income and cannot be realised in aggregate.

**On investment without prior saving.** Directly: it cannot, ex post, and it never needs to, ex ante. Ex post, I ≡ S for the world as a whole — no exception, and anyone who says otherwise has made an error. For a *single sector* I can exceed S, financed by net borrowing, and the flow-of-funds records it as negative net lending. But the US nonfinancial corporate sector is not doing that: in 2026:Q1 its gross saving ($3,979bn SAAR) exceeded its capital expenditure ($3,200bn) by $779bn, the largest surplus in the post-war record. Ex ante, investment requires **finance**, not prior saving. Keynes (1937, *EJ*) settles this: finance is a revolving fund that employs no savings and is, for the community as a whole, a book-keeping transaction; the investment market can never be congested through shortage of saving. Godley & Lavoie formalise it as the finance/funding distinction: *initial finance* is a balance-sheet operation (a loan, a bond, a drawdown of existing balances); *final funding* is completed by the saving the investment itself generates through the multiplier. Kalecki supplies the closure — profits are determined by investment plus the government deficit minus workers' saving plus the trade balance — so the retained earnings that "fund" the capex ex post are *created by* the capex and the deficit. Crucially, the money stock need not rise for either reason: (a) the revolving fund means a constant stock of deposits supports an unbounded flow of finance if turnover is fast enough — Minsky's 1957 point about position-making innovation raising velocity; (b) when private credit funds lend, they lend *existing* deposits, creating no new ones. So flat M2 is not evidence that no funding is occurring. It is what you should expect when funding runs through a revolving fund, through non-bank intermediation of existing balances, and out of a profit stream generated by a 7.7%-of-GDP fiscal deficit.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** Minsky's 1957 QJE paper is the direct answer and it predates the ZIRP episode the operator describes by fifty years: institutional innovation in position-making lets a given money stock carry a larger volume of positions. Purchasing power for asset transactions is not the money stock; it is the money stock times its turnover in asset markets, plus the credit that can be created against positions at the moment of transaction. Four categories are invisible to M1–M3 by construction. (i) **Institutional money-fund shares.** Total MMF assets were $8.29tn at 2026:Q1; only retail shares are in M2. (ii) **Repo and securities lending** — a repo creates settlement balances for the borrower without creating a deposit; the Z.1 and OFR repo collections measure it, M2 does not. (iii) **Margin and prime-brokerage financing** — FINRA margin debt is published; prime-broker balances are not. (iv) **Cross-currency funding** — the >$80tn FX swap/forward stock the project has already established is dollar purchasing power created off balance sheet and outside every domestic aggregate. The deeper point is Godley's: M2 is a *stock* on the liability side of one sector's balance sheet. Asset transactions are *gross flows*, and gross flows are unbounded relative to the stock. There is no accounting relationship at all between the size of M2 and the volume of asset purchases that can be settled through it in a quarter. The operator's ZIRP observation is therefore not a puzzle; it is the expected outcome. What monetary policy did was compress l, the liquidity premium in Keynes's own-rate formula, which raises P_K directly and leaves P_C untouched.

**On the illusion of wealth.** The gap is measurable and currently about $31tn. Nonfinancial corporate equity outstanding at market value is $69.51tn (2026:Q1) against the sector's net worth at current replacement cost of $38.22tn — a ratio of 1.82, having peaked at 1.94 in 2025:Q3, the highest in the 1945–2026 series, against 1.47 at the dot-com peak and a 1945–95 median of 0.66. The Fed's own aggregate makes the double-count explicit: U.S. net wealth (Z.1 B.1) is $166.68tn while household net worth alone is $182.98tn, because households carry corporate equity at market while national wealth carries the underlying assets at current cost. Over four quarters to 2026:Q1, 80% of the $13.37tn increase in household net worth was revaluation, not transactions. **But "illusion" is the wrong word for the whole of it, and this is where the tradition must be disciplined.** Minsky's P_K capitalises expected quasi-rents, and quasi-rents legitimately include monopoly rents and returns to intangible capital that is expensed rather than capitalised (Corrado, Hulten & Sichel). Replacement cost genuinely understates the capital stock. The gap is a *forecast*, not a fraud. The Minsky–Godley claim is narrower and defensible: the gap has no funding behind it and no counterparty obligation behind it, so it can close without any sector defaulting on anything — and the closing shows up only in the revaluation account, where nothing is owed. What *is* owed is the debt that was written against it. The operator's collateral-call intuition is exactly right in structure: a fall in P_K does not change any sector's net lending, but it changes the value of pledged collateral, which forces units into what Minsky called making position by selling position — and *that* is a transaction, which does move prices, which reduces collateral again. Debt-to-market-value ratios that currently look benign (nonfinancial corporate debt at 16.9% of equity market value, down from 25.6% in 2019) look benign only because the denominator is the inflated term. Against net worth at current cost the same debt is 37.9%. That divergence between the two leverage measures *is* the illusion, expressed as a ratio.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) **Why assets and not CPI, without invoking money.** The two-price system gives asset prices and output prices genuinely different determinants — capitalisation rate and liquidity premium versus markup on money wages. A representative-agent model with one price level cannot state the question; a quantity-theoretic account has to explain away flat M2. Minsky predicts the decoupling as the normal case. (2) **Why net worth grows at a multiple of saving with no credit growth.** Godley's ΔNW = NL + NKG + OVC makes revaluation a separate account that is not a transaction and is not bounded. Mainstream national accounting has the same identity but almost never uses it as an analytical object; the Z.1 revaluation tables (S.1M.r, S.11.1.r) are barely cited outside this tradition. (3) **Why the funding question has an answer with no aggregate credit expansion.** Kalecki plus the sectoral identity: a 7.7%-of-GDP government deficit is generating a corporate profit stream large enough that nonfinancial corporates ran a record $779bn internal-funds surplus in 2026:Q1. The trade is being funded out of profits whose ultimate source is the deficit. No M2 growth is required and none is observed. (4) **Why aggregate exit is impossible rather than merely unlikely.** Stock-flow consistency, not behavioural assumption: a revaluation cannot be converted into purchasing power except by a transaction with an equal and opposite counterparty. Household equity plus mutual fund holdings ($58.3tn) stand against household money-like assets of $20.5tn and an M2 of $23.2tn. The claims are roughly three times the money. That is the operator's intuition as an identity.

### Analysis

**1. Minsky's two prices, stated so you can look for them.** Minsky's clearest exposition is *John Maynard Keynes* (1975, chs. 4–5) and *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986, chs. 7–9). He splits Keynes into two price systems. Current-output prices (P_I for investment goods, P_C for consumption goods) are cost-plus: P = (1+markup) × unit cost, with money wages the anchor. Capital-asset prices (P_K) are the capitalised value of expected quasi-rents, valued by the same Chapter 17 own-rate logic Keynes applied to every asset: total return = q − c + l + a. Money's return is almost entirely l, the liquidity premium. Investment occurs where the demand price exceeds the supply price — Keynes GT ch. 17, that assets whose normal supply-price is below their demand-price will be newly produced; and GT ch. 12, that Stock Exchange revaluations exert a decisive influence on current investment because nobody builds what can be bought cheaper.

Tymoigne's reconstruction (Levy/Minsky Summer School 2010) makes the financial structure explicit. The supply price relevant to an externally-funded buyer is P_I^s = P_I + the unit cost of external funds (lender's risk: rates, maturity, covenants). The demand price is P_K discounted by borrower's risk. Both discounts steepen as expected external funding rises relative to what is conventionally regarded as a proper funding structure. Investment is set where the two schedules cross. Two things shift the whole system: a rise in expected quasi-rents, and a fall in the two risk premia. **Neither requires money creation.** This is the precise answer to "why assets and not CPI": the two price levels have disjoint determinants, and the transmission from finance to P_K runs through the capitalisation rate and the liquidity premium, not through the quantity of money. During ZIRP the operator is describing exactly a compression of l.

**2. Position-making — why flat flows are not reassuring.** Minsky's most underused concept, from "Central Banking and Money Market Changes" (*QJE* 71(2), 1957, 171–187) and *Stabilizing* ch. 10, is *position-making*: the set of transactions by which a unit obtains cash to carry its position in assets. He argued the fed funds market and dealer repo let institutions economise on money, so a **given** money stock supports a larger volume of positions — financial innovation raises velocity and severs the reserve-to-activity link. He also argued the character of the position-making instrument determines the character of the crisis: a crisis is a run on the position-making market, not on deposits. This matters directly for the operator's own findings. Fragility in Minsky is a property of the **stock** and its maturity profile, not of the growth rate of the flow. A flat FX-swap flow sitting on >$80tn of outstanding dollar obligations with ~$25tn off balance sheet is, on Minsky's terms, maximum rollover exposure with no growth — which is what a mature position-making structure looks like, not evidence against fragility. The same reading applies to flat collateral velocity: Minsky's mechanism does not require it to be rising.

**3. Godley: what the identity permits and forbids.** Godley & Lavoie, *Monetary Economics* (2007, ch. 2) impose quadruple-entry (Copeland 1947): every payment is an outflow for one sector and an inflow for another, and is recorded once in the current account and again in the balance sheet. Zezza & Zezza (Levy WP 919, 2019, §2.1) give the operational identities: NL = S − I + NKT; ΔNFA = NL + NKG; and, from the flow of funds, NL = ΔFinancialAssets − ΔFinancialLiabilities where Δ means *net issues*.

The consequences are sharp. (i) Net lending sums to zero across all sectors including the rest of the world. (ii) **NKG is not in the transactions matrix at all.** No sector borrowed to create it; it never passed through any income statement; it is not constrained to sum to zero across sectors and is not bounded by anything. (iii) Therefore aggregate net worth can rise by a multiple of aggregate saving with no credit growth and no monetary expansion — legally, in the accounts. (iv) But converting NKG into purchasing power requires a **transaction**, and a transaction requires a counterparty giving up an equal amount. There is no row in the transactions-flow matrix that turns a revaluation into money. Aggregate realisation is impossible by construction, not merely improbable. Gabaix–Koijen's ~5× multiplier is the empirical estimate of the price slope at which the impossibility is enforced.

**4. The US numbers (Z.1, 12 June 2026 release, through 2026:Q1).** Four quarters to 2026:Q1: household and nonprofit net worth rose $13.37tn, of which **transactions $2.04tn and revaluation $10.70tn** (other volume changes $0.63tn). **5. Where the funding actually comes from.** This is where the tradition disciplines the question rather than romanticising it. Four quarters to 2026:Q1, US sectoral balances (Z.1 S.1.2.i.q, $bn): households +598, nonfinancial noncorporate −74, nonfinancial corporate +216, financial sectors +430 → **private domestic surplus +$1,171bn (+3.7% of GDP)**; federal −2,090 and state/local −325 → **government deficit −$2,415bn (−7.7%)**; rest of world +1,030 (+3.3%); residual −214 = statistical discrepancy. The private sector is **not** net-borrowing to fund the AI trade; it is in surplus while the trade runs. Nonfinancial corporate gross saving ran $3,979bn SAAR in 2026:Q1 against $3,200bn of capital expenditure — an internal-funds surplus of **+$779bn, the largest in the post-war series**. This is the Kalecki closure the project has already established, and it is Tymoigne's final slide: ex ante the entrepreneur expects to need external funds; ex post P_I·OI = Π_if, because aggregate investment plus the government deficit *creates* the profits. Investment does not need prior saving; it needs *finance*, and Keynes (1937, *EJ*, "The 'Ex-Ante' Theory of the Rate of Interest") is explicit that finance is essentially a revolving fund that "employs no savings."

**6. Confirmed, refined, refuted.** Confirmed: the secular rise in Ponzi units. Davis, de Souza & Hernandez (Compustat 1970–2014) find the Ponzi share rising from 10.8% to 31.6%, concentrated in small firms and not sector-specific. **Refuted:** the same paper finds output gaps correlated with an almost-zero change in the probability a firm becomes Ponzi — Minsky cycles do *not* operate at business-cycle frequency in the post-1970 US. Refined: Nersisyan & Wray (Levy WP 1122, July 2026) map circular AI financing onto Galbraith's 1920s investment trusts and classify PIK-financed data centres as Ponzi by construction; the Levy Strategic Analysis (Papadimitriou, Yajima & Zezza, July 2026) runs the operator's exact scenario through an SFC model and gets the government deficit widening endogenously from 9.67% to 11.41% of GDP by 2028 with fiscal policy unchanged — the deficit as consequence, not cause.

**7. Honest measurement caveats.** For US nonfinancial corporate business the two published measures of the same net-lending concept — capital account vs financial account — disagree by $130–225bn *per quarter* (2025:Q1–2026:Q1). Anyone tracing AI funding through the flow of funds is working inside a discrepancy of order $0.5–0.9tn annualised. Zezza & Zezza flag precisely this. Separately, the 2026:Q1 nonfinancial-corporate "miscellaneous assets" acquisition of $2,691bn SAAR is a residual bucket that has historically swung from −$950bn to +$3,368bn; it is suggestive of circular financing but is not evidence on its own.

## Kalecki's profit equation and the distributional (Mian–Straub–Sufi) answer to the savings puzzle

**Core mechanism.** In a closed accounting system, gross business saving is not drawn from a pre-existing pool of household thrift; it is the residual of everybody else's spending decisions. Kalecki's identity — gross profits after tax = gross investment + government deficit + export surplus + capitalists' consumption − workers' saving — says that when capitalists (firms) and the state spend, the money returns to firms as profit, and the causality runs from the spending decision to the profit, not the reverse, because investment and fiscal decisions are made independently of current profit while wage-earners' spending is largely determined by their wages. The corollary is that a large government deficit at full employment mechanically manufactures corporate gross saving, and a capex boom partly funds itself. The distributional line adds that the aggregate household saving rate is the wrong denominator: saving is concentrated at the top of the wealth distribution (and increasingly inside corporations owned by that top), so a 2.7% aggregate personal saving rate is consistent with a very large flow of saving from the owners of capital. Both flows appear in no monetary aggregate.

**On investment without prior saving.** Directly: Kalecki's answer is that investment never requires prior saving, only prior *finance*, and the two are different things. Saving is a flow created by the act of investing; finance is a stock of liquidity that revolves. Kalecki held from his 1933 essay onward that investment is financed by bank credit created for the purpose, and the ex-post saving materialises as profit in the same period — Keynes made the same distinction explicit in his 1937 *Economic Journal* replies with the "finance motive": the demand for finance is a revolving fund, not a claim on accumulated thrift. This dissolves the operator's premise entirely. There is no requirement anywhere in the accounts that somebody first abstain from consumption. But — and this is the honest part — in the current US data this mechanism is *not* what is happening yet at the aggregate level. Business gross saving covered 106.7% of business gross investment in 2025 and 110.5% in 2026Q1; NFC internal funds covered 103.5% of NFC capex in 2025. The AI capex to date has been funded from internal cash flow, not from credit creation. What *is* new is 2026Q1: nonfinancial corporate debt securities and loans issuance at $1,249bn SAAR against $445bn in 2024, and net equity issuance turning positive at +$124bn after fifteen years of buybacks. If those persist, the Kalecki-Keynes finance mechanism becomes the operative one and the question passes to the credit tradition. As of the 2026Q1 accounts, the honest statement is: investment has not exceeded prior corporate saving; it has been *equal* to it, because the fiscal deficit manufactured the saving in the same period the investment happened. Half of business gross saving ($2,294bn of $4,423bn in 2025) has a government-net-borrowing counterpart.

**On invisibility to aggregates.** The Kalecki flow is invisible to M1–M3 for a structural reason, not a measurement failure: monetary aggregates count *stocks of deposits*, and the profit-generating circuit is a *flow* that leaves the deposit stock roughly unchanged. When the Treasury deficit-spends, the money lands in private deposits, is spent on goods, arrives as corporate revenue, and is spent again on data centres; the deposit stock turns over faster but does not grow, so M2 grows at nominal GDP pace (the project's ~+5.5% y/y) while $2.3tn/yr of purchasing power is being routed to the corporate sector. Kalecki's equation measures the flow; M2 measures the residue. The same is true of corporate retained earnings: ~$1,244bn SAAR of undistributed profits (W274RC), of which roughly $625bn is top-1% saving on DFA equity-ownership shares, is spent on capital goods without ever appearing as household saving or as monetary growth. Three concrete, auditable examples of invisible purchasing power in the current data: (i) the $2,294bn/yr government net borrowing (AD01RC), 7.5% of GDP, which no monetary or credit aggregate reports; (ii) the ~$168bn one-quarter federal capital transfer to business in 2026Q1 (W020RC spiking to $807.9bn SAAR) — the IEEPA tariff refunds ordered by the Supreme Court in February 2026, which BEA records as a capital transfer that does not touch GDP, does not touch profits, and does not touch M2, but is real cash to firms; (iii) ~$82bn/yr of share-based compensation at the Big 5, which is purchasing power delivered to employees in newly issued equity — it funds consumption and frees corporate cash for capex without any monetary creation at all. The general lesson for the operator: purchasing power invisible to M1–M3 is not exotic. It is the normal case for fiscal flows, for retained earnings, and for equity-denominated compensation, and the three together are of order $3tn/yr in the current US accounts.

**On the illusion of wealth.** Kalecki has nothing to say about valuation — his equation prices profit flows, not multiples — so on this question the tradition is honest only if it says so. What it does contribute is the *denominator*. The Fed's own accounts supply the numerator, and the answer is stark: in Z.1 Table S.1.M.r (June 11 2026 release), of the $39.16tn increase in US household and nonprofit net worth over 2023–2025, **$31.40tn (80.2%) was net holding gains** and only $7.17tn was net investment; 83.7% of those holding gains were equity-linked. Cumulatively since 1995Q4, personal saving plus corporate retained earnings totals $36.6tn against a $152.9tn rise in net worth — 24%. Fagereng, Holm, Moll & Natvik reach the same order of magnitude by an entirely different route on Norwegian administrative panel data: "saving by holding" accounts for up to 80% of the rise in Norway's wealth-to-income ratio from ~4 to ~7 over 1995–2015. So the operator's intuition is right and is measured, by the Fed, quarterly. The Kalecki-specific addition is the *mechanism by which the illusion would be revealed*. The valuation rests on a profit base that is itself half a fiscal counterpart. Profits are what validate the debt written against those valuations — Minsky's use of the Kalecki-Levy equation in *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986) is exactly this: profit flows are the cash that services the liability structure. So the collapse channel does not require anyone to try to sell. It requires only that one of the terms on the right-hand side falls: a fiscal consolidation, a capex pause, or a rise in household saving. Any of the three cuts profits one-for-one in the identity, which cuts the flow validating the collateral, which triggers the calls the operator describes — and the Gabaix–Koijen multiplier then does the rest on the stock. The reflexivity is the point: the funding source and the funded asset are the same accounting object. Note also that 2026Q1 has already printed net holding gains of −$859.9bn (corporate equities −$1,384.5bn) with total household net worth change of just +$113.1bn — the mechanism is not hypothetical.

**Explains what mainstream does not.** Four things. (1) It explains why the aggregate household saving rate can collapse to 2.7% (June 2026, PSAVERT — lowest since 2005) while corporate investment booms with no financing gap: household thrift is not the funding source and has not been since roughly 1980. The mainstream loanable-funds framing makes this look paradoxical; Kalecki makes it an identity. (2) It identifies a very large funding flow — government net borrowing of $2,294bn (7.5% of GDP, 2025) — that appears in no monetary aggregate, no credit aggregate, no collateral-velocity measure and no FX-swap stock, and that is arithmetically 52% of business gross saving. The project's established findings (M2 growing at nominal GDP pace, flat collateral velocity, ECB SFTDS against the liquidity-windfall hypothesis, FX-swap growth in hedging not leverage) are all consistent with a large funding flow existing, because none of those measures would see it. (3) It explains the sign asymmetry the operator intuits: because profits are the counterpart of spending decisions, a fiscal consolidation or a capex pause reduces profits one-for-one in the identity — the funding source and the thing being funded are the same object, which is what makes the structure reflexive rather than merely leveraged. (4) The distributional line explains why "the rich are saving a lot" and "the household saving rate is at a two-decade low" are simultaneously true, and — more usefully — why that saving is not what is funding the capex: MSS show it went into safe assets and other people's debt, not into capital formation.

**Ideology flagged.** Four things I would name as ideology, priors or unfalsifiable framing. (1) **The identity is not a theory.** P = I + (G−T) + NX + C_p − S_w is true by construction and cannot be refuted by data. Kaleckians routinely present the identity's arithmetic as though it demonstrated causation. It does not. The causal claim — that capitalists' spending decisions are exogenous to current profits — is a separate, contestable behavioural assumption, and the financing-constraint literature finds the opposite at the firm level. Anything I have written that assigns a *cause* to the fiscal term is a claim about behaviour, not about accounting. (2) **The class framing is prior, not finding.** Kalecki's "Political Aspects of Full Employment" (*Political Quarterly*, 1943) argues that business opposes full employment because it erodes discipline over labour — an interesting hypothesis, but it is asserted from a Marxian prior and functions in the literature as an unfalsifiable explanation for any observed policy outcome. It carries no weight in the present analysis and I have not used it. (3) **"Capitalists' consumption" is unmeasured.** In the modern accounts it is not separately observable, so the empirical Kalecki equation always folds it into a residual or into household saving — which means every applied version is doing something the original theory did not authorise. (4) **On the MSS side**: the "saving glut of the rich" rests on the PSZ/DINA distributional baseline, which Auten & Splinter and Smith–Zidar–Zwick contest with tax-data methods that produce materially smaller top shares; MSS adjust for some of the criticisms but the choice of baseline is not neutral. More seriously, Fagereng, Holm, Moll & Natvik show that whether the rich "save more" depends entirely on the treatment of capital gains — with net saving rates roughly flat across the wealth distribution in Norwegian administrative panel data. The framing "the rich supply the funds that the rest borrow" is intuitive, politically resonant, and may be substantially a valuation artefact. It should not be treated as settled. Finally, both literatures have a normative gravity toward redistribution as the policy answer; the project's no-recommendations rule handles that, but the reader should know the pull exists.

### Analysis

**1. The identity, derived and checked against the accounts.** Kalecki's derivation (Kalecki 1942, *Economic Journal* 52; *Theory of Economic Dynamics* 1954, Part II, "The Determinants of Profits", Ch. 3; reprinted as Ch. 7 of *Selected Essays*, CUP 1971) starts from national income measured two ways. Product side: Y = C_w + C_p + I + (G−T) + (X−M). Income side: Y = W + P. Substituting and cancelling gives P = I + (G−T) + (X−M) + C_p − S_w. Kaldor's 1955 gloss (*Review of Economic Studies* 23) is the memorable one: workers spend what they get; capitalists get what they spend.

I rebuilt this exactly in US NIPA form from BEA's quarterly flat file (NipaDataQ, 2026Q1 vintage) using Table 5.1 sector rows, and it holds to the dollar:

Business gross saving (W997RC) = Business gross investment (W987RC) + net capital transfers (W989RC) + business net lending (W995RC) − statistical discrepancy (A030RC); and business net lending = government net borrowing (−AD01RC) − household net lending (W996RC) − current-account deficit.

2025 (annual, SAAR $bn): 4,423 = 4,147 + 20 + (2,294 − 673 − 1,134) − 230. ✓

**2. The result is decisive for the operator's premise.** US business gross saving covered **106.7%** of US business gross investment in 2025 and **110.5%** in 2026Q1. On the Fed's Z.1 measure, nonfinancial-corporate internal funds ($3,184bn) covered **103.5%** of NFC total capital expenditures ($3,076bn) in 2025 — no financing gap at all. The domestic business sector's net lending reached **+1.6% of GDP** in 2025, the highest since 2011 outside the crisis-deleveraging years, having been **−1.0%** in 2022. Over the AI-capex window the corporate sector swung from net borrower to net lender by roughly $750bn/yr while capex rose. Corporate after-tax profits with IVA and CCAdj hit **11.0% of GDP** in 2025 — second only to 2021 in the annual series. So there is no aggregate funding puzzle. Nothing had to be sold and no net new money was needed.

**3. How much is fiscal?** Government net borrowing (NIPA, all levels) was **$2,294bn = 7.5% of GDP** in 2025 (federal cash-basis FY2025: $1,775bn, 5.8% of GDP). That is **51.9% of business gross saving** and **55.3% of business gross investment**. Decomposing the *change*: of the +$929bn rise in business gross saving 2022→2025, the widening of government net borrowing contributed **+$1,325bn** — more than the whole increase — offset by −$444bn from higher household net lending and −$132bn from a wider current-account deficit; the investment term itself contributed +$537bn. For 2019→2025 (+$1,401bn): investment +$1,189bn, deficit +$849bn, households +$182bn, external −$680bn.

Held-everything-else-constant counterfactual: with a balanced government budget, business gross saving would have been $2,129bn, covering **51%** of capex, and corporate America would have faced a ~$2.0tn/yr financing gap. That is not a general-equilibrium counterfactual and I flag it as arithmetic, not prediction.

**4. The distributional line.** Mian, Straub & Sufi (NBER 26941, revised July 2025) "unveil" Z.1 cross-holdings via a Leontief-style inverse and map DINA tax microfiles to primary assets. Their numbers: the top 1% saving rate rose from **42.9% (1963–82) to 53.6% (1983–2019)**, while the rest fell **13.8% → 7.1%**; saving rates by wealth are >40% (top 1%), ~20% (next 9%), ~12% (51st–90th), ~0 (bottom 50%). Post-1982 the top-1% saving flow rose **+2.9pp of national income** on average (>$680bn/yr in 2024 dollars), peaking at **+7.1pp** on a 10-yr MA over the 1982 base of 2.0%; bottom-99% saving fell **6.7pp**, a 60% decline. Crucially — §4.4, Fig. 11 — none of it shows up as investment: it financed middle-class borrowing to 2008 (+1.3% of NI/yr of bottom-99% debt-financed expenditure, 57.2% funded by the top 1%) and federal deficits thereafter, with top-1% safe-asset demand rising **42.1% of NI** (1980–2019) against the rest-of-world's 54.8%.

Scaled to 2026Q1 national income ($26,361bn), MSS's post-1982 *average* top-1% saving of ~4.9% of NI is ~**$1,292bn/yr** — versus total NIPA net private saving of $2,240bn and NIPA personal saving of only $916bn. **My extrapolation, not theirs**: their DINA microfiles stop at 2019.

**5. The unification — and the double-counting trap.** MSS's household saving is a DINA concept: it attributes corporate retained earnings to the shareholders. So "top-1% saving" and "corporate gross saving" are substantially *the same dollars*. Undistributed corporate profits with IVA/CCAdj were **$1,244bn SAAR** in 2026Q1; the Fed's DFA (WFRBST01122) puts the top 1% at **50.2%** of corporate equities and mutual-fund shares. Roughly **$625bn/yr** of top-1% saving is therefore accruing inside corporations and being spent directly on data centres, never passing through a deposit, a saving rate, or a monetary aggregate. Chen, Karabarbounis & Neiman (NBER 23133 / *JME* 2017) confirm the global version: corporate saving rose from below 10% to nearly 15% of global GDP since 1980, and "nearly two-thirds of global investment is funded by corporate saving" — the corporate sector went from net borrower to net lender worldwide. MSS themselves cite this as evidence the glut is global. **Do not add MSS's top-1% flow to corporate gross saving: they overlap.**

**6. What the null hypothesis does *not* dissolve.** (a) *Intra-sector reallocation.* The aggregate is a net lender; the specific builders are not. In the SEC XBRL data, latest fiscal year: Amazon capex $173.0bn vs OCF $161.4bn; Oracle capex $55.7bn vs OCF $32.0bn — both in deficit — while Alphabet (+$73bn), Microsoft (+$67bn) and Meta (+$46bn) are in surplus. Big-5 aggregate: capex $506bn, OCF $657bn, buybacks $94bn plus ~$40bn dividends — free cash after shareholder returns is roughly zero. (b) *The external-finance turn.* Z.1 2026Q1 shows nonfinancial corporate debt securities and loans at **$1,249bn SAAR** (2024: $445bn; 2025: $552bn) and net equity issuance **+$124bn**, the first positive reading after fifteen years of buybacks (2023: −$611bn). Kalecki says nothing about *who* lends; that is the credit tradition's ground. (c) *Valuation.* The profit equation prices no multiple.

**7. Steelmanning the counter.** The equation is an identity, so it has no causal content by itself; Kalecki's causal reading requires that investment and fiscal decisions are independent of contemporaneous profit. That is an assumption, and reverse causation (profits → investment) is exactly what the financing-constraint literature finds at the firm level. Second, Fagereng, Holm, Moll & Natvik (NBER 26588) show with Norwegian administrative panel data that *net* saving rates excluding capital gains are approximately constant across the wealth distribution — the rich look like high savers only because they "save by holding", which accounts for up to **80%** of the rise in Norway's wealth-to-income ratio from ~4 to ~7 (1995–2015). If that generalises, a large part of the "saving glut of the rich" is revaluation rather than a flow of loanable funds. MSS's July 2025 revision engages this directly (citing Iacono & Palagi 2023 and Fagereng et al. 2025) and argues that imputing asset-price inflation consistently with the national accounts restores their pattern; the dispute is live. Third, Auten & Splinter and Smith, Zidar & Zwick find smaller rises in top income and wealth shares than the PSZ/DINA baseline MSS build on, which would shrink the composition-effect driver.
