**Not disclosed.** THE COMPONENTS OF META'S HEADLINE NUMBER ARE NOT TABULATED. Meta names four components of the $46.03bn but never itemises them. Three are quantified elsewhere in the same note (equity carrying value $2.92bn; lease commitments ~$12.31bn; maximum RVG threshold ~$28bn). The fourth — "estimated future funding commitments" — is quantified NOWHERE in any of the three filings. Those three named components total $43.23bn, leaving a $2.80bn arithmetic residual. I flag explicitly that $2.80bn IS A RESIDUAL I COMPUTED, NOT A DISCLOSURE, and it must never be cited as one; it is also only as good as the "approximately" attaching to the $28bn and $12.31bn, either of which could absorb it entirely.

THE FINANCING BENEFIT — THE ENTIRE HUT 8 MECHANISM — IS QUANTIFIED BY NEITHER FILER. Meta does not disclose the Venture's own debt, its coupon, its rating, or whether the RVG and the lease are what made that debt investment grade. Alphabet does not disclose what its backstop does to the backstopped party's cost of capital. The ~100bp/halving-of-financing-cost effect is real in the Hut 8 case but it is nowhere in these filings. The created creditworthiness is visible only as the guarantor's contingent ceiling, never as the beneficiary's saved basis points. Both filers do report the fair value of the enhancement as effectively nil — Meta records no liability, Alphabet calls the financial guarantee fair value "not material" — which is precisely the accounting reason the value transferred is invisible.

ALPHABET DISCLOSES NO AGGREGATE MAXIMUM EXPOSURE TO LOSS AT ALL. Say this plainly: Alphabet's Note 5 has no Meta-style single number. For three of its four unconsolidated-VIE categories it only DEFINES the ceiling and points elsewhere — data-centre leases to Note 4, credit backstops to Note 3, energy backstops to Note 10. Only the funding-commitment category ($21.9bn) is quantified inside the VIE note. The numbers exist, but the reader must assemble them across three notes and the MD&A, and Alphabet never states an ASC 810 total. So the answer to "does Alphabet disclose a guarantee or VIE exposure of the Meta type?" is: YES in substance and YES in quantum, but NO in the form Meta uses, and no aggregate is offered.

ALPHABET'S BACKSTOP CEILING IS GROSS AND THE OFFSETS ARE UNQUANTIFIED. Alphabet says that on default it may receive equity or cash from counterparties, or may assume the leases for internal use or sublease them, and states these inflows are excluded from the notional. It never quantifies them. The $43.8bn is therefore a gross ceiling with an unmeasured recovery leg.

ALPHABET DOES NOT IDENTIFY OR STRATIFY ITS COUNTERPARTIES. No names, no credit quality, no per-counterparty notional, no concentration disclosure, and no split of the $43.8bn between VIE and non-VIE counterparties. The $20.0bn milestone-contingent funding commitment likewise names no counterparty; I did not infer one.

META GIVES NO OPERATING/FINANCE SPLIT of the $278.99bn not-yet-commenced, and no maturity schedule for it — only a 2026-2036 window and a one-to-30-year term range.

THE EL PASO VENTURE HAS NO MAXIMUM EXPOSURE FIGURE YET. Only the ~$13bn RVG ceiling and the ~$2.3bn contribution are given; there is no VIE consolidation assessment because the entity is not yet formed.

NEITHER FILER DISCLOSES THE OVERLAP BETWEEN ITS OWN NUMBERS. Meta's ~$12.31bn Venture lease commitment commences in 2029 and is therefore almost certainly inside the $278.99bn of not-yet-commenced leases as well as inside the $46.03bn maximum exposure — but Meta never says so. Anyone adding those two Meta figures may be double-counting ~$12.31bn. Likewise Alphabet's data-centre-VIE finance leases sit inside the Note 4 lease totals, and Alphabet says so but gives no amount.

NO PARTY QUANTIFIES THE POPULATION. Neither filer says how many VIEs, how many backstopped counterparties, or how many campuses. The count behind every one of these aggregates is undisclosed.

**Summable?** WHAT MAY LEGITIMATELY BE ADDED.

(1) ASC 842 leases not yet commenced, at 30 Jun 2026 — the cleanest sum available. Meta $278.99bn + Alphabet $85.2bn = $364.19 BILLION. Same standard, same disclosure concept (undiscounted future payments on executed leases not yet recorded), same as-of date, both taken from the Leases/Commitments notes of 10-Qs filed a week apart. Comparative at 31 Dec 2025: Meta $103.77bn + Alphabet $58.5bn = $162.27bn. So this basis grew 124% in six months across my two filers. Add-on items that must be listed SEPARATELY and not folded in: Meta's ~$68bn of July 2026 leases (post-balance-sheet), Alphabet's ~$5.8bn June 2026 short-term lease commencing Q3, and Alphabet's $9.9bn PPA-as-lease (January 2026, contingent).

(2) Meta's ASC 810 maximum exposure to loss, within Meta only. $46.03bn (Venture) + $6.41bn (other unconsolidated VIEs) = $52.44 BILLION at 30 Jun 2026. Both carry Meta's own label "maximum exposure to loss" and sit in consecutive sentences of Note 5. Caveat to state whenever this is used: the two are compositionally unlike — the Venture figure bundles equity carrying value, lease commitments, funding commitments and an RVG threshold, while the other is carrying value only. Same basis, different content.

(3) Alphabet's "maximum potential amount of future payments", within Alphabet only. $7.6bn financial guarantees + $43.8bn credit derivatives = $51.4 BILLION at 30 Jun 2026, against $22.64bn at 31 Dec 2025. Strictly these sit under different codification homes (ASC 460 and ASC 815), which would normally bar the sum — but ALPHABET ITSELF ADDS THEM IN ONE MD&A SENTENCE under one shared phrase. The filer's own presentation is the warrant. I would not have summed them otherwise.

WHAT MAY NOT BE ADDED.

(a) META'S $46.03bn AND ALPHABET'S $51.4bn MUST NOT BE SUMMED. They measure the same economic phenomenon through incompatible disclosures: one is an ASC 810 VIE maximum exposure to loss, the other a pair of ASC 460/815 guarantee ceilings. Report them side by side, never as ~$97bn.

(b) META'S ~$28bn RVG IS ALREADY INSIDE THE $46.03bn. Adding it is a straight double count. The same goes for the ~$12.31bn lease commitment and the $2.92bn carrying value.

(c) THE PURCHASE-OBLIGATION FIGURES ARE THE MOST DANGEROUS ON THIS PAGE. Meta's $349.31bn "non-cancelable contractual commitments" and Alphabet's $811.0bn "purchase commitments and other contractual obligations" are narrative disclosures under no common standard, with different scopes and different cancellation conventions. Alphabet's own filing carries TWO different figures for overlapping concepts — $811.0bn in MD&A versus $707.0bn in Note 10 — differing by $104bn purely on whether open purchase orders are included. A "Meta plus Alphabet purchase obligations" total is therefore either $1,160bn or $1,056bn depending on an undisclosed scoping choice. I do not endorse either as a sum.

(d) MAXIMUM EXPOSURE FIGURES MAY NOT BE ADDED TO LEASE OR PURCHASE FIGURES. Stock versus stock, but different regimes and heavily overlapping populations.

STOCK, NOT FLOW, AND CEILING, NOT EXPECTATION. Every figure above is a point-in-time stock and every "maximum exposure" is an upper bound under an adverse scenario the filers say is not probable. None is an expected loss, a drawdown, or an annual rate. The six-month growth rates I cite are differences between two stocks, not flows.

ON THE INDEPENDENCE TEST — this matters given the project's repeated failure mode. Meta and Alphabet do NOT share disclosure language here. Meta gives one bundled ASC 810 maximum-exposure number for a named joint venture with an RVG; Alphabet decomposes by instrument into credit derivatives, financial guarantees, finance leases and funding commitments, and offers no aggregate. Different structures, different standards, different words. These are TWO GENUINELY INDEPENDENT DISCLOSURES of the same mechanism, and that is the strongest thing in this report. The one place I DID find template repetition is inside Meta alone: the El Paso RVG sentence in Note 13 mirrors the Louisiana RVG sentence in Note 5 almost exactly. That is one filer reusing its own structure a second time — evidence the template is being scaled, but ONE fact, not two.

ON THE $662bn AND $970bn GHOSTS. Neither is reproducible from my two filers on any basis. My clean lease sum is $364.19bn; the two purchase-obligation totals are $1,056bn or $1,160bn. Nothing in Meta's or Alphabet's filings produces $662bn or $970bn, and I found no primary source for either.

VERDICT ON THE CHARGE. No retreat is warranted. The measurable cooperated. The mechanism is disclosed, it is quantified by both filers, and on the one basis where a cross-filer sum is legitimate it is $364.19bn at 30 Jun 2026. The single most telling datum is not a level but a trajectory that required no estimation at all: Alphabet's data-centre credit backstops went $0 at 31 Dec 2024, $16.94bn at 31 Dec 2025, $43.79bn at 30 Jun 2026, with a further $24.1bn of backstop capacity agreed and awaiting final terms.

**Counterparty note.** The receiving side is essentially absent from these two filings, and that asymmetry is itself the finding. Meta names no counterparty to the Louisiana Venture, gives no counterparty to the El Paso venture, and does not name the cloud provider behind the $14.72bn contingent capacity obligation or the counterparties to the $10.80bn of escrowed infrastructure purchase agreements. Alphabet names no backstopped data-centre counterparty, no energy-infrastructure counterparty, and does not name the private company behind the $20.0bn milestone-contingent funding commitment; I deliberately did not infer an identity for any of them. So the guarantor discloses a ceiling while the beneficiary is unidentified in the guarantor's own filing — meaning the obligation can only be matched to the receiving side by going to the beneficiary's filings, which for private JVs and private companies may not exist at all. Where the receiving side IS a public filer, the same obligation should appear as the credit support behind rated project debt: the Hut 8 case in the confirmed template is the demonstration — the lease guarantee is what the rating agency underwrites, and it shows up in the ISSUER's offering documents and rating rationale, not in the guarantor's footnote. Two structural asymmetries follow and both should be stated whenever this work is used. First, DIRECTIONAL: the guarantor discloses a maximum ceiling with no liability recorded and a fair value called not material, while the beneficiary books real debt at a real coupon; the guarantee is a liability nowhere and an asset nowhere, yet it is priced in the beneficiary's spread. Second, WITHIN ALPHABET, a partial internal counterparty match does exist and is worth noting: Alphabet states that on default under the backstops it retains the right to assume the underlying leases for internal use or to sublease to third parties, and may extinguish the obligation by a termination payment partially offset by equity or cash receipts from counterparties — so Alphabet has told us a recovery leg exists on the receiving side, and then declined to quantify any part of it. Note finally that Alphabet's Q2 2026 GFiber transaction (contributing a wholly owned subsidiary into a newly formed entity for $1.5bn cash and a $2.0bn instrument) appears in both the notes and MD&A but carries no guarantee or maximum-exposure disclosure, so I did not treat it as an instance of this mechanism.

**Not disclosed.** Six specific gaps, each of which I looked for and did not find.

1. ORACLE DISCLOSES NO VIE INFORMATION AT ALL. The string "variable interest entit" appears zero times in Oracle's FY2026 10-K. There is no maximum-exposure-to-loss number because there is no VIE note. Meta's $45.99bn has no Oracle analogue — not a smaller one, none.

2. ORACLE DISCLOSES NO RPO COUNTERPARTY CONCENTRATION. This is the sharpest gap in the set. RPO went from $137.8bn to $638bn in twelve months — an increase of $500bn — and Oracle attributes it only to "certain significant cloud contracts." No counterparty is named, no share is given, no top-customer percentage of RPO appears anywhere. The one concentration statement Oracle makes is on a different measure entirely: no customer at 10% or more of REVENUES. That is backward-looking and recognised; it tells you nothing about who owes the $638bn. The nearest thing to an admission is a risk factor: in certain OCI offerings Oracle is "more concentrated among a number of large customers" — qualitative, unquantified, and in Item 1A rather than the financial statements. Anyone circulating a number for Oracle's single-customer RPO share did not get it from the filings.

3. NO RESIDUAL VALUE GUARANTEE AT ANY OF THE THREE. Meta's RVG sentence has no counterpart at Microsoft, Amazon or Oracle. This one is genuinely absent, not merely unquantified.

4. MICROSOFT QUANTIFIES NO GUARANTEE EXTENDED TO ANYONE. Its Contingencies note is purely legal — the Irish DPC matter and $553m of accrued legal liabilities. No financial guarantee, no third-party debt guarantee, no VIE ceiling. Microsoft's only quantified forward obligation to an AI counterparty is the $13.0bn OpenAI funding commitment, which is an investment, not credit support.

5. NEITHER AMAZON NOR MICROSOFT QUANTIFIES ITS VIE CEILING. Both use the ASC 810 escape hatch. Amazon's "generally limited to" wording is doing real work: "generally" is unbounded and "future funding commitments" is open-ended. Amazon happens to disclose the components elsewhere, so a reader can build a number — but building it is the reader's inference, not the filer's disclosure, and it should not be presented as the latter.

6. THE BIGGEST NUMBERS ARE NOT MACHINE-READABLE. I checked the SEC XBRL companyfacts API for all three filers. Not one of them tags leases-not-yet-commenced. There is no LesseeOperatingLeaseLeaseNotYetCommenced fact for Microsoft, Amazon or Oracle; searching Microsoft's entire fact set for the values 329,100,000,000 and 92,700,000,000 returns zero hits, and Oracle's $260bn and $3.3bn are likewise untagged. These figures exist only as narrative prose inside a footnote. This is probably the mechanical explanation for how confected aggregates get into circulation: there is no structured series for anyone to pull, so intermediaries reconstruct one and the reconstruction acquires a provenance it never had. It also means the only way to get these numbers right is to read the footnotes, which is what this pass did.

ON THE DISCREDITED $662bn AND $970bn: I tried to reproduce them and could not. Microsoft's total contractual obligations are $743,821m (FY2026) and $397,045m (FY2025) — both arithmetically verified against the stated totals. Amazon's total commitments are $650,034m (30 Jun 2026), $569,280m (31 Mar 2026) and $439,661m (31 Dec 2025). The three-filer same-basis not-yet-commenced sum is $726.3bn. No figure at any of these three filers, on any basis or at any vintage I examined, equals $662bn or $970bn. Amazon's $650.0bn is the closest at about 2% below $662bn, which is close enough to tempt a match and not close enough to be one — I am recording the near-miss so nobody else mistakes it for the source. My conclusion is negative: these three filers are not where those numbers came from.

**Summable?** WHAT MAY BE SUMMED.

Leases signed but not yet commenced. One disclosure, one standard, all three filers, all excluded from the balance sheet by the same rule:
  Microsoft $329.1bn (2026-06-30) + Oracle $260bn (2026-05-31) + Amazon $137.2bn (2026-06-30) = $726.3bn.
Vintage caveat: Oracle's is one month earlier than the other two. That is a real but small mismatch and should be stated rather than smoothed over. Prior-period equivalents sum to roughly $232.5bn — Microsoft $92.7bn (2025-06-30), Amazon $96.4bn (2025-12-31), Oracle $43.4bn (2025-05-31) — but those three vintages span seven months, so treat the ~3x growth as an order-of-magnitude statement, not a precise multiple. This $726.3bn is the properly sourced replacement the project asked for. Every component was read in the filing and re-verified against raw HTML; the Microsoft figure additionally reconciles to the penny against its own MD&A table.

Unconditional purchase obligations, Oracle and Amazon only: $13.3bn + $130.1bn = $143.4bn. Both are unrecorded unconditional purchase obligations excluding cancellable open orders. Oracle's is XBRL-confirmed.

WHAT MAY NOT BE SUMMED.

Microsoft's $194.1bn "purchase commitments" must NOT be added to the $143.4bn above. Microsoft's line includes open purchase orders; Amazon's explicitly excludes them. Same-sounding label, different measurement, and the difference is large enough to swamp Oracle's entire figure. This is exactly the trap the project's rule exists to catch.

Microsoft's $34.6bn construction commitments belong to no shared basis — neither of the other two presents the category.

Oracle's $638bn RPO must not be added to anything here, for two independent reasons. It is ASC 606 rather than ASC 842 or ASC 460, and more fundamentally it points the other way: it is money owed TO Oracle, an asset-side expectation, not an obligation Oracle owes. Netting it against obligations, or stacking it on top of them, would be wrong in opposite directions.

Amazon's $650.0bn "Total commitments" is a cross-basis total that Amazon itself computes. It is fine to quote as Amazon's own figure but it cannot be added to Microsoft's $743.8bn or to anything from Oracle, and note that it already contains Amazon's $137.2bn of not-yet-commenced leases — so quoting both the $650.0bn and the $726.3bn in the same breath double-counts Amazon.

Oracle's $2.2bn (Feb) and $3.3bn (May) are the same guarantee at two dates. $5.5bn is not a number.

Oracle's $19bn subsequent-event purchase commitment is additive to its $13.3bn in substance but sits at a later, unstated date; combining them yields a total as of no particular day.

ON THE CONVERGENCE-IS-NOT-CONFIRMATION RULE. All three filers use near-identical construction for the not-yet-commenced sentence — a single narrative figure, "primarily for datacenters," a commencement window, a term range. That similarity is a disclosure convention under ASC 842, not three independent corroborations of a common underlying fact. It should be read as one accounting practice observed three times. Amazon is the partial exception worth crediting: it alone gives a maturity schedule, which is why Amazon's figure supports a duration claim and the other two do not.

WHAT THE MEASUREMENT ACTUALLY SETTLES. The external charge was that the project retreated to the unmeasurable. It did not need to. The measurable produced $726.3bn on a single clean basis, and it produced something better than an aggregate: Oracle's guarantee of up to $3.3 billion of a lessor's borrowing is the created-creditworthiness mechanism itself, disclosed, quantified, dated, and observably growing 50% in one quarter. It is a contingent guarantee that is not a financial instrument until drawn — the exact object the project went looking for — and it sits in a footnote with no XBRL tag. One instance is not a pattern, and $3.3bn is small against $260bn of leases behind it. But it is disclosed, and it is the receipt the argument needed before it may move on to securities-based lending, NAV loans, portfolio margin or internalised synthetic prime.

**Counterparty note.** I looked for the receiving side of each extended obligation and could not close any of them from public filings. Recording that as a finding rather than leaving it implied.

Oracle's guarantee of up to $3.3bn of the lessor's borrowing: the lessor is not named in the 10-K. Without a name I cannot search EDGAR for the corresponding liability, and I did not guess. If this lessor is an SEC registrant, the guarantee should appear on its side as credit support on a datacentre financing — that is a findable object for a later pass, but it needs the counterparty identified first, most plausibly from an Oracle 8-K or the lessor's own bond documentation rather than from the 10-K. This is the single highest-value open thread I am leaving.

Oracle's $638bn RPO: no counterparty is named, so there is no receiving side to look up. Whoever owes it does not appear in Oracle's filings.

Microsoft's $13.0bn OpenAI funding commitment and Amazon's $35.0bn OpenAI equity commitment: OpenAI is private and files nothing with the SEC, so neither has a locatable counterparty disclosure. Note that these two commitments are to the same entity from two different filers, and Amazon separately holds preferred stock in both Anthropic and OpenAI. The exposures are related through a common counterparty even though they are disclosed independently and under different standards — worth flagging because it is the kind of linkage that a per-filer reading structurally cannot see.

Structural point for the template. In the confirmed Hut 8 case the guarantee and its beneficiary are visible on both sides because the beneficiary issued rated public bonds. None of the three obligations above has that property. The mechanism becomes measurable only when the credit-receiving party happens to issue public debt; where the counterparty is private, the created creditworthiness is real, is doing collateral work, and leaves no trace on the receiving side at all. That asymmetry is a property of the disclosure regime, not of the exposure.

Hut 8's 10-Q (period 2026-06-30, accession 0001104659-26-090025) describes Beacon Point DC LLC's $4.25 billion (not $4.3bn) of 6.129% senior secured notes due 2042, rated Baa2 by Moody's, and says the data center is "leased in its entirety to a high-investment-grade company" — unnamed. The word NVIDIA appears ZERO times in Hut 8's 10-Q. In Hut 8's 2026-07-20 press release (EX-99.1, accession 0001104659-26-084862) NVIDIA is mentioned exactly twice, and only as an equipment standard: the campus is "designed to NVIDIA's DSX reference architecture". The tenant is again only "the high-investment-grade company that executed the Phase 1 lease". Moreover Hut 8 states the Beacon Point Notes are "not guaranteed by the parent company, the tenant, or any of their respective subsidiaries or affiliates" — so at Beacon Point there is NO guarantee at all; the lease covenant alone does the credit work. The project's belief that NVIDIA is the Hut 8 lessee/guarantor is NOT filing-supported and should be demoted to press attribution. This is precisely the "N sources citing one paper" failure mode.

CORRECTION B: The clean, NAMED instance of the mechanism at Hut 8 is a different deal and a different guarantor. Hut 8 DC LLC's River Bend project (St. Francisville, Louisiana, 245 MW) issued $3.25 billion of 6.192% senior secured notes due 2042; the tenant is Fluidstack USA IV Inc., and the lease "is supported by a financial backstop provided by Google LLC for rent payments and certain other financial obligations" (Hut 8 10-Q, Note 9). Google is named as the credit-enhancer; the notes are not guaranteed by Hut 8, the tenant, or the lease guarantor. Hut 8 states plainly in its 2026-07-20 release that 100% of its contracted AI datacentre capacity is "leased to or backstopped by investment-grade counterparties". The mechanism is real and the lessor quantifies it precisely ($19.6bn aggregate base-term contract value across the two Beacon Point leases, up to $50.2bn with all renewals; $26.6bn base-term across the whole Hut 8 portfolio). Alphabet's own quantification of the Fluidstack backstop was not examined in this lens and is the obvious next target.

**Summable?** WHAT MAY BE SUMMED.

(a) The commitments basis, within NVIDIA, at a single vintage. At 2026-04-26: manufacturing/supply/capacity $119bn + multi-year cloud services $30bn + other vendor $6bn + investment commitments $27bn = $182 billion. This sum is legitimate because NVIDIA's own FY2026 10-K grouped all four categories under a single note (Note 12, Commitments and Contingencies); the 10-Q merely relocated investment commitments to Note 6. The comparable FY2026 year-end figure at 2026-01-25 is $95.2 + $27 + $11.4 + $3.4 = $137.0 billion. Caveat: NVIDIA itself says some of these agreements are cancellable or reschedulable, so this is a gross contractual ceiling, not a firm obligation.

(b) Nothing else within NVIDIA sums cleanly. The $3.5bn facility lease guarantee is already stated as the aggregate "under all agreements" — there is nothing to add to it.

WHAT MAY NOT BE SUMMED, AND WHY.

(c) $105bn (residual value guaranties, 8-K, basis UNSTATED) + $3.5bn (facility lease guarantees, explicitly ASC 815 credit derivatives) ≠ $108.5bn as an accounting aggregate. Economically both are ceilings on NVIDIA's payment obligation under guarantees of third parties' lease obligations, and a reader may reasonably cite "$108.5 billion of disclosed gross lease-guarantee ceiling" — but ONLY with three caveats stated in the same breath: (i) the $105bn accounting basis is not yet characterised by the filer and may not be ASC 815; (ii) the $105bn is not yet effective — each guaranty becomes effective on commencement of its lease, with ready-for-service expected beginning 2028, so essentially none of it is live today; (iii) the $3.5bn is an inception ceiling whose current level is undisclosed and is stated to be lower. Given rule "sum within one basis or do not sum", the defensible presentation is to report $105bn and $3.5bn separately and let the reader see the arithmetic, not to publish $108.5bn as a single measured quantity.

(d) NVIDIA's $105bn may NOT be added to Meta's $45.99bn. Meta's figure is an ASC 810 maximum-exposure-to-loss disclosure for unconsolidated variable interest entities. NVIDIA's is a residual value guaranty of an operating lease with a named tenant. Different standards, different trigger conditions, different meanings of "maximum". A cross-filer "off-balance-sheet AI credit support" total built by adding these is exactly the unprovenanced aggregate the project has already been burned by.

(e) $32.4bn of not-yet-commenced leases (ASC 842, NVIDIA as lessee) may not be added to any guarantee figure. NVIDIA is the obligor in one and the contingent backstop in the other; adding them conflates a direct obligation with a contingent one. Nor may it be added to the $30bn of cloud-service commitments without a double-count warning, since both are compute-capacity procurement.

(f) $22.7bn and $32.4bn may NOT be added. Same ASC 842 disclosure at two vintages. Use $32.4bn (2026-04-26) and discard the older figure.

(g) $2.3bn equity-method maximum loss exposure may not be added to the guarantee figures — ASC 323, different standard, and it includes already-invested capital that is on balance sheet.

(h) $42.3bn of non-marketable equity securities is an on-balance-sheet asset, not an exposure disclosure, and may not be summed with anything here.

INDEPENDENCE WARNING. The $3.5bn appears three times across two filings (FY2026 10-K Note 10; FY2026 10-K Item 7 MD&A restated as "land, power, and shell guarantees"; Q1 FY2027 10-Q Note 8) in near-identical language. That is one disclosure carried forward, not three confirmations. Likewise, the FY2026 10-K and Q1 FY2027 10-Q wording of the facility lease guarantee differs only in "Other income and expense" versus "Other income (expense), net" — a boilerplate carry-forward, i.e. a disclosure convention, not evidence of re-measurement.

STOCK NOT FLOW. Every figure above is a stock and, where labelled "maximum" or "capped", a ceiling. The $105bn is the maximum NVIDIA could ever pay cumulatively over up to 20 years per lease if OpenAI defaults AND recoveries from reletting or sale are zero — and NVIDIA further discloses that OpenAI has agreed to reimburse and indemnify it for any amounts actually paid. None of these are expectations, and the expected value of the $105bn is nowhere disclosed.

**Counterparty note.** THREE counterparty legs, two of which I verified against primary filings.

LEG 1 — Portsmouth / SB Energy / OpenAI (the $105bn). Verified only from NVIDIA's side. The 8-K (accession 0001045810-26-000069) names SB Energy Corp. as Lessor and an affiliate of OpenAI Group PBC as tenant. SB Energy is not an SEC reporting company I located, and OpenAI Group PBC does not file periodic reports, so there is NO receiving-side filing to check. The structure NVIDIA describes: on OpenAI insolvency-default or payment failure, NVIDIA pays the shortfall between the lease's guaranteed minimum value and amounts recovered via replacement lease or sale; NVIDIA may then assume the lease, force a relet, initiate a sale, allow termination, or defer remedies for up to a year. Termination triggers include the 20th anniversary of lease commencement and — the thesis in the filer's own words — "OpenAI achieving a satisfactory credit rating" (NVIDIA 8-K, Item 1.01). OpenAI has agreed to reimburse and indemnify NVIDIA for amounts actually paid, an unquantified offsetting receivable from an unrated private counterparty.

LEG 2 — Hut 8 / Beacon Point. VERIFIED, and it CONTRADICTS the project template. Hut 8 Corp. 10-Q, filed 2026-08-04, period 2026-06-30, accession 0001104659-26-090025, Note 9 'Loans, notes payable, and other financial liabilities': Beacon Point DC LLC issued $4.25 billion of 6.129% senior secured notes due 2042-11-30 at par, rated Baa2 by Moody's, non-recourse to Hut 8, financing 352 MW critical IT capacity in Nueces County, Texas. The site is "leased in its entirety to a high-investment-grade company under a triple-net lease" — TENANT UNNAMED. NVIDIA appears zero times in the 10-Q, and only as "NVIDIA's DSX reference architecture" (an equipment standard) in the 2026-07-20 EX-99.1. Critically, Hut 8 states the notes are "not guaranteed by the parent company, the tenant, or any of their respective subsidiaries or affiliates" — there is no guarantee at Beacon Point; the lease covenant alone earns the investment-grade rating. Lessor-side quantification is precise: Phase 2 base contract value ~$9.8bn over 15 years with 3% escalators, campus total ~$19.6bn base-term, ~$50.2bn if all three five-year renewal options per lease are exercised, 704 MW critical IT (~1 GW utility). The template's attribution of this deal to NVIDIA is not supported by any filing and should be re-sourced or dropped.

LEG 3 — Hut 8 / River Bend / Fluidstack / Google. VERIFIED, and this is the clean named instance of the mechanism. Same Hut 8 10-Q, Note 9: Hut 8 DC LLC issued $3.25 billion of 6.192% senior secured notes due 2042-11-15 at par, financing 245 MW at St. Francisville, Louisiana; tenant is Fluidstack USA IV Inc.; the lease "is supported by a financial backstop provided by Google LLC for rent payments and certain other financial obligations". Hut 8 refers to Google as "the lease guarantor". Hut 8 does NOT quantify the Google backstop. Alphabet Inc.'s own filings were not examined in this lens and are the obvious next target: the question is whether Alphabet quantifies the Fluidstack backstop anywhere, and under which heading. Hut 8's own summary of the mechanism, from its 2026-07-20 release, is the plainest statement of the thesis I found in any filing: 100% of its contracted AI data center capacity is leased to or backstopped by investment-grade counterparties.

NET ASYMMETRY. The lessors quantify the obligation to the dollar and the year, because rent is their revenue. The guarantors quantify it late, coarsely, or not at all — NVIDIA's $105bn exists today only in an 8-K narrative with no stated accounting basis, and Google's backstop is unquantified by the party that discloses it. The disclosed thing is now measurable on the guarantor side for exactly one filer, for exactly five days, and only because an Item 1.01/2.03 trigger forced it.

**Not disclosed.** NINE gaps, each of which I searched for specifically and did not find.

1. NO RATING RATIONALE EXISTS IN ANY FILING, ANYWHERE. The task asked what the rating agency said the lease did to the credit. Rating agency reports are not SEC filings and none is furnished as an exhibit. Hut 8 discloses six words — "rated Baa2 by Moody's Ratings" — in MD&A and nothing more. There is no discussion of notching, no lease-quality analysis, no comparison to the tenant's own rating. The mechanism's central evidentiary claim (that the lease is WHY the bonds are investment grade) is nowhere asserted by an agency in a document I can cite.

2. WORSE: HUT 8 DISCLOSES NO RATING AT ALL FOR THE RIVER BEND NOTES. The 27 April 2026 press release is headlined "Hut 8 Announces Pricing of $3.25 Billion of Investment-Grade Senior Secured Notes for River Bend Data Center Project" and the word "rating" does not appear in its body. Neither the release nor the 10-Q names an agency or a rating for those notes. A $3.25 billion issue is publicly characterised as investment grade in a furnished exhibit with no rating disclosed to support it. That is the single most quotable disclosure failure in this lens.

3. NO ISSUER DISCLOSES A SPREAD TO ANY BENCHMARK. Not one of Hut 8, Applied Digital, Cipher, TeraWulf or Galaxy states a spread to Treasuries, to swaps, or to any index. Every deal is disclosed as a coupon and a price. THIS IS WHY THE "HALVES THE FINANCING COST" CLAIM CANNOT BE VERIFIED FROM FILINGS: the ratio depends entirely on a risk-free base rate that appears in no filing. On coupon, which IS disclosed, the reduction is never a halving — Applied Digital 9.250% to 6.750% is 27%, Cipher 7.125% to 6.125% is 14%, and the widest pair I found (Galaxy 9.875% versus Hut 8 6.129%) is 38%. A halving would require the base rate to be roughly half the safe-tenant coupon, which is arithmetically possible in early-2026 conditions but is an assumption, not a finding.

4. NO BOND OFFERING DOCUMENT IS ON EDGAR. Every one of these deals is Rule 144A / Regulation S. There is no prospectus, no offering memorandum, no ratings letter, no pricing supplement. The indentures are filed as 8-K exhibits (Hut 8's are EX-4.1 to the 8-Ks of 1 May and 10 June 2026), but an indenture does not contain pricing, tenant identity or ratings. Everything I have on pricing comes from press releases and investor presentations FURNISHED under Item 7.01 or 8.01 — which are expressly not deemed "filed" and are not audited.

5. NO TENANT IS NAMED WHERE THE TENANT IS INVESTMENT GRADE. This is a perfect inverse correlation across the whole dataset and it is worth stating as a pattern. Where the tenant is CoreWeave (non-IG) it is named: Applied Digital names it, Galaxy names it in its presentation. Where the tenant is investment grade it is never named: Hut 8 says "a high-investment-grade company (i.e., rated AA- or higher)", Cipher says "an investment grade hyperscaler tenant", Applied Digital says "a U.S.-based investment-grade hyperscaler" and, for Delta Forge 1 and Polaris Forge 3, "a second U.S.-based investment-grade hyperscaler". Four filers, four unnamed IG tenants. The credit that is doing the work is systematically anonymous.

6. TWO PROJECT BONDS TOTALLING $2.40 BILLION HAVE NO DISCLOSED TENANT AT ALL — Cipher's Stingray 2031 ($810m at 6.000%) and Applied Digital's ComputeCo 3 2031 ($1.59bn at 7.000%). Their coupons sit squarely in the IG-tenant band, which is suggestive, but suggestion is not disclosure and I have excluded both from every total.

7. THE SIZE OF THE DATA-CENTRE ABS/CMBS MARKET IS NOT ESTABLISHABLE FROM EDGAR AND I WILL NOT ESTIMATE IT. ABS-15G is a due-diligence findings form: it discloses the accountant, the date, the procedures and sometimes a lease count. It discloses no deal size, no coupon, no tranche rating and no tenant name. I found no registered (Regulation AB) data-centre ABS prospectus — no 424B5, no ABS-EE — in the period searched; the channel is entirely 144A. Anyone circulating a dollar figure for outstanding data-centre ABS did not obtain it from EDGAR.

8. HUT 8 DISCLOSES NO CONSIDERATION PAID TO GOOGLE. TeraWulf and Cipher each disclose exactly what the backstop cost them ($515.5m and $544.5m of warrant fair value). Hut 8's River Bend lease carries an identical-function Google backstop and Hut 8 discloses no warrants, no fee, and no deferred charge for it. Either the consideration sits inside the lease economics, or it was given for nothing, or it is undisclosed. The filings do not permit a choice between these, and the difference matters: it is the difference between a priced credit enhancement and a free one.

9. THE OFFSETS ARE UNQUANTIFIED ON BOTH SIDES. Google's backstop at TeraWulf gives Google the OPTION either to pay the termination fee or to assume the lease as tenant; Hut 8's is described only as covering "rent payments and certain other financial obligations". Neither the recovery leg nor the termination-fee quantum is disclosed by any beneficiary. And no beneficiary discloses what it would have paid without the covenant — the counterfactual is constructed by me from sibling deals, not stated by anyone.

**Summable?** WHAT MAY LEGITIMATELY BE ADDED.

(1) THE PRICE OF THE GUARANTEE, RECEIVING SIDE. $515.5 million (TeraWulf) + $544.5 million (Cipher) = $1,060.0 MILLION. Identical measurement basis: the initial fair value of penny warrants issued to Google as consideration for a lease backstop, capitalised as a deferred charge and amortised over the lease. Both at 30 Jun 2026, both disclosed as the same concept in the same words ("in consideration of Google providing the Google Backstop" / "the value of Google agreeing to make the lease payments from Fluidstack"). This is the summable number this lens exists to produce, and it is the only place in the entire project where the created creditworthiness has an arm's-length PRICE rather than a ceiling. Set it against Alphabet's own statement that its financial-guarantee fair value is not material and Meta's "no liability has been recorded to date": the guarantors record approximately nothing; the beneficiaries recorded $1.06 billion of equity given away.

(2) THIRD-PARTY DEBT PRICED OFF AN INVESTMENT-GRADE TECH COVENANT. $16,583 MILLION at 22 Aug 2026, one instrument class (144A senior secured project notes whose sole or principal source of debt service is a data-centre lease), four filers:
  Google-backstopped Fluidstack leases — Hut 8 River Bend $3,250m (6.192%, Apr 2026) + TeraWulf 2030 Secured Notes $3,200m (7.75%, Oct 2025) + Cipher Compute 2030 $1,733m incl. the $333m tap (7.125%, Nov 2025) = $8,183m.
  Direct investment-grade tenant leases — Hut 8 Beacon Point $4,250m (6.129%, Jun 2026, tenant rated AA- or higher) + Cipher Black Pearl $2,000m (6.125%, Feb 2026) + Applied Digital ComputeCo 2 $2,150m (6.750% at 98, Mar 2026) = $8,400m.
  Vintage caveat: these are ISSUANCE amounts spanning Oct 2025 to Jun 2026, not a single as-of date, and none has begun amortising. Excluded and listed separately: $2,400m of tenant-undisclosed deals (Cipher Stingray $810m, APLD ComputeCo 3 $1,590m). Also excluded: $5,857m of CoreWeave-tenant comparators (APLD ComputeCo 2030 $2,350m, Galaxy Helios II $3,507m) — those are the control group, not the population.

(3) THE COVENANT IN BASIS POINTS. Measured within one instrument class, the range is 100 to 390bp of coupon. Four observations, best-controlled first:
  (a) SAME SPONSOR, TENANT SWAPPED — Applied Digital: 9.250% at 97.000 with CoreWeave (13 Nov 2025) versus 6.750% at 98.000 with an IG hyperscaler (3 Mar 2026). Coupon delta 250bp; the 1-point issue-price difference adds roughly 19bp over a ~5.2-year life (my arithmetic), so approximately 270bp on yield. Contaminated by 3.7 months of market movement.
  (b) SAME SPONSOR, TENANT SWAPPED — Cipher: 7.125% Google-backstopped Fluidstack (13/24 Nov 2025) versus 6.125% direct IG hyperscaler (11 Feb 2026), both at par. Coupon delta 100bp. This is the LOW end and it is the honest low end: a Google backstop gets you most of the way to an IG tenant.
  (c) DATE-MATCHED, SPONSOR SWAPPED — November 2025: Cipher 7.125% (Google backstop) versus Applied Digital 9.250% at 97 (CoreWeave). Coupon delta 212.5bp, roughly 275bp on yield. Same month, same market, same instrument class; different sponsors and sites.
  (d) NEAR-DATE-MATCHED, WIDEST — June/July 2026: Hut 8 Beacon Point 6.129% (AA--or-better tenant, 16.5-year fully amortising) and Cipher Stingray 6.000% versus Galaxy Helios II 9.875% (CoreWeave, 5-year, only 4%/yr amortisation). Delta 375-387bp, and the cheap end has three times the tenor.
  My working figure is 250bp, the same-sponsor Applied Digital observation, because it holds sponsor, jurisdiction, asset type and instrument class constant and varies only the tenant. Applied to the $16,583m population that is approximately $415 million of interest avoided per year (my arithmetic). For Hut 8's two deals alone the disclosed illustrative interest schedules imply $739 million of lifetime interest per 100bp, so 250bp is roughly $1.85 billion over the life of $7.5 billion of notes — about a quarter of principal.

(4) THE PROJECT BOND PRICES OFF THE TENANT, NOT THE SPONSOR OR THE ASSET. This is the strongest structural claim the data supports and it needs no estimation. Galaxy's CoreWeave-tenant project bond priced at 9.875% in July 2026 while CoreWeave's own unsecured 2032 notes had priced at 9.625% a month earlier — the first-priority lien on a purpose-built data centre bought essentially nothing. Meanwhile Applied Digital, one sponsor with one balance sheet, priced 250bp apart on two structurally identical deals. The sponsor is irrelevant, the collateral is nearly irrelevant, and the tenant covenant is the credit.

WHAT MAY NOT BE ADDED.

(a) THE $1.06 BILLION OF WARRANT VALUE MAY NOT BE ADDED TO ANY GUARANTOR-SIDE FIGURE. It is the price of the enhancement, not the exposure. Adding it to Alphabet's $43.785 billion of credit-derivative notional or its $7.6 billion of financial guarantees mixes a paid consideration with a maximum-payment ceiling.

(b) NO RECONCILIATION IS POSSIBLE BETWEEN THIS LENS AND ALPHABET'S NOTIONAL. Alphabet discloses $43.785 billion of credit-derivative gross notional at 30 Jun 2026, described as backstops of third-party data-centre payment obligations. On the receiving side I can identify exactly one quantified Google-backstopped lease value — Hut 8's River Bend at $7.0 billion base-term ($17.7 billion with renewals). TeraWulf and Cipher disclose the existence of their Google-backstopped leases but not their contract values in the notes I read. So a $7.0 billion beneficiary-side figure sits against a $43.8 billion guarantor-side ceiling and the remaining $36.8 billion is unattributed. I did not attempt to close it and nobody should.

(c) CONTRACT VALUES MAY NOT BE ADDED TO DEBT. Hut 8's $26.6 billion of base-term contract value, Galaxy's $10.4 billion of minimum contracted lease payments and the $16.583 billion of project notes are three different things: undiscounted future rent, undiscounted future rent, and principal outstanding. Rent is the source of debt service, so the two overlap almost completely.

(d) COUPONS FROM DIFFERENT DATES ARE NOT A SPREAD. Every pair above except (c) and (d) is separated by weeks or months, and the whole market tightened materially between Oct 2025 and Jun 2026 — visible in the fact that Applied Digital's own CoreWeave-tenant deal at 9.250% (Nov 2025) is inside Galaxy's CoreWeave-tenant deal at 9.875% (Jul 2026), which cuts the other way and warns against reading a clean time trend either. Treat the 100-390bp range as a range, not a series.

(e) THE MEASUREMENT IS COUPON, NOT SPREAD, AND THE TWO ANSWER DIFFERENT QUESTIONS. Nothing in EDGAR converts one into the other.

ON THE INDEPENDENCE TEST. There are genuinely FOUR independent beneficiary disclosures here, not one convention observed four times, and the evidence for that is that the four filers disagree structurally. TeraWulf and Cipher both capitalise the Google warrants as an asset — that IS a shared convention and should be read as two applications of one accounting answer to one deal template, though the warrant counts (73,580,000 versus 24,178,576) and the amounts ($515.5m versus $544.5m) are independently determined, and Cipher alone carries a $430.0 million value floor. Hut 8 discloses no consideration at all, which is a genuinely different fact rather than different wording. Applied Digital and Galaxy have no backstop and describe their tenants in opposite ways. And the pricing observations are hard market outcomes at four different sponsors, which is the strongest form of independence available. The one place I DID find pure convention: all five issuers use near-identical 144A/Reg S language and near-identical "senior secured obligations... first-priority liens... non-recourse" formulations. That is one law-firm template observed five times and proves nothing about the economics.

ON THE $662bn AND $970bn GHOSTS. Nothing in this lens reproduces either figure on any basis. My largest constructed number is $16.583 billion of project notes; my largest single disclosed number is Hut 8's $50.2 billion of potential campus contract value if all renewals are exercised, which is a contingent maximum on one campus. Neither ghost is anywhere near.

STOCK, NOT FLOW; PRICE, NOT CEILING. One correction to the project's own framing is warranted here. The guarantor-side figures the earlier lenses produced are ceilings under adverse scenarios the filers call improbable. The $1.06 billion in this lens is NOT of that kind: it is consideration actually transferred, at a fair value actually recorded, for a guarantee actually given. It is the one number in the whole exercise that is a price rather than a bound, which is exactly why it is the right answer to the charge.

**Counterparty note.** The guarantor side of these same obligations sits in Alphabet's filings, and the two sides do not reconcile — which is itself the finding. Google LLC is the named backstop provider in Hut 8's River Bend lease (Hut 8 10-Q Note 9), TeraWulf's Fluidstack HPC Leases (TeraWulf 10-Q, Google Recognition Agreements) and Cipher's Barber Lake Facility lease (Cipher 10-Q, Google Warrant Agreement). Hut 8's 8-K of 2025-12-17 discloses the guarantor's own credit: "Google, a subsidiary of Alphabet Inc. (S&P: AA+, Moody's: Aa2)". The prior Alphabet lens established the guarantor-side quantum at $43,785 million of credit-derivative gross notional at 30 Jun 2026, described by Alphabet as backstops of third-party data-centre payment obligations and as the maximum potential amount of future payments, plus $7.6 billion of ASC 460 financial guarantees.

Three things follow. FIRST, Alphabet's $43.785 billion is not decomposable to these three beneficiaries and they do not decompose to it: the only quantified Google-backstopped lease value I could read on the receiving side is Hut 8's River Bend at $7.0 billion over the 15-year base term (up to $17.7 billion with three 5-year renewals), and TeraWulf and Cipher disclose the backstop's existence without disclosing their leases' contract values in the notes I read. Roughly $36.8 billion of Alphabet's notional has no beneficiary-side counterpart I can point to. Neither side discloses a counterparty breakdown, so this gap is structural, not a search failure.

SECOND, the valuation asymmetry is stark and is disclosed on both sides. Alphabet calls its financial-guarantee fair value not material; Meta records no liability for its residual value guarantee because payments are "not probable". On the receiving side the identical enhancement is worth $515.5 million to TeraWulf and $544.5 million to Cipher — $1.060 billion of equity handed to Google, recorded as an asset and amortised. The guarantors book approximately nothing for something the beneficiaries paid a billion dollars for. That is not an accounting error on either side; it is the exact accounting reason the transfer is invisible in every aggregate the project has been looking at.

THIRD, TeraWulf's disclosure shows the guarantor taking a direct, non-contingent role that the guarantor's own filings would not reveal: in connection with the $3.2 billion 2030 Secured Notes, Google pledged its warrants to Wilmington Trust as collateral agent for the lenders' benefit, and "an affiliate of Google unconditionally agreed with the collateral agent for the benefit of the lenders that it shall (or shall cause Google to) pay the Termination Fee". An unconditional undertaking to a bond trustee is a materially stronger commitment than a contingent recognition agreement, it is disclosed only by the beneficiary, and whether it is inside Alphabet's $43.785 billion notional is not determinable from either filing.

One further counterparty note on Meta: no Meta-backstopped third-party issuer appears anywhere in this lens. The systematic full-text sweep for backstop language turned up Google in every instance and Meta in none. Meta's mechanism runs through residual value guarantees inside its own joint ventures, which produces no third-party bond issuer on EDGAR to observe from the receiving side. So the receiving-side test is currently a test of the Google structure only, and the absence of a Meta analogue is a real limit on how far this lens generalises.
