## Yen Repatriation Starts with the Whale — Japan Macro Advisors (individual author not named in text), September 10, 2026

- **kind:** research note | 9 pages

- **thesis:** GPIF (Japan's Government Pension Investment Fund) cut its target domestic-bond weight from 60% to 35% in 2014 and to 25% in 2020 because it judged JGB yields abnormally low and expected a future "crash"; the author's replication of GPIF's own optimizer shows that with the 10-year JGB now at 3%, the real-return math that justified 25% no longer holds and points to roughly 50%. The author expects GPIF to raise its domestic-bond weight — possibly already underway in the September 2026 quarter — with foreign bonds (>50% of which are USD) the likely asset class funding the shift, which he frames as a "big turn" in Japan's cross-border capital flows.

  Internal terminology note: the note's own title calls this "repatriation," but its conclusion explicitly says the move "should be characterized as rebalancing, rather than a repatriation" — the two terms are used inconsistently within the same document, which matters for a taxonomy that treats channels/flow-types precisely.

- **channels:**
  - (d) market structure and flows — the whole piece is about a large institutional investor rebalancing between yen and non-yen (chiefly USD) bonds, i.e., a cross-border portfolio flow, not a fundamentals or credit story.
  - (e) fundamentals incl. rates and term premia — the rebalancing case rests entirely on realized/expected real returns on JGBs (yield up from ~0.5% to 3%) versus wage growth, per GPIF's own allocation rule.
  - (f) other — political-economy constraints ("Washington/Tokyo problem": Scott Bessent's likely reaction, the Takaichi government's deficit-funding needs) shape the timing and public communication of the move, independent of the economics.
  - (a) direct money creation — background only: BoJ's 2013 QE launch and the resulting near-zero/negative JGB yields are the historical trigger for GPIF's original 2014 cut; no claim about current BoJ operations.
  - P (pension/insurer flows, see note above) — the entire note is a pension fund's (GPIF's) target-weight mechanics, quantified in yen, plus three named sister public pension funds run on the same model.
  - Not applicable / not discussed: (b) shadow banking, (c) collateral/leverage/basis trades.

- **US Treasury market:** No discussion of auctions, bid-to-cover, indirect bidders, or the Fed's balance sheet/reinvestment policy — none of these appear. The relevant claim is narrower: more than 50% of GPIF's foreign-bond book is USD-denominated, so a cut to GPIF's foreign-bond weight (to fund a higher JGB weight) would reduce GPIF's purchases of, among other USD assets, US Treasuries. The note states outright that "Scott Bessent will not like a public announcement that one of the largest pension …" — i.e., this is framed as a foreign-official-adjacent, Japan-public-pension pullback from USTs, not from JGBs. No mention of banks, money funds, hedge funds/basis trade, or passive index funds as US Treasury buyers. "Term premium" as a term is not used anywhere in this note; the closest analogue is GPIF's real-return-vs-wage-growth optimizer, a distinct framework.

## The Grant Williams Podcast, Episode 129 — Grant Williams (host) & Marvin Barth (guest, Thematic Markets), published August 13, 2026

- **kind:** podcast transcript | 17 pages

- **thesis:** Marvin Barth argues that bond-market term premium is driven mainly by central-bank credibility, not by the policy rate itself: a Fed seen as hawkish and rule-bound lowers long-term borrowing costs (his read of the June 2026 FOMC), while a Fed seen as capitulating raises them (his read of the July 2026 FOMC), because investors demand more compensation to lend long to a government they don't trust to control inflation. He judges the US primary fiscal deficit (about 3.5% of GDP) unsustainable in the long run, but expects the US to be the last major economy to face a funding crisis because it retains a safe-haven "foreign bid," whereas Japan — a large net saver able to fund itself — pulling back capital would pressure France and Spain before it seriously threatens the US.

- **channels:**
  - (a) direct money creation incl. Fed and Treasury operations — central topic: Fed policy credibility, QE criticized in hindsight ("we should ban any central bank for ever being able to do QE again" — Barth), Kevin Warsh's Fed reform task forces, and an explicit claim that Treasury issuance has been "skewing more and more...to the front end."
  - (d) market structure and flows incl. foreign flows — Barth's "foreign bid, this foreign marginal buyer" argument for the US, and his claim that a pullback in Japanese capital exports would hit French/Spanish bonds before US Treasuries.
  - (e) fundamentals incl. rates and term premia — extensive: definition and drivers of term premium, the primary-deficit/growth/real-rate arithmetic of debt sustainability, and Barth's preferred term-premium proxy (far-forward less medium-forward rates).
  - (f) other — Fed governance reform (Warsh's task forces), critique of the "Neo-Keynesian synthesis" and central-bank groupthink; relevant to the research project only via the credibility-to-term-premium channel above, not directly to flows.
  - Not applicable / not discussed: (b) shadow banking, (c) collateral/leverage/basis trades, P (pension/insurer flows — no pension fund or insurer, in Japan, Europe, or the US, is named anywhere in this transcript).

- **US Treasury market:** No mention of auctions, bid-to-cover ratios, indirect/direct bidder shares, banks, money-market funds, hedge funds/basis trade, or passive bond-index funds as UST buyers. Barth (line ~509) states the Treasury "borrows across the curve" but "they've been skewing more and more of it to the front end" — i.e., a claim that issuance itself is tilting toward bills even though, in his view, a fiscally stressed borrower would ideally "borrow longer" to lock in financing and cut rollover risk. On term premium, Barth defines it as "the additional incremental interest you charge for longer term bonds," argues it should rise when central-bank credibility is in doubt, and says the back end of the US curve has risen relative to the rest of the curve over the "last two to three years," with the rise steepening since early 2025. He attributes the US's relative insulation to a persistent "foreign bid" / "foreign marginal buyer" for US assets specifically because the US remains safer than the alternatives — while also conceding "the US will eventually pay some sort of price" if a Japan-triggered European shock reduces capital available to fund the US.

- **pension funds and insurers:** Not discussed. No mention of GPIF, Japanese life insurers, European pension reform (Dutch or UK LDI), or US pension/insurance allocation anywhere in this transcript. Japan is discussed only in aggregate macro terms — net saver status, current-account surplus, foreign-asset stock — never through a named pension or insurance institution.

  - Term premium rises back toward historical (at least 1990s) norms | ongoing, already "starting to happen," no end date given | conditioned on continued fiscal deterioration and perceived central-bank non-credibility, "not just the US"
