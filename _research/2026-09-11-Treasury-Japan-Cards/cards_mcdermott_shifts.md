## The Grant Williams Podcast, Episode 131 — Grant Williams & Andrew McDermott, Published August 21, 2026

*Source file: The_Grant_Williams_Podcast_Transcript_0131_Andrew_McDermott.txt*

- kind: podcast transcript | pages: 20
- thesis: McDermott argues the August 2026 yen intervention (Bank of Japan acting with US Treasury/Bessent assistance) reflects a deepening industrial and financial "fusion" of the US and Japanese balance sheets rather than a Japanese financial crisis, given Japan's indispensable role supplying the US defense-industrial base (Patriot missiles, frigates, hypersonic missiles) and Japanese corporations' record profitability and strong fiscal position. He contends the real open question is not whether foreign capital stays in Japan but whether domestic Japanese capital reclaims control of Japan's best companies from foreign private equity (e.g., Bain) as long-standing corporate cross-shareholdings unwind. He treats the shift from negative to positive nominal rates as Japan's more acute, under-discussed domestic financial-stability risk (bank and floating-rate-mortgage balance sheets), separate from the Western press's focus on possible Japanese Treasury sales.
- channels:
  - (a) direct money creation — Bank of Japan currency intervention in the yen, described as "aided and abetted" by the US Treasury/Bessent; discussion of BoJ's multi-year shift from negative to positive nominal rates.
  - (c) collateral, leverage, basis trades — foreign investors (McDermott names Bain) borrowing cheap yen "at the wholesale level" to fund purchases of Japanese assets; McDermott says a strengthening yen is "far more likely to bankrupt" these foreign carry-trade borrowers than to hurt Japan itself.
  - (d) market structure and flows — foreign ownership of Japanese equities risen from ~5% (early 1990s) to ~35% today even as no major global fund holds >5% of assets in Japan; a price-weighted Nikkei concentrated ~40% in five stocks; unwinding corporate cross-shareholdings shifting the "marginal buyer" of Japanese equities from foreign to contested.
  - (e) fundamentals — record (fifth consecutive year) Japanese corporate profits on flat sales; a Japanese fiscal position McDermott calls "close to being a surplus"; the mechanical revaluation of Japan's dollar assets (incl. US Treasuries) in yen terms as the yen moves.
  - (f) other — extensive geopolitical/industrial-policy material: Japan as an indispensable supplier to US defense manufacturing, the US-Japan "fusion of balance sheets," and the "Pax Nipponica" framing of Japan as a third way between the US and China. This is a large share of the transcript's content and does not map to (a)-(e).
  - P (direct relevance to the project's core asset-price question) — the transcript repeatedly raises, then downplays relative to domestic-political drivers, the possibility that a Japan-driven Treasury sale or a carry-trade unwind could transmit stress into US and global asset prices.
- US Treasury market: McDermott frames the dominant Western press narrative — that Bessent acted because he feared Japan would dump its Treasury holdings — as real but incomplete. He characterizes Japan's options as either (i) selling US Treasury bonds to fund yen defense/domestic priorities, or (ii) letting the US "rent balance sheet," which is what Bessent chose; he adds the US could alternatively have simply issued more Treasuries directly, but that would not supply the Japanese industrial capacity ("battleships") the deal is really about. On existing holdings, he offers an illustrative balance-sheet point: Japan is "the largest overseas creditor of the world," and because a weakening yen makes its dollar assets (he cites "a trillion dollars in US treasuries" as an example figure, source and precision unstated) worth more in yen terms, currency moves have an "inverse balance sheet effect" on Japan's reported wealth independent of any sale. No claims are made about pensions, insurers, banks, money-market funds, hedge funds/the basis trade, or passive funds as Treasury buyers or sellers, and the transcript never uses the terms "term premium," "auction," or "bills vs. coupons" — this card cannot report on those categories from this source.
- pension funds and insurers: Grant Williams raises Japanese pension funds' unrealized bond losses as a concern, explicitly likening them to the 2022 US regional-bank unrealized-loss crisis, but supplies no figures; McDermott does not confirm or quantify this and instead redirects to Japanese banks and floating-rate mortgage borrowers as the more acute stress point from rates moving roughly 0% to 3% over "the last five years," citing payment center Zentoshin's bankruptcy as a "canary in the coal mine." The only insurer named is Tokio Marine (Japan's largest P&C insurer), raised solely via Warren Buffett/Berkshire Hathaway's investment in it as a bet on de-globalization and shipping-war insurance risk (likened to Lloyd's of London) — no claim is made about its bond or Treasury allocation. On domestic ownership, Grant Williams (not McDermott) notes Japanese equities are increasingly held "through the pension complex and through the NISA" retail scheme; McDermott engages this only regarding equity ownership, not JGBs or US Treasuries. GPIF, Dutch pension reform, and UK LDI are not mentioned anywhere in this transcript.

## The Grant Williams Podcast: Shifts Happen, Episode 013 — Grant Williams & Luke Gromen, Published August 5, 2026

*Source file: The-Grant-Williams-Podcast_Shifts_Happen_013.txt*

- kind: podcast transcript | pages: 18
- thesis: Gromen argues the US is pivoting toward "Hamiltonian economics" (tariffs, protectionism, self-sufficiency) that he says is structurally incompatible with an open capital account and dollar reserve-currency status, a tension he reads recent events (heavy Chinese gold buying, a new Hong Kong gold-custody option, the Bessent/Fed dollar-yen intervention) as forcing into the open. He frames US "fiscal dominance" — gross interest expense plus entitlement outlays nearing 95% of federal receipts — as the mechanism that triggers Treasury-market selloffs once real yields rise far enough (10-year real yields hit 2% the week before this episode), because the actual marginal Treasury buyer he identifies is a leveraged, flighty basis trade run by Cayman-Islands-domiciled hedge funds, while foreign official creditors (Japan, Germany, Korea, UK, China) increasingly compete for capital via their own defense borrowing rather than recycling surpluses into Treasuries. He and Grant Williams discuss, as a live possibility rather than a settled fact, that a full Japanese pivot to defending the yen — selling Treasuries and repatriating GPIF assets — would force the US to backstop its own bond market directly.
- channels:
  - (a) direct money creation — Fed/Treasury dollar swap lines extended to Japan, described by Gromen as "a weak form of yield curve control"; a hypothetical Fed/Treasury backstop of Treasury demand; discussion of gold as an alternative settlement/reserve asset within a more Hamiltonian system.
  - (c) collateral, leverage, basis trades — Cayman-Islands-domiciled, highly levered hedge funds running the basis trade, named as the actual marginal Treasury buyer, which "degross" (sell) their Treasury books whenever equity volatility rises.
  - (d) market structure and flows — foreign official creditors (Japan, Germany, Korea, UK, China) described as shifting from recycling surpluses into Treasuries toward competing for capital via their own (defense-related) issuance; China described as having stopped buying US bonds.
  - (e) fundamentals — Gromen's "fiscal dominance" framework (true interest expense/entitlements as % of receipts), the 2% real-10-year-yield threshold, headline PCE inflation, and US debt-to-GDP.
  - (f) other — extensive Hamiltonian-economics/tariff/capital-controls geopolitics, the US-China gold/reserve-currency contest, and Gulf-state gold-custody politics; a large share of the transcript and outside the (a)-(e) taxonomy.
  - P (direct relevance to the project's core asset-price question) — the transcript's central, repeated subject: what specifically moves US Treasury yields, the dollar (DXY), gold, and equities.
- US Treasury market: Gromen states plainly that "the Chinese aren't buying our bonds anymore" (no source given) and that Japan, Germany, Korea, and the UK all announced, within "a week or two" of each other, plans to borrow more for defense spending — which, since Germany and Korea are net creditors and the UK is (his words) "the second-biggest creditor of the United States," he says pulls up US yields by competing for capital rather than recycling surpluses into Treasuries. He identifies the actual marginal Treasury buyer as leveraged, Cayman-Islands-domiciled basis-trade hedge funds, who sell ("degross") their Treasury books whenever equity volatility rises — a pattern he says has repeated "five or six different times" since 2020. He describes Fed dollar-swap-line support for Japan — which the transcript names as "FEMA swap lines" [almost certainly a mishearing/mis-transcription of "Fed swap lines"; reported here as it appears in the source text] — as "a weak form of yield curve control" meant to avert outright Japanese Treasury sales. In a hypothetical about Japan "going full bore" (selling Treasuries, buying yen), Gromen suggests Bessent/Fed Chair Warsh would simply backstop demand ("we'll buy them all... at 4.6%") — his own inference about a likely policy response, not a reported fact. The transcript never uses the terms "term premium," "auction," or "bills vs. coupons"; the nearest analogue is Gromen's claim of "insufficient demand... at the long end" of the market (i.e., a coupon-market framing, without using "bills" as a contrast) and the Calomiris real-yield threshold cited below. Passive bond funds and money-market funds are not mentioned as Treasury buyers anywhere in this transcript.
- pension funds and insurers: GPIF (Japan's Government Pension Investment Fund) is the only named pension or insurer entity in this transcript, and it appears strictly inside a hypothetical. Grant Williams first floats it — "were Japan to go full bore, sell treasuries, buy yen, encourage the GPIF to …" of a "beggar-thy-neighbor" policy — and Gromen affirms and extends the same hypothetical ("they were talking about GPIF, repatriation... bring capital back to finance their own stuff, …"). Both speakers frame this as a possible future Japanese response to a stronger yen, not as a claim that GPIF is currently repatriating assets or reducing Treasury holdings; Gromen's own gloss is that if Japan tried it, the US would simply absorb the selling ("in a pinch, Bessent can buy all those... We'll buy them all"). No Japanese life insurers, no European pension funds (no Dutch reform or UK LDI mention), and no US pension funds are named; the closest adjacent aside is a rhetorical jab that bond manager Lacy Hunt's fund would go "from buying half of Texas to buying a 7-Eleven store... on a real basis" under runaway inflation — illustrative rhetoric about long-duration bond investors generally, not a sourced claim about pension or insurer portfolios.
