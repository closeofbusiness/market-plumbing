# What the Gorton–Lewellen–Metrick ratio actually measures: denominator and numerator construction, absolute levels, authors' own causal reading, and whether the series survives extension past 2010. Sources: NBER WP 17777 (Jan 2012, incl. its Appendix) and the Federal Reserve Z.1 Financial Accounts, release of 2026-06-11 (2026:Q1 data), z1_csv_files.zip, 5,581 quarterly series indexed locally.

**Verdict: `cannot_determine`**

**Headline.** The operator's sharpening is wrong on its facts but right in its conclusion, and the conclusion is stronger than he stated. GLM's denominator is NOT market-valued wealth: it is FL894194005, "All sectors; total liabilities and equity" — an unconsolidated sum of gross financial claims containing no directly-held tangible assets (household real estate, $62.9trn at 2026:Q1, is entirely outside it) and only 9–31% market-valued equity across the sample. So "safe supply drives the valuation in the denominator" mis-describes an aggregate that is 62–90% par/book claims. But substitute the Fed's own consolidated wealth series (FL892090005, table S.1.b) for GLM's gross-claims denominator and the constancy vanishes: safe assets rise from 48.7% of US wealth (1952:Q4) to 68.5% (2026:Q1), trend +0.001591/quarter (s.e. 0.000050), t=31.5, +47.3pp over 297 quarters. The constancy is a property of the denominator GLM chose, not of the economy. And the cited "s.e. 0.003" is the standard error of a regression intercept, not the dispersion of the share: the residual SD is 0.0231 — 7.7x the mean in CV terms, range 26.95–37.77% — confirmed three independent ways.

**Discriminating test.** Nothing inside the GLM ratio can separate (2) from (3): the numerator is a strict subset of the denominator, so co-movement in the par block is near-accounting. Identification requires variation that hits one side only, and the Z.1's own three-way decomposition supplies it — the Fed publishes levels (FL), transactions (FA) and revaluations (FR) for each series. Run local projections both ways on quarterly data: (i) FA_safe (net new safe-asset issuance, transactions only) on lagged FR_equity (holding gains, no transactions); (ii) FR_equity on lagged FA_safe restricted to the collateral-eligible block (Treasuries plus agency MBS held outside the Federal Reserve, i.e. free float, not gross outstanding). Wealth-driven demand predicts (i) positive and (ii) null; collateral-driven supply predicts the reverse. Both must be conditioned on the policy rate — Nagel (QJE 2016) shows the T-bill-supply coefficient collapsing from −95.35 (s.e. 27.83) to −9.51 (s.e. 12.00) once the fed funds rate enters, so an unconditioned result is uninterpretable. Only ~130 FR series ship in the standard z1_csv_files.zip (mostly federal-sector); the full FR set must be pulled from the Fed's Data Download Program. The sharpest single natural experiment is the 1998–2001 Treasury paydown, which cut collateral supply while equity wealth peaked: GLM's own Panel B coefficient of −0.466 (s.e. 0.048) says private issuance filled the gap, which is evidence against a binding aggregate collateral constraint — but that same coefficient is the one their Monte Carlo could not clear (≈30% of simulated coefficients were smaller, leaving mechanical ratio construction as a live explanation), so it cannot carry the argument alone. A second, cleaner instrument is QE, which swapped Treasuries for reserves — changing collateral composition without changing net wealth.

## Analysis

**(a) The denominator.** GLM never name a series. They write that they examine "the total liabilities and equity in all sectors (which approximates total assets)" (w17777, p.5). The Z.1 publishes exactly one series with that name: **FL894194005.Q, "All sectors; total liabilities and equity"**, Flow of Funds Matrix row 2 col 16. Identification is by inference, but it survives three checks: (i) GLM say assets run "about four times GDP in 1952 to more than ten times GDP" in 2010 — FL894194005/GDP is 4.56x at 1952:Q4 and 11.01x at 2010:Q4; (ii) they say the ratio grew "by a factor of 2.5" — I get 2.41; (iii) my reconstruction of their ratio on this denominator reproduces their Table 1 intercept and standard errors (below).

What this denominator is: the liability-plus-equity side of every sector's balance sheet, summed **without consolidation**. It therefore counts a Treasury bill once as a federal liability, again as the MMF share issued against it, again as any repo written on it. It is gross claims, not wealth. Comparison to the Fed's own consolidated measure is stark: FL894194005 was 1.32x US net wealth in 1952:Q4 and is **2.51x** at 2026:Q1. Chain-lengthening in intermediation is roughly half of the denominator's growth in ratio terms.

It contains **no directly-held tangible assets**. Owner-occupied housing and consumer durables — $62.93trn of household nonfinancial assets at 2026:Q1 — never appear, because households issue no equity. Real capital enters only capitalised inside corporate equity (at market) and "miscellaneous other equity" (noncorporate net worth). The project's stated inference — "the denominator is substantially market value (equities, real estate)" — is false on real estate and overstated on equity. **This is the finding that most constrains the operator.** The $31.40trn of net holding gains in household net worth 2023–25, 83.7% equity-linked, maps onto GLM's denominator only through its equity component; the real-estate portion does not enter at all.

**(b) The numerator, and the par/market question.** The Appendix (w17777 pp.13–14) itemises ~45 Z.1 series: bank deposits, MMF shares, fed funds and repo, commercial paper, bankers' acceptances, interbank, Treasuries, savings bonds, agency and budget-agency securities, municipal securities, plus 85% of MBS/ABS and long-term financial-sector debt. Removed: mutual fund shares, life insurance reserves, pension entitlements, taxes payable, REIT mortgages, and intragovernmental holdings (removed from **both** sides). The 85% haircut is asserted, not derived.

Verdict on the project's sharpening: **partly right, but it does not bite the way the project thought.** The numerator is essentially all par/book — correct. But the denominator is *also* mostly par/book. Market-valued instruments (corporate equities + mutual fund shares; the Z.1 revalues only these, per the Financial Accounts guide) were **11.6%** of the denominator at 1952:Q4, fell to **9.3%** at 1980:Q4 and 10.0% at 1990:Q4, and are **31.1%** at 2026:Q1. Crucially, the numerator is a strict **subset** of the denominator. GLM's ratio is not money-over-wealth; it is safe par liabilities over all liabilities-and-equity — a **leverage-composition** statistic. GLM saw the nesting problem and ran a Monte Carlo (10,000 draws, eight series grown by iid normal shocks), finding fewer than 7% of simulated trend coefficients smaller than theirs; that simulation does not impose the accounting nesting, so it under-tests the concern.

The Fed's own extension confirms the measurement issue is real: Almadani, Batty, Nemschoff & Passmore (FEDS Notes, 2020-11-09) rebuild the denominator from the **asset** side plus "instrument discrepancies" precisely because "the liability is at book value and the asset holding is reported at market value." Correcting it changes nothing material — they report mean **31.1%**, SD **2.3%**, 1952–2020.

**A correction the project needs.** "33.2% (s.e. 0.003) every year since 1952" over-reads the paper. 0.332/0.003 is the *intercept* of a trend regression (Table 1 Panel A, n=239, 1952:Q1–2011:Q3, R²=0.012). Back out the residual SD analytically: with Σ(t−t̄)²=1,137,640, se(a)=s·0.12978, so **s=0.02312** — and this reproduces their reported se(trend)=0.00002 exactly. My independent Z.1 reconstruction gives residual SD **0.0231**, mean 33.03%, min 27.66%, max 37.77%, and OLS intercept 0.3272 (s.e. 0.0030). The Fed's 2020 note gives SD 2.3%. Three routes, same answer. The share is "roughly one-third ±2.3pp", not pinned to three decimals. GLM's own text says 30–35%. Further: their **low** estimate is *not* constant — trend −0.00021 (s.e. 0.00002), t=−11.71, R²=0.367, a fall of **5.0 percentage points** over the sample. The constancy result depends on the high estimate, which is the one that includes "financial business total miscellaneous liabilities" — the least money-like component.

**(d) The authors' own reading.** They give both, and are explicit that they cannot separate them. Demand-side: the stability "implies that the demand for information-insensitive debt has been relatively constant as a fraction of the total assets" (p.3), hedged as holding only "to the extent that the demand … equates with supply" (p.6). Supply-side, and much sharper: "The production of total assets appears to require safe debt as an input" (p.11) — which is the operator's channel (3) stated by GLM themselves. They close by asking "Why is the safe-asset share constant?" as an open question (p.12). GLM is not a null; it is a documented puzzle whose authors decline to sign the causality.

**(e) Extension past 2010 — and the constancy breaks.** The only published extension I found is the FEDS Note through 2020. Nobody has published past that. My reconstruction (2026:Q1 vintage) gives: 2011:Q4 34.13%, 2015:Q4 30.85%, 2019:Q4 28.06%, 2021:Q4 28.19%, 2024:Q4 28.02%, **2025:Q4 26.95% (the minimum of the entire 1952–2026 sample), 2026:Q1 27.49%.** The 2012–2026 mean is 29.97%. The share has left GLM's stated band.

**Why it broke — and this is the result that matters.** Decompose the identity log(share) = log(numerator/par block) + log(1 − equity share). From 2011:Q4 to 2026:Q1 the numerator grew 1.95x and the par (ex-equity) denominator grew 1.95x, so **numerator/par block went 44.46% → 44.43%** — unchanged to two decimals over fourteen years — while the market equity wedge grew 3.97x. Holding the equity wedge at its 2011 share would leave the GLM ratio at 34.10% instead of 27.49%. Over the full 1952–2026 sample the par ratio has CV 5.27% versus 7.70% for GLM's headline ratio, and post-1980 it is dramatically tighter (subperiod SDs 1.37, 1.18, 1.11pp versus 2.33, 2.11, 1.63pp). Variance decomposition of the log share: **55.0% comes from the equity-valuation term, 42.1% from the par ratio, 2.9% covariance.** Most of the movement in GLM's "constant" is equity revaluation, which has nothing to do with safe assets.

**Direct test of wealth-driven demand.** Regress Δlog(safe-asset stock) on Δlog(market equity wedge) and Δlog(par block), quarterly 1952:Q2–2026:Q1, n=296: par block **0.978 (s.e. 0.048), t=20.2**; equity wedge **−0.090 (s.e. 0.0095), t=−9.5**; R²=0.582. Equity alone: **−0.0077 (s.e. 0.0132), t=−0.59, R²=0.001.** Post-1980 (n=185): par 1.227 (0.055), equity −0.126 (0.0096). The par coefficient near unity is close to tautological (the numerator is ~44% of the par block). The equity coefficient is **not** tautological — equity is disjoint from the numerator — and it is zero-to-negative. Over 74 years the safe-asset stock has no unconditional relationship with market-valued equity wealth, and if anything rises when equity falls (flight into MMFs, which are in the numerator). Explanation (2) as stated — richer holders want proportionally more safety, so the numerator follows the denominator — fails on the revaluation margin, which is where 80.2% of the 2023–25 wealth gain came from.

**Cannot establish.** (1) GLM never name their denominator series. FL894194005 is an identification by inference from their verbatim phrase plus three quantitative checks (assets/GDP 4.56→11.01, factor 2.41, and reproduction of their Table 1 intercept and standard errors). It is not a citation and should not be published as one without writing to the authors. (2) I could not reconstruct their numerator line-by-line: FL793130005, FL723169103, FL643169103, FL783169605, FL723163003, FL733163003, FL443163053, FL613163003, FL643163003, FL503163005, FL693168005, FL313112003, FL313161400 and FL343150005 are all absent from the current Z.1 — the bank-holding-company, savings-institution, finance-company and funding-corporation sub-sectors were consolidated away after 2012. My top-down reconstruction (sector totals minus GLM's stated exclusions minus the 15% haircut) matches their sample mean to 0.2pp and their standard errors exactly, but it is my series, not theirs. Two known biases, both of which make my numerator slightly too HIGH and therefore make the post-2011 decline, if anything, understated: I do not haircut bank loans n.e.c. to nonbank financials, and I could not locate and remove equity-REIT mortgage liabilities. (3) The 2026-06-11 vintage differs from the 2011:Q3 vintage GLM used; 1952–2011 levels have been revised, which is the likely reason my trend coefficient is +0.000025 (s.e. 0.000022) where theirs is −0.00004 (s.e. 0.00002) — both statistically indistinguishable from zero, but the sign flip means the trend result is vintage-dependent and should not be cited in either direction. (4) The AER website appendix (referenced elsewhere as Tables A.5/A.6) was not retrieved; I worked from the NBER WP appendix, which itemises the numerator only and never documents the denominator. (5) GLM's 85% haircut on MBS/ABS and long-term financial debt is asserted without justification anywhere in the paper; the entire "high vs low" gap, and hence the constancy result itself, is sensitive to it. (6) No causal direction is established. Quarterly orthogonality between the safe stock and equity revaluation does not rule out low-frequency or state-dependent causality — Geanakoplos's leverage cycle is episodic, not linear, and a linear quarterly regression is the wrong instrument for detecting it. (7) I have not verified the project's Gabaix–Koijen, KVJ or Fed FSR figures; they are outside this lens.

# Rebuild the GLM safe-asset ratio on current Fed Z.1 Financial Accounts (2026Q1 vintage, released 2026-06-10) and test the operator arithmetic directly: chart the path, and decompose both numerator and denominator into transactions versus revaluation.

**Verdict: `cannot_determine`**

**Headline.** The operator's sharpening is correct on the facts but the constancy it attacks no longer exists — and the one construction choice that produces the constancy is whether equity market value sits in the denominator. Rebuilt on Z.1, the GLM share replicates at 0.3294 (s.e. 0.0030) over their exact 239-quarter window, then breaks: 35.15% in 2011Q3 to 27.99% in 2026Q1, with 2025Q4 at 27.45% the lowest of 297 quarters since 1952. Swap the denominator from "all-sector liabilities and equity" (FL894194005) to "all-sector liabilities" (FL894190005) and the same numerator is flat — 45.13% to 45.01% over the identical period. Sixty-seven per cent of the variance of the famous "constant" is explained by one regressor: equity as a share of the denominator (corr -0.818).

**Discriminating test.** A wealth-orthogonal shock to the *par supply* of the most money-like collateral, observed at a frequency at which wealth is unchanged. Two are already dated and available. **(i) Debt-ceiling bill-supply collapses and rebounds (2011, 2013, 2015, 2021, 2023, 2025).** Treasury bill supply falls by hundreds of billions and rebounds within weeks for purely legislative reasons; household wealth over such a window is fixed. Collateral-driven supply predicts that GC repo volume contracts and risk-asset valuations soften with a lag of weeks, with the sign reversing on the post-resolution bill deluge. Wealth-driven demand predicts the opposite signature: quantities are demand-determined, so the *price* of safety absorbs the shock one-for-one (bill-OIS, GC repo minus IORB, the KVJ convenience yield) while private substitutes — MMF take-up at the Fed's RRP, uninsured deposits, ABCP — fill the quantity gap inside the same quarter with the total par-safe stock unchanged. **(ii) The 2022–2025 QT and RRP drain**: ~$2.5trn moved out of the RRP and roughly $2trn of Treasuries returned to private balance sheets on a pre-announced schedule set by the FOMC, not by asset prices. In both cases the observable that separates the hypotheses is *whether the total par-safe quantum is invariant to the shock*: invariance implies demand-determined quantity (composition-only, GLM's own reading); co-movement implies supply-determined quantity. My complementarity result (+0.509, NW s.e. 0.089) is the low-power version of exactly this test and points to co-movement, but it is contemporaneous, unidentified, and cannot carry the claim. Without one of these designs there is no publishable statement about direction — only the negative results above, which are themselves publishable and which the constancy alone can never deliver.

## Analysis

**1. What GLM actually built (read from the paper, not inferred).** NBER w17777 / AER 102(3):101-06. Their numerator is not an instrument list; it is *total liabilities of the government and financial sectors*, minus exclusions (mutual fund shares, life insurance reserves, pension fund reserves, taxes payable, REIT mortgages, intragovernmental claims), with a 15% haircut on MBS/ABS and long-term financial debt. My narrow par-money numerator (Treasuries, agency, checkable deposits and currency, time and savings deposits, other deposits, MMF shares, repo and fed funds, open market paper, interbank, municipals) grew $0.43trn → $97.96trn, of which **98.8% is cumulated transactions and 0.3% revaluation**.

In the data there is no correlation to explain. # Supply-drives-valuation: the collateral/leverage-capacity mechanism (Geanakoplos, Adrian–Shin, Brunnermeier–Pedersen), argued at full strength and then tested at its first step.

**Verdict: `cannot_determine`**

**Headline.** The collateral multiplier ran backwards over the period the operator hypothesis needs it to run forwards: US repo liabilities per dollar of marketable Treasury fell from 1.070 (2007Q4) to 0.219 (2026Q1) — Treasuries +580.0%, repo +39.2% — so safe-asset supply cannot have relaxed a leverage ceiling that was itself tightening. The one fully-specified model of the mechanism (Lenel 2018) moves bill yields 28bp on a QE2-sized safe-asset shock and corporate bond yields 1bp, with equity effects negligible, and the measured shadow price of Treasury collateralisability is 0.53bp (s.e. 0.009).

**Discriminating test.** Use exogenous, high-frequency variation in Treasury bill supply that moves collateral without moving wealth, and require the intermediate link to move before crediting the outcome. Lenel's design already exists for credit: regress changes in the BAA spread in the week around mid-April tax dates on *unexpected* debt issuance (2005–2015 ex-2008), slope 4.9bp per 1% of consumption. Extend it to the equity denominator with three joint requirements. (i) OUTCOME: the response of the aggregate equity valuation ratio (or the equity risk premium) to the same bill-supply surprises, over tax dates, debt-ceiling suspensions/resolutions and TGA rebuilds — episodes where supply swings by hundreds of billions for statutory reasons orthogonal to household net worth. Wealth-driven demand predicts a coefficient statistically indistinguishable from zero; collateral-driven supply predicts a positive coefficient. (ii) MECHANISM: the same shocks must move the observable price of collateral in the right direction — Ross's maturity-matched collateral spread (mean 0.53bp, s.e. 0.009), GC repo minus IORB, and non-centrally-cleared haircuts. If bill supply surges and the collateral spread does not compress, no collateral channel operated regardless of what equities did. (iii) MAGNITUDE GATE: the estimated equity response must be reconcilable with the measured shadow price. A channel whose price is 0.53bp and whose marginal dealer-financing pass-through is 4.9 cents per Treasury dollar cannot deliver percentage-point moves in valuation ratios; a large estimated coefficient without a corresponding move in (ii) is evidence of a confound (the same fiscal news moving deficits, growth expectations and the policy rate) rather than of collateral. Run the same specification separately on credit and MBS, where Haddad–Muir find intermediaries do price (0.57, s.e. 0.22 and 0.30, s.e. 0.13) versus stocks (0.12, s.e. 0.09): the collateral hypothesis requires the equity coefficient to be of the same order as the credit coefficient. If it is an order of magnitude smaller, as the cross-asset evidence already implies, the supply→valuation arrow is dead at the aggregate and the constancy must be explained on the demand side or as a denominator artefact.

## Analysis

## 1. The mechanism at full strength

Geanakoplos's leverage cycle is not a story about credit volume; it is a story about *who is marginal*. In collateral equilibrium the asset price is set by the most optimistic agent who can still lever, and the asset trades above every agent's private valuation because holding it confers borrowing capacity ("The Leverage Cycle", Cowles P1304 / NBER Macro Annual 24, 2009). Loosen the collateral constraint and fewer optimists are needed to absorb the float, so the marginal buyer moves up the belief distribution and the price rises with *no change in fundamentals*. Adrian–Shin supply the balance-sheet mechanics: in Flow of Funds data 1963–2006, households show a strongly negative leverage-to-asset-growth scatter (passive), commercial banks cluster on zero (targeted), and security brokers and dealers show a strongly positive relationship — leverage is procyclical, and the adjustment margin is repo. Their panel of five investment banks (10-K/10-Q, N=235) gives leverage growth on total-asset growth of 0.83 (p=0.00, R²=62%) and on repo growth 0.22 (p=0.00, R²=24%, N=196). Brunnermeier–Pedersen (RFS 22(6), 2009) close the loop: margins are destabilising when funding and market liquidity are mutually reinforcing, producing spirals. Stack these and the operator's claim follows: more Treasuries → more pledgeable collateral → more dealer and non-bank leverage → higher risk-asset prices → a bigger GLM denominator. The safe-asset share would then be an equilibrium of its own making.

That chain has one load-bearing link: **collateral availability must be the binding constraint on risk-asset holdings.** Everything below tests that link.

## 2. The shadow price of collateralisability is 0.53 basis points

If collateral were scarce relative to the demand to lever, Treasuries valued for their collateral services would trade rich by an economically large amount. Ross ("The Treasury Collateral Spread and Levered Safe-Asset Production", draft 17 Apr 2025, Federal Reserve Board; ~540,000 repo transactions, confidential FR2052a-based supervisory data, 13-year sample) measures exactly this: the maturity-matched spread between Treasuries used more often as repo collateral and those used less is **0.53bp (s.d. 0.49bp; s.e. of the mean 0.009bp)**, roughly 60% of the 5-year cheapest-to-deliver basis and 25% of the 5-year on-the-run/off-the-run spread. He also reports that banks in his sample pledged **2.6% of a CUSIP's total market value** as repo collateral. The constraint is real and priced — the spread rose to 0.38bp around March 2020 versus a 0.18bp pre-2020 average — but its unconditional shadow price is half a basis point. A constraint with a half-basis-point multiplier cannot be the thing setting equity valuations.

## 3. The multiplier is small at the margin and has collapsed in level

Fed staff measured the marginal pass-through directly (FEDS Note, 22 Oct 2024, "Assessment of Dealer Capacity to Intermediate in Treasury and Agency MBS Markets"; FR Y-9C, FR 2004, TreasuryDirect, Z.1). Treasuries outstanding rose from $8.3trn (2012) to $22.9trn (Jul 2024), +176%; dealer gross Treasury positions rose 80% and dealer secured financing 43%. Regressing dealer financing on Treasuries outstanding gives **0.049 over Jan 2012–Jul 2024** — 4.9 cents of dealer secured financing per additional dollar of Treasury supply (0.073 in QT2, Jun 2022–Jul 2024); gross positions give 0.019. And the SLR was not binding: the six largest dealers ran 5.45–6.67% with $7.307trn of aggregate headroom (39% of leverage exposure) at Jun 2024, enough to expand Treasury intermediation by 316%.

At the aggregate level the ratio has fallen by four fifths. Z.1 all-sector security repurchase agreement liabilities (FL892150005, 2026Q1 vintage, released 2026-06-11) against MSPD total marketable Treasury debt: **1.070 (2007Q4: $4.854trn / $4.537trn) → 0.374 (2012Q4: $4.129trn / $11.053trn) → 0.219 (2026Q1: $6.757trn / $30.846trn)**. Over 2007Q4–2026Q1 marketable Treasuries grew 580.0% and repo liabilities 39.2%. Caveats: Z.1 repo is not exclusively Treasury-collateralised, and the denominator includes SOMA holdings unavailable for private repo — but the Fed's SOMA share was far smaller in 2007 than now, so netting it out steepens the decline rather than reversing it. This is the project's earlier finding restated on a different series: velocity is not the growth engine, and Singh's own velocity series ran 3.0 (end-2007) → ~2.5 (end-2011) → 2.2 (end-2012), IMF WP/11/256 and successors.

## 4. Where marginal collateral actually goes: back into safe assets

The most levered collateral users in the system deploy their borrowing to hold *Treasuries*, not risk assets. Fed FEDS Note, 22 Jun 2026, "Decomposing Hedge Funds' U.S. Treasury Exposures" (SEC Form PF, ~85% of aggregate gross assets): at Sep 2025 hedge funds held $2.4trn long and $1.6trn short Treasuries ($4.0trn gross) against **$3.0trn of repo cash borrowing**, more than doubled since early 2023. The cash-futures basis trade was ~$830bn (35% of long exposure, 3.5% of privately-held Treasuries), swap-spread arbitrage ~$305bn. FSB, "Vulnerabilities in Government Bond-backed Repo Markets" (4 Feb 2026): ~$16trn of government-bond-backed repo globally at end-2024; hedge fund repo borrowing ~$3trn, 25% of their assets; most non-centrally-cleared repo at zero haircut; reuse rates of 65% of collateral received (nine US intermediaries, Infante et al. 2020) and ~85% of incoming Treasuries (Hempel et al., OFR, Nov 2024). This is a closed loop inside the safe-asset complex. It expands the gross measured stock of collateralised claims; it does not finance the equity denominator.

## 5. The model that grants the mechanism and still gets ~zero on equities

Lenel ("Safe Assets, Collateralized Lending and Monetary Policy", Sep 2018, FRBSF working version) builds precisely the operator's economy: heterogeneous risk tolerance, risk-tolerant agents issuing collateralised bonds to lever, long safe assets held *because* they are good collateral, calibrated to measured safe-bond quantities 1990–2015. A permanent 5%-of-consumption reduction in long safe assets (QE2 scale) lowers the short rate by **28bp**, raises the term spread 8bp — and moves the corporate bond yield by **+1bp**, with equity effects he calls negligible. The reverse swap (long for short) moves T-bills +17bp, long safe +11bp, BAA by **less than 1bp**. His stated reason is the decisive one for this lens: the marginal investor in risky assets is the intermediate-risk-aversion agent, not the levered collateral user, who is marginal only in the *safe* market. His identified empirical counterpart — regressing BAA-spread changes around mid-April tax dates on unexpected debt issuance (2005–2015, ex-2008) — gives a slope of **4.9bp per 1% of consumption**, matching the model's 4.1bp. So the collateral channel exists, is identified, and is a credit-spread phenomenon of single-digit basis points.

## 6. Cross-asset evidence says equities are the wrong asset class

Haddad & Muir (JF 76(6), 2021; NBER w28692) test whether intermediary risk-bearing capacity prices aggregate risk premia by exploiting household participation. Predictive regressions of normalised excess returns on the standardised AEM/HKM intermediary factor, Hodrick (1992) standard errors: **stocks 0.12 (0.09), R²=0.8%, insignificant, N=167 quarters**; Treasuries −0.01 (0.07); options 0.29 (0.10)***; sovereigns 0.38 (0.17)**; commodities 0.18 (0.10)*; FX 0.18 (0.09)*; MBS 0.30 (0.13)**; credit **0.57 (0.22)***, R²=31.6%, N=47**. Six of eight are significant and all six are the more-intermediated classes. They attribute ~60% of CDS risk-premium variation to intermediaries and ~40% of stock risk-premium variation to households. Equities — the overwhelming share of the revaluation the project has already established ($31.40trn of the $39.16trn 2023–25 rise in household net worth was net holding gains, 83.7% equity-linked) — are the class where intermediary and therefore collateral capacity has the least measured pricing power.

Direct collateralisation of equities is correspondingly trivial. FINRA margin debit balances (Rule 4521(d)) were **$1,417,225mn in July 2026** (peak $1,502,072mn in June), against Z.1 all-sector corporate equities at market value of **$106.89trn (LM893064105, 2026Q1)** — **1.33%** (2.04% against nonfinancial corporate equity liabilities of $69.51trn).

## 7. Where the mechanism does work, and why that does not rescue the hypothesis

The leverage cycle's best empirical case is housing, and it is strong. Geanakoplos documents Alt-A/subprime down payments falling from ~13% (2000Q1) to under 3% at the 2006 peak — leverage from ~7.7 to above 30 — with house prices peaking with leverage. Greenwald & Guren (AER 115(10), Oct 2025) show the disputed range collapses once rental-market segmentation is estimated, and conclude credit standards explain **32–53%** of the boom (against Kaplan–Mitman–Violante, JPE 128(9), 2020, who find credit conditions move homeownership and leverage but essentially not prices). But this channel runs through *haircuts on the risk asset itself* — LTV and PTI on mortgages — not through the quantity of the safe asset. Treasury supply does not set mortgage LTVs. Conflating "leverage against houses rose" with "Treasury issuance financed the housing boom" is the equivocation the operator hypothesis needs and cannot have.

Two further constraints. First, the crisis evidence: Krishnamurthy, Nagel & Orlov (JF 69(6), 2014) find repo backed by private-sector collateral fell only **$182bn** in the crisis, that most repo is Treasury/Agency-backed, and that the pattern was a dealer credit crunch rather than a run — the collateral system's stress was concentrated where Treasuries were not. Second, the sign is not identified. Caballero, Farhi & Gourinchas (JEP 31(3), 2017) predict the *opposite* sign from the same premise: safe-asset shortage raises safe-asset prices, depresses safe rates and pushes valuations of risky claims up. Both are "safe assets cause valuations" theories with opposing signs, so any observed positive co-movement between safe supply and valuations discriminates between neither. And Jank, Moench & Schneider (Bundesbank DP 39/2021; SAFE WP 355) show reuse rises endogenously when Eurosystem purchases make collateral scarce — the effective collateral supply is elastic in the raw stock, which is exactly what a binding quantity ceiling would not be.

## 8. What this bounds

Adrian & Shin's own forecasting table is the honest residue of the mechanism. Weekly FR2004 primary-dealer data, 3 Jan 1990–2 Apr 2008: lagged repo growth forecasts VIX changes with coefficient −0.28 (p=0.00), raising adjusted R² from 4.9% to 9.0%, and forecasts the *volatility risk premium* at −0.21 (p=0.05) — but forecasts realised S&P 500 volatility at **0.01 (p=0.89)**. Dealer balance-sheet expansion compresses the *price* of risk without predicting the *quantity* of risk. That is a genuine collateral-capacity effect on risk premia. It is also a second-order effect on an aggregate whose 2023–25 change was 80.2% revaluation, and it is the wrong order of magnitude to generate a 47.3pp move in safe assets as a share of consolidated wealth, or to explain a ratio that is stable to three decimals for reasons the project has already shown are a property of GLM's chosen denominator.

**Cannot establish.** This lens tests one arrow and can only reject it. Refuting supply→valuation does not establish wealth→safe-assets; the affirmative case for demand-following-wealth is untested here and Haddad–Muir's 40%-of-stock-risk-premium-to-households is evidence about who prices equities, not about what determines safe-asset quantities. Three specific gaps. (1) The repo/Treasury ratio (1.070 → 0.219) mixes measurement bases: Z.1 FL892150005 repo liabilities are not exclusively Treasury-collateralised and are partly netted, MSPD marketable is par outstanding including SOMA. It is an order-of-magnitude indicator of a declining multiplier, not a clean multiplier. I did not construct a Treasury-collateral-only repo series, and no public series supports one. (2) Lenel's near-zero equity effect is a calibrated model result, not an estimate; his identified empirical counterpart covers the BAA spread only (slope 4.9bp), and no published work I located estimates the response of *equity valuations* to exogenous Treasury supply at any frequency. That is the missing regression, not a settled null. (3) Collateral capacity is not exhausted by repo: securities lending, derivatives initial margin, FHLB advances and prime-brokerage borrowing bases use the same stock, and no single-basis aggregate exists — the project's own invariant forbids summing them. I also could not verify a post-2012 Manmohan Singh velocity figure with a citable vintage; the 3.0 → 2.2 path is 2007–2012 and everything after is commentary. Finally, the episodic case survives: March 2020, September 2019 and quarter-end CIP dislocations show the constraint binds occasionally and hard, and nothing here bounds what a binding episode does to valuations — only what non-binding steady state does.

# THE RIVAL — wealth-driven demand for safety (safe assets as an effect of rising wealth, not a cause)

**Verdict: `third_factor`**

**Headline.** Treasury supply did not grow relative to the economy across the entire span of the "constancy" — federal liabilities were 0.84 of GDP in 1952:Q4 and 0.87 in 2011:Q3 (×1.04), having fallen to 0.49 by 1980 — so the collateral-quantity first stage has no in-sample variation; what grew was repo re-use, which is velocity, not stock. But the rival cannot claim the ground: household safe-asset holdings have ZERO elasticity to wealth (β=-0.071, s.e. 0.088; H0 β=1 rejected at t=-12.1) and unit elasticity to nominal GDP (1.049), because safe assets are bought out of saving and ~76% of the wealth increase is revaluation, which generates no saving. Neither hypothesis is supported by the constancy.

**Discriminating test.** The sign of the RISK PREMIUM response to a legislatively-driven safe-asset supply shock. The two hypotheses predict the same quantity correlation and opposite price responses, so the price is the discriminator — not the quantity ratio, and not the GLM share, which is uninformative either way.

Under wealth-driven demand, safe supply meets a pre-existing downward-sloping demand schedule: an exogenous supply increase moves ALONG that schedule — quantity up, convenience yield DOWN (safe yields rise relative to risky), and because the safe discount rate has risen, risk-asset valuations are flat or slightly LOWER. Risk premia unchanged or wider.

Under collateral-driven supply, the same shock RELAXES an intermediary constraint: repo volumes rise, haircuts fall, dealer balance sheets expand, and risk premia COMPRESS — valuations HIGHER. Opposite sign on the risk premium, and an observable quantity signature (haircuts, dealer netted repo) that the demand story does not predict.

Identification: use debt-ceiling episodes (2011, 2013, 2015, 2023), where bill supply contracts and then rebounds for legislative reasons orthogonal to wealth and risk appetite. The 1998–2001 surplus paydown must NOT be used — the surplus was itself driven by equity-boom capital-gains receipts, so the supply shock is endogenous to the denominator, which is exactly the trap the operator objection is about.

The single best window is the 2023 post-suspension bill surge (June–December 2023, >$1trn of net bill issuance). It has a built-in demand-schedule reading: the Fed's ON RRP is itself a safe asset, so the fraction of new bill supply absorbed by MMF drawdown of ON RRP rather than by new saving directly measures whether the demand was pre-existing and merely reallocating (wealth-driven: a schedule was already there) or newly created by the collateral itself (supply-driven). Pair the ON RRP drawdown share with (a) the change in the T-bill convenience yield (bill–OIS), (b) primary-dealer haircuts and cleared repo volumes, and (c) the equity risk premium over the same window. Concordant signs across all three settle it.

Nagel (QJE 2016) is the standing warning: the T-bill supply coefficient collapsed from −95.35 (s.e. 27.83) to −9.51 (s.e. 12.00) once the fed funds rate was included. Any such test must control for the policy rate, or it will recover the monetary stance and call it a supply effect.

## Analysis

**0. What the operator's citation omits.** GLM's Table 1 (NBER w17777, 239 quarters, 1952:Q1–2011:Q3) reports two specifications, not one. High estimate: constant 0.332 (s.e. 0.003), trend −0.00004 (s.e. 0.00002), t=−1.72, R²=0.012. Low estimate: constant 0.310 (s.e. 0.003), trend −0.00021 (s.e. 0.00002), t=−11.71, R²=0.367 — a decisive rejection of constancy, −5.0pp over the sample on GLM's own arithmetic (they concede this: "only 1.5%" under the high estimate, 5% under the low). The "33.2% every year since 1952" claim is the high estimate alone. GLM's Monte Carlo defence places their trend at roughly the 7th percentile of 10,000 simulated ratio regressions — a 7% margin — and for Panel B they concede ~30%, "leaving some combination of chance and the mechanical relationship" (GLM, w17777, p.9) as possible. GLM also list "Why is the safe-asset share constant?" among their open questions. They assert the fact; they do not explain it. Citing them for a demand-side explanation is unsupported by their own text.

**1. The rival's portfolio-choice foundation fails.** The premise — investors hold a roughly constant share of wealth in safe assets — is false cross-sectionally. Fed Distributional Financial Accounts (release 2026-06-18, 2026:Q1), safe (deposits + MMF + govt/muni securities) over financial assets: bottom 50% 25.68%, next 40% 22.10%, next 9% 18.74%, remaining top 1% 15.84%, top 0.1% 15.82%. Monotone decline in wealth. This matches Bach, Calvet & Sodini (AER 2020, 110(9): 2703–47), whose Swedish administrative panel finds the expected return on net wealth rising with net worth and exceeding the risk-free rate by roughly the full equity premium at the top 0.01% — a portfolio beta near one, i.e. a safe share near zero. Fagereng, Guiso, Malacrino & Pistaferri (Econometrica 2020, 88(1): 115–70): moving 10th→90th percentile of net worth raises returns 18pp gross, 10pp net of tax.

Note the denominator dependence, which recurs everywhere in this project: scaled by *total* assets (including housing and durables) the same 2026:Q1 shares are nearly flat across the top four groups — 14.16 / 13.28 / 14.10 / 12.41% — with only the bottom 50% differing (8.47%). Whether "safe shares are flat in wealth" is true depends entirely on the denominator chosen. Same disease as GLM.

**2. The distributional channel is quantitatively negligible.** Aggregate household safe/financial assets fell 28.32% (1989:Q3) → 18.71% (2026:Q1), −9.61pp. Shift-share decomposition: composition (wealth weights shifting toward the top) −0.42pp; within-group behaviour −9.03pp; interaction −0.16pp. The top 0.1% share of household financial assets rose 9.9% → 16.6% and the bottom 50% fell 3.5% → 2.5% over those 36 years — the sharpest documented rise in US wealth concentration — and it moved the aggregate safe share by four-tenths of a percentage point, 4.4% of the total change. The mechanism as stated in my brief ("if the rich hold a stable share in safe assets, rising top wealth mechanically raises safe-asset demand") is arithmetically valid and empirically trivial.

Mian, Straub & Sufi survive this, because their mechanism is not the one in my brief. "The Saving Glut of the Rich" (NBER w26941, tax records 1963–2019) is about *income concentration → saving flow → accumulation of debt claims*: roughly +3pp of national income in top-1% saving across periods, with top income growth explaining ~75% of household debt held as a financial asset. That is a flow story, not a wealth-revaluation story — and the distinction is decisive.

**3. Zero elasticity to wealth, unit elasticity to income.** Δlog(safe) on Δlog(household financial assets), DFA quarterly 1989:Q3–2026:Q1, n=146, Newey–West(4): β = −0.0706 (s.e. 0.0883), t = −0.80; H0 β=1 rejected at t = −12.12. At four quarters: β = −0.1305 (s.e. 0.1091), H0 β=1 at t = −10.36. Against net worth: −0.0995 (s.e. 0.1106). Against nominal GDP the long-difference elasticity is 1.049: safe/GDP 0.736 → 0.801 (trend +0.00157/qtr, NW s.e. 0.000435, t=3.61) while financial assets/GDP went 2.599 → 4.280 (trend +0.01216, s.e. 0.00073, t=16.61), a trend 7.7× larger. At business-cycle frequency the sign is actively wrong for the rival: Δlog(safe) on Δlog(GDP) = −0.2777 (s.e. 0.1005, t=−2.76). Safe accumulation is counter-cyclical — precautionary, not wealth-elastic.

The crux: safe assets are accumulated out of saving, and saving comes out of income. This project has established that ~76% of the wealth increase is revaluation ($31.40trn of the $39.16trn 2023–25 rise; $36.6trn saving plus retained earnings against a $152.9trn net-worth rise since 1995). Revaluation generates no saving and therefore no safe-asset demand. That is precisely why the elasticity to wealth is zero and the elasticity to income is one. **The rival's mechanism cannot ride on the part of the denominator that actually moves.** This also predicts the post-2011 break another agent found: the GLM share must fall once wealth/GDP rises faster than income, and wealth/GDP went 3.85 (2011:Q3) → 5.23 (2026:Q1).

**4. The pension/LDI channel runs backwards in the US.** DB/(DB+DC) entitlements: 83.6% (1989:Q3) → 58.4% (2026:Q1); DC grew ×19.1 against DB ×5.3. Broad safe (adding corporate bonds, life reserves, annuities, DB entitlements) over household financial assets: 57.67% → 35.80%, −21.9pp. Liability-driven mandated safe-asset holding is a UK/Dutch phenomenon; the US institutional shift has been from mandated fixed claims to member-directed equity. This channel has been *subtracting* from safe-asset demand for 36 years.

**5. Where the rival still lives.** Foreign official/reserve demand is genuine and is wealth-driven: rest-of-world Treasury holdings went 1.2% of GDP (1952:Q4) → 31.8% (2011:Q3), and 1.4% → 36.5% of federal liabilities — peaking exactly at the end of GLM's window, then retreating to 25.3% by 2026:Q1. Caballero, Farhi & Gourinchas (JEP 2017, 31(3): 29–46) are right that the imbalance is global, that safe supply scales with the GDP of producing countries (consistent with my unit GDP elasticity), and — critically — that its signature is a *price*, the secular decline in safe real rates, not a quantity ratio. But ROW Treasuries are ~8% of GLM's numerator. This channel cannot carry the constancy.

**6. What actually produced the constancy.** Relative to GDP over GLM's own window (1952:Q4→2011:Q3): GLM denominator FL894194005 ×2.35 (4.58→10.77 of GDP); financial-sector liabilities FL794190005 ×3.55 (1.25→4.43); federal liabilities FL314190005 ×1.04 (0.84→0.87); household deposits+MMF+Treasuries ×1.39 (0.429→0.597). The implied GLM numerator grew ×2.35, and households' direct share of GLM's safe stock roughly halved, 28.4% → 16.8%. So the growth in GLM's numerator is intra-financial gross claims — repo, fed funds, interbank, agency, 85% of MBS/ABS, financial CP — which are simultaneously a large and growing part of GLM's denominator. Numerator and denominator share the object that grew. GLM's definition is itself collateral-based (information-insensitive = repo-eligible without haircuts), so the numerator is a collateral measure by construction. The ratio is close to a share of gross intermediation *in* gross intermediation.

**7. Constraints on the operator hypothesis.** Beyond the flat federal-supply first stage in the headline: fed funds + repo went ~0.000 of GDP (1952) → 0.283 (2007:Q4) → *down* to 0.143 (2026:Q1). Bivariate lead-lag on Δlog repo (FL792150005) and Δlog household net worth (FL152090005), 1970:Q1–2026:Q1, n=224, NW(4): wealth lags predict repo (sum +1.624; lag1 +0.546, t=2.16; lag4 +0.641, t=2.43; R²=0.174) while repo lags do not predict wealth (sum −0.0043; R²=0.103). Collateral expands *after* valuations rise. Steelman: Geanakoplos's leverage cycle is an explicit feedback loop, so this is consistent with his model and does not refute it — it only shows the impulse is unidentified in aggregates. Third, direct equity leverage is ~2% of market capitalisation and untrended (FINRA margin debt $1.502trn, June 2026, against ~$75.3trn; ratio 1.99% vs 1.96% median) — secondary sourcing, and it omits derivatives and prime-brokerage leverage.

**8. The par/market sharpening.** Correct about the numerator: the household safe stock is 77.0% par-denominated at 2026:Q1 (deposits 56.6%, MMF at $1 NAV 20.4%; govt+muni securities 23.0% at market value), and 79.2% in 1989:Q3. But the prior finding stands that GLM's denominator is 62–90% par/book claims. Par against par is not a striking co-movement requiring explanation.

**Cannot establish.** (1) Pre-1989 distributional evidence. The DFA begins 1989:Q3, so every elasticity, shift-share and cross-sectional result here covers 147 quarters, not GLM's 239. The claim "the distributional channel is negligible" is established for 1989–2026 and merely extrapolated backwards. The 1952–1989 portion of GLM's window has no comparable household-level data. (2) Direction of causality from aggregates. The lead-lag result (wealth Granger-causes repo, not the reverse) is a bivariate test on two endogenous series with an obvious omitted common driver (monetary policy, nominal growth, intermediary risk appetite). It is suggestive, not identifying, and it does not refute a feedback model in which both directions operate. (3) Non-household safe-asset demand. The zero wealth-elasticity is measured on households. The marginal safe-asset demanders in the Pozsar/CFG account are institutional cash pools — corporate treasurers, insurers, sovereign reserve managers, securities lenders — which the DFA does not cover and which I did not measure. My lens is falsified for households; it is untested for institutional cash. (4) Whether the constancy is "mechanical." I have shown numerator and denominator share the intra-financial claims that grew; I have not run GLM's Monte Carlo on current data to quantify how much of the ratio's stability that sharing produces. (5) The counterfactual. Nothing here establishes what the safe-asset stock or asset valuations would have been under different Treasury supply — no exogenous variation was used. (6) GLM's exact numerator. My decomposition uses FL314190005 and FL794190005 as proxies; GLM make specific adjustments (removing federal retirement holdings and intragovernmental loans, reclassifying non-MBS agency debt, haircutting MBS/ABS to 85%) that I did not replicate, so the ×1.04 and ×3.55 figures are directionally right but not GLM's own series. (7) The margin-debt ratio is from a secondary aggregator, not FINRA's own release, and covers only one leverage channel.
