# The Manmohan Singh corpus, read first-hand: rehypothecation, collateral velocity, the source-collateral method, and what Singh himself claims the mechanism acts on

**Verdict: `undermines`**

**Headline.** Across the five core Singh collateral papers (WP/10/172, 11/256, 12/179, 17/113, 19/106) the strings "asset price", "equity price" and "stock price" appear ZERO times. Singh's formal model puts collateral efficiency into the LM curve, where it acts on OUTPUT and monetary transmission — a goods-side channel. And in the ZIRP window the operator is asking about, Singh's measured velocity FELL from 3.0 (2007) to 1.8/1.9 (2015) while equity prices doubled. The corpus does not support a collateral-circuit explanation of asset inflation; it points the other way.

**Mechanism.** What actually does the work in Singh, and it is not an asset-price mechanism: pledged collateral is a money-substitute whose circulation determines how much SECURED LENDING a given stock of securities can support. The chain is source collateral (hedge-fund margin + securities-lending on-loan) → dealer central collateral desks → repledged onward, N times. Velocity N is chain length. When N falls, financial "lubrication" falls, secured lending contracts, the cost of capital rises and OUTPUT falls — Singh's augmented LM curve M = f(Y, r, cp), WP/21/6 Annex. The dependent variable throughout is output and monetary-policy transmission, never valuation.

If someone wants to look for an asset-price version, the only Singh-sanctioned route is the pledgeability premium (WP/21/94, citing Chen et al 2019 and Caballero-Farhi): an asset's equilibrium price depends on its fundamentals AND its pledgeability, so a rise in effective safe-asset supply (= nominal supply x reuse rate) compresses the collateral premium. Note the sign: that raises the price of PLEDGEABLE assets — government bonds — i.e. it lowers their yield. It is a discount-rate mechanism dressed in collateral clothing, and it does not reach equities.

The observable to look for now, if the thesis is to be salvaged: not velocity (flat at ~2.0, and per Singh Jan-2026 possibly unmeasurable), but the LEVEL of the numerator. It is in the 10-K/20-F footnote "collateral received permitted to be sold or repledged", filed each February, and it grew 21.8% at the five largest US dealers in calendar 2025.

## Analysis

**1. What the method actually is, and what it excludes**

Velocity = (pledged collateral received by the 10–15 large dealer banks, from the "collateral received permitted to be repledged" footnote in their annual reports) ÷ (source collateral, being hedge-fund collateral plus securities-lending on-loan via custodians). Singh's own caveat, WP/17/113 p.14: it "is not intended as an exact metric", it "gives an idea of the length of the collateral chains in a given year."

Excluded by construction: triparty repo (WP/11/256 fn: collateral there "cannot be re-pledged beyond the privileged club"); the Fed RRP, where the counterparty "gets ownership but not possession" (WP/17/113 p.16); CCP initial margin and other non-reusable regulatory collateral ($2–4trn of incremental demand, WP/17/113 p.12); central-bank QE holdings; all non-movable real-economy collateral; and — the Jan-2026 point — everything intraday, because the source is an end-of-day balance-sheet footnote.

Two further method facts the project should hold: (a) the 2010 "churning factor of 4" is **not** the same series as the later velocity. It divides 40% of $10trn by $1trn of rehypothecated hedge-fund AUM (WP/10/172 Box 2). Never splice 4 → 3.0. (b) The series is not vintage-stable. WP/19/106 (2019) revised the 2010–2015 volumes upward versus WP/17/113 (2017) after adding Canadian banks: 2015 volume 5.6 → 5.8, velocity 1.8 → 1.9; 2014 1.9 → 2.0. The headline "1.8 versus 3.0" is a 2017-vintage number that its own author revised two years later.

**2. The measurements, with dates**

- end-2007: $10.0trn / $3.3trn ≈ **3.0** (WP/11/256, Nov 2011)
- end-2010: $5.8trn / $2.45trn ≈ **2.4** (WP/11/256); revised to 6.0/2.4 ≈ 2.5 in 2019
- end-2011: $6.2trn / $2.4trn ≈ **2.5** (WP/12/179, Aug 2012)
- end-2015: $5.6trn / $3.1trn ≈ **1.8** (WP/17/113, May 2017); revised to **1.9**
- end-2017: $7.5trn / $3.7trn ≈ **2.0** (WP/19/106, May 2019) — Singh calls this "a first time increase since the continuous decline since the Lehman crisis"

**The last full velocity table in any IMF working paper is WP/19/106, published May 2019, with data to end-2017. That is seven years stale as of today.** Everything after it is trade-press commentary: $8.5trn "inching back" (WP/21/6, Jan 2021); $9.4trn across **18** banks at end-2020, "highest in a decade" (Risk.net, Singh & Prince, 7 Jun 2021 — note the sample widened from 10–15 to 18, another basis change); and ~$13trn global with velocity "near 2.0 for the past decade" (Risk.net, Singh, 12 Jan 2026).

**3. Register entry C-015 needs one amendment — not a reversal**

The claim "velocity flat 2–3 years on Basel III constraints" was **not** unsourced. It is Singh, verbatim, on Mercatus Macro Musings, **30 May 2022**: "The collateral velocity is not going up. It has stuck roughly there in the last two, three years." The defect was vintage, not provenance: "the last two, three years" spoken in mid-2022 covers roughly 2019–2022. Carrying it into 2026 silently re-dates it. C-015's operative instruction (stop citing it as current) is correct and is reinforced, not undone. Singh's own Jan-2026 statement supersedes it and is stronger: flat at ~2.0 for a **decade**, and — this is the new part — the flatness may be an artefact of end-of-day accounting, because tokenised intraday reuse "leaves no accounting trace."

**4. What velocity IS now: a partial replication, primary sources, this session**

Singh's numerator is reconstructible. I pulled the footnote from the FY2025 10-Ks of the five largest US dealers (all filed Feb 2026), "collateral permitted to be sold or repledged":

JPM $1,771.0bn · GS $1,312.1bn · MS $1,190.7bn · BAC ~$1.1trn · Citi $1,064bn → **$6.44trn at 31-Dec-2025**, versus **$5.29trn at 31-Dec-2024: +21.8% y/y.** (BAC discloses only to one decimal in trillions; ±$50bn rounding on the aggregate.)

This reconciles Singh's Jan-2026 figures precisely. He wrote that JPM offers "about $1.8 trillion"; JPM's Q3-2025 10-Q (filed 4-Nov-2025) reports $1,829.9bn. He wrote US banks are "over half of a roughly $13 trillion global market"; five banks alone are 49.5% of $13trn, and Wells, BNY and State Street are not in my five. His numbers check out.

The **reuse intensity** moved the other way. Actually-repledged ÷ permitted-to-be-repledged, four banks with both figures: **82.7% (Dec-2025) versus 84.0% (Dec-2024)**. The FSB's 4-Feb-2026 repo report independently puts euro-area government-bond re-use at 50–90%, 65% of collateral received rehypothecated at nine US intermediaries, and ~85% of incoming Treasuries later used in outgoing transactions (citing Hempel et al, OFR, Nov 2024). My 82.7% sits inside that band.

So the circuit is expanding fast in **level** while flat-to-down in **intensity**. Singh's $13trn ÷ 2.0 implies ~$6.5trn of source collateral, roughly double the $3.7trn of end-2017 — consistent with hedge-fund AUM growth. The pipe is wider; the fluid is not moving faster.

**5. Does Singh claim this acts on asset prices? No.**

Zero occurrences of "asset price", "equity price" or "stock price" across WP/10/172, WP/11/256, WP/12/179, WP/17/113 and WP/19/106. What he does claim, in his own words:

- WP/11/256 §IV: a collateral shortage has "a negative cascading impact on lending similar to the impact on the money supply of a reduction in the monetary base", and "the first round impact on the **real economy**" comes from shrinking source-collateral pools. Direction: collateral contraction → less lending → less output. Deflationary for goods.
- WP/21/6 (Jan 2021) Annex: the formalisation is an augmented LM curve, M = f(Y, r, cp), where cp is collateral efficiency. Lower cp means "a larger change in benchmark interest rates (r) will be needed to produce the same change in output (Y)". The dependent variable is **output**.
- WP/11/256 Annex 1 (the Shin decomposition): collateral price y is an **exogenous input** to debt capacity. Causality runs asset prices → chain length, not chains → asset prices.
- QE: Singh's argument is that QE *removes* good collateral and *lowers* velocity (WP/17/113 p.12; WP/19/106 fn12). Under a collateral-circuit theory of ZIRP-era asset inflation, this is backwards — the circuit contracted 40% while the S&P doubled.
- WP/21/6 (Singh, Stella, Bhargava): the money/inflation breakdown is explained by payments technology and secularly declining demand for central-bank liabilities; QE is "essentially a debt management operation". No financial-circuit absorption story.

The single asset-price passage in the whole corpus is WP/21/94 §: pledgeability enters the equilibrium price of the **pledged asset itself** (Chen et al 2019; Caballero-Farhi safe assets; "effective supply is nominal supply times reuse rate"). That is a liquidity premium on collateral — mechanically, a lower yield on safe bonds. It feeds the discount-rate synthesis rather than displacing it.

**Cannot establish.** 1. **No current velocity ratio can be established, by anyone, including Singh.** The denominator — source collateral — is not observable from filings. It requires hedge-fund AUM times an assumed gross-leverage multiple (Singh used a "consensus estimate" of 1.75 in 2010, 40% strategy shares, 4/7 long-short adjustments) plus RMA securities-lending data behind a commercial licence. I reconstructed the numerator this session; I cannot reconstruct the denominator from primary sources. Singh's $13trn / 2.0 implies ~$6.5trn of source collateral at end-2025, versus $3.7trn at end-2017. That is arithmetic on his published ratio, not a measurement, and I am not treating it as one.

2. **The Jan-2026 Risk.net figures are paywalled and I read them through an automated fetch of the accessible portion, not the full article.** The $13trn, the "near 2.0 for the past decade", the >$200bn per-bank intraday float and the >50% US share are quoted as the article's claims. The JPM ~$1.8trn cross-check against the Q3-2025 10-Q ($1,829.9bn) is strong evidence the extraction is faithful, but I have not read the body.

3. **Whether measured velocity is flat because reuse is genuinely constrained, or because the measurement has stopped working, is undecidable on present evidence** — and that is Singh's own current position, not a gap I am filling. His Jan-2026 argument is that tokenised intraday reuse settles and unwinds inside the day and so never reaches the end-of-day footnote the whole method rests on. If that is right, the true reuse rate is unknown and unbounded above by 2.0. It cannot be tested until someone publishes intraday collateral-movement data, which no one does.

4. **The C-015 hypothesis that reuse migrated from dealer rehypothecation into CCP netting is not tested here and my data cannot test it.** CCP netting compresses gross volumes, which would depress Singh's numerator without any fall in economic reuse. My +21.8% numerator growth is consistent with either interpretation.

5. **Singh's silence on asset prices is evidence about Singh, not about the world.** He never tested an asset-price channel and never claimed one; his metric is silent on valuation by construction. "Undermines" here means the corpus does not carry the operator's mechanism and its ZIRP-era direction runs the wrong way — not that a collateral-to-asset-price channel has been shown absent. Nobody in this literature has looked.

6. **The 2007 "3.0" and the 2010 "4" are different metrics on different bases and the series was revised in 2019 without a documented revision policy.** Singh's WP/19/106 text says end-2017 was "an increase of 25 percent relative to end-2016"; his own Table 2 shows 6.1 to 7.5, which is 23.0%. That internal inconsistency in a published IMF table is a fair measure of the precision available here — roughly one significant figure.

# Collateral velocity: what it is actually doing now (FR2004 recomputation, dealer 10-K source collateral, and the CCP-netting migration hypothesis)

**Verdict: `undermines`**

**Headline.** The reuse INTENSITY is flat — on every measurement system with a defensible denominator — and the one micro-mechanism by which reuse would create spendable liquidity has now been directly tested and found absent. Singh's velocity has been near 2.0 for a decade; the ECB's transaction-level reuse rate is 11.63% with a standard deviation of 0.85%; the four US dealers that disclose both legs repledged 79.2% of received collateral in 2025 against 77.8% in 2019. What grew is the QUANTITY of collateral in the pipe (+23.9% in 2025), not the number of times each piece turns. The collateral chain got fatter, not longer — which is the opposite of what a financial-circuit multiplier story needs.

**Mechanism.** What actually does the work is quantity and valuation, not turnover. Decompose the +23.9% rise in six-firm source collateral (FY2024→FY2025) into: (i) reuse intensity, which contributed roughly nothing — the four-firm repledge share moved +0.6pp to 79.2%, the five-firm share moved −0.5pp to 81.4%; (ii) collateral supply, with federal debt held by the public +9.5% over calendar 2025 ($24,690.7bn 2025-01-01 → $27,041.1bn 2026-01-01); (iii) collateral fair value, since ASC 860-30-50-1A reports fair value and the S&P 500 rose 16.4% and the 10y yield fell 4.58%→4.18% over 2025, lifting the marked value of both equity and bond collateral without any change in securities transferred. The residual after (ii) and (iii) is genuine volume growth in intermediation, but it is not multiplicative — it is a wider pipe carrying a repriced stock. The chain length that a multiplier story requires (Singh ~2.0; ECB 2.95 all-bond / 3.05 government) has not moved. Crucially, ECB WP 3147 tested the specific channel by which reuse would generate spendable cash — the "liquidity windfall", a positive haircut wedge between the leg on which a dealer receives collateral and the leg on which it delivers — and found it absent on average, often negative, and most negative when collateral moves from bilateral into central clearing. That is the mechanism the financial-circuit thesis would need, measured directly, and it is not there. The one place a real mechanism survives is netting: DTCC reports $1.4trn of balance-sheet capacity released on $3.1trn of sponsored/agent buyside volume (31-Dec-2025), i.e. 45%. That relaxes dealer leverage-ratio constraints and permits more gross intermediation per unit of dealer equity — a capacity mechanism, not a money-creation mechanism, and it shows up as a shrinking FR2004 numerator rather than a rising velocity.

## Analysis

**Route 2 first, because it is the one the register asked me to check, and it holds exactly.**

I rebuilt the Singh footnote measure from primary filings, not from the prior lens's write-up. Using the SEC XBRL company-concept API against the six FY2025 10-Ks (all filed Feb 2026, balance-sheet date 31 Dec 2025), the ASC 860-30-50-1A(c)(1) disclosure — collateral received that the firm is permitted to sell or repledge — totals: JPM $1,771.0bn, BAC $1,100.0bn, Citi $1,064.0bn, WFC $469.2bn, GS $1,312.079bn, MS $1,190.694bn = **$6,906.97bn**. FY2024: $5,574.57bn. Growth **+23.90%**. The register's $6,907bn / $5,574bn / +23.9% is confirmed to the dollar, independently, from the filings themselves. The seven-year series (n=6 firms every year): 2019 $4,037bn; 2020 $4,634bn (+14.8%); 2021 $4,831bn (+4.3%); 2022 $4,646bn (−3.8%); 2023 $4,988bn (+7.4%); 2024 $5,575bn (+11.8%); 2025 $6,907bn (+23.9%). 2025 is the fastest year in the series.

**But the same filings disclose the second leg, and it is the second leg that kills the velocity story.** ASC 860-30-50-1A(c)(2) — the portion actually sold or repledged — gives a reuse *intensity*. For the four firms tagging both legs consistently (JPM, WFC, GS, MS): 77.8% (2019), 73.7%, 75.9%, 76.4%, 76.8%, 78.6%, **79.2% (2025)**. Six years, +1.4pp. Add BAC (which discloses both) and the five-firm ratio goes 81.9% (2024) → 81.4% (2025) — *down*. GS fell 87.9%→85.3%; MS 77.6%→75.6%; BAC 95.3%→90.9%; JPM and WFC rose. There is no acceleration in reuse intensity anywhere in this data. A 23.9% rise in source collateral against a flat repledge share means the dealers took in $1.33trn more collateral and pushed roughly the same fraction of it back out.

**Route 1: I actually computed it.** The Eastern Economic Journal paper is Michl & Park, "Shadow Banks and the Collateral Multiplier", EEJ 49(2) 156–175 (2023; UMass WP 2021-12). Their formula is c = (R_T + R_M)/R_T, where trading-book repo R_T = total outgoing Treasury repo minus total incoming Treasury reverse repo, and matched book R_M is the incoming leg. I pulled the full FR2004 panel from the NY Fed markets API (1,539 series) and computed it weekly through **2026-08-12**. Treasury ex-TIPS, annual means: 2015 11.10; 2016 12.67; 2019 7.57; 2020 7.38; 2021 7.95; 2023 13.74; 2024 10.85; **2025 10.85 (n=7 weeks); 2026 10.16 (n=6 weeks)**; latest single observation 2026-08-12 = **11.68** (repo out $3,001bn, reverse repo in $2,744bn, residual trading book $257bn).

Three honest caveats on this. (a) I could not reconcile my recomputation with their published statement of "up to five times" over Jan-2015–May-2021 — my implementation of their stated formula gives 7.4–12.7 by year over that window. I state the discrepancy rather than paper over it. (b) The measure is arithmetically fragile: the denominator is a small residual of two large numbers, so a 5% error in either leg swings c by 50%. (c) Substituting the *actual* dealer net outright Treasury position (PDPOSGST-TOT) for the residual gives a multiplier that falls monotonically — 40.6× (2015) → 7.4× (2019) → **6.36× (2026)** — because dealer net Treasury inventory ballooned from $42bn (2015 mean) to $496bn (2026 mean). Two defensible denominators, opposite trends. The level of the Michl-Park multiplier is not robust; I would not put it in the register as a number.

**What I would put in the register is a measurement finding.** The NY Fed's own Treasury repo total is going dark. PDSORA-UTSETTOT was suppressed ("*") in 0 of 52 weeks in every year 2013–2021, then 15/52 (2022), 16/52 (2023), 21/52 (2024), **33/53 (2025)**, 17/32 (2026 YTD). I verified that summing the 24 venue×tenor components reproduces the published total exactly (max deviation 0.000%) when all components are present — but zero suppressed weeks are reconstructable, because the components are suppressed more often than the total. The public weekly series that would let an outsider audit dealer collateral intermediation is now redacted in a majority of weeks, exactly as the phenomenon grows.

**Route 3: the migration hypothesis is real but is not a velocity story.** The revised FR2004 breaks repo out by venue, including sponsored. Dealer-reported sponsored Treasury repo (cleared-bilateral-sponsored plus triparty-sponsored, securities out) went from $179bn / 7.4% of dealer Treasury repo (2024Q3) to $428bn / **13.3%** (2026Q2), $335bn / 11.0% in 2026Q3 (n=3 weeks). Sponsored reverse repo in has been $362bn→$467bn, a stable 16–19%. Separately, DTCC states Sponsored and Agent Clearing buyside volumes peaked at $3.1trn on 31 Dec 2025, "delivering almost USD1.4 trillion in balance sheet capacity" (DTCC 2026 outlook, Jan 2026). That $1.4trn — 45% of gross buyside volume — is netting benefit, i.e. intermediation that by construction does *not* appear on the dealer balance sheets FR2004 measures. It is roughly half the size of the entire dealer-reported Treasury repo book. So yes: measured gross dealer repo understates gross economic intermediation, and the understatement is growing. But netting removes *balance sheet*, not collateral chains; it is not evidence that reuse intensity rose. It is evidence that the FR2004-based multiplier's numerator is being deflated by an accounting mechanism, which is a reason to distrust route 1, not a reason to believe velocity is secretly high.

**The counter-evidence is not a counter — it corroborates.** ECB WP 3147 (Angelis Alexiou, Pereira, Rodrigues-Gomes, Nov 2025), SFTDS transaction-level, Jan-2021–Feb-2025, ~15m repo trades: reuse rate mean **11.63%, SD 0.85%**, min 9.18% (Jan-2021), max 15.25% (Nov-2021); reused collateral averaging **EUR 49bn/day**. Total daily collateral delivered grew EUR 307bn → ~495bn (+61%) while reused collateral grew EUR 35bn → 58bn (+66%) — the same decomposition as the US 10-Ks: volume up sharply, rate flat. Average chain length **2.95** (all bonds), **3.05** (governments only), "consistently above 2.9" and never below 2.7. They explicitly map this to Singh: he had ~3 pre-Lehman falling to 2.4 by end-2010; they conclude chains have rebounded to pre-crisis levels. And their direct test of the mechanism — the haircut wedge that would hand a dealer free cash for standing in the middle — finds it absent on average, often negative, and **most negative when a position moves from bilateral into central clearing**. That is a targeted empirical refutation of the micro-mechanism a financial-circuit story would need.

**Singh's own current number.** Writing in Risk.net on 12 January 2026, Singh puts collateral velocity "near 2.0 for the past decade", global pledged collateral at roughly $13trn with US banks over half, and JPM and Barclays at around $1.8trn each of pledged collateral for reuse. His JPM figure matches my verified $1.771trn; my verified US-six total is 53.1% of his $13trn. His figures cross-validate mine. His central argument is that tokenised intraday repo opens and closes within the day and never reaches end-of-day filings, so the metric is degrading — the same direction as the FR2004 suppression I measured.

**Bottom line for the register.** "Collateral velocity has been flat 2-3 years on Basel III constraints" should be replaced by: *reuse intensity has been flat for roughly a decade, at ~2.0 on Singh's ratio (his own estimate, Jan-2026) and 11.63% ±0.85pp with chain length ~3.0 on ECB transaction-level euro-area data through Feb-2025; the six largest US dealers' repledge share moved from 77.8% (FY2019) to 79.2% (FY2025). What changed in 2025 is quantity, not turnover: source collateral at those six rose 23.9% to $6,907bn (31-Dec-2025). Attribute the flatness to no single cause — Basel III is unsourced — and note that all three measurement systems are degrading.*

For the project's central question this **undermines** the collateral leg of the financial-circuit thesis. The circuit is not spinning faster. It is carrying more, and much of what it carries is the market value of the very assets whose inflation is to be explained.

**Cannot establish.** 1. SINGH'S VELOCITY RATIO CANNOT BE INDEPENDENTLY RECONSTRUCTED FOR 2025. I verified his numerator (source collateral at the largest dealers) to the dollar for the US six. # QE, collateral scarcity and the ZIRP puzzle (2009–2019): the historical test case for a collateral / financial-circuit mechanism

**Verdict: `undermines`**

**Headline.** The collateral circuit ran backwards during the ZIRP asset boom. Between end-2007 and end-2016 Singh's pledged-collateral market contracted 39% ($10.0trn to $6.1trn) and its reuse rate fell 40% (3.0 to 1.8) — while the S&P 500 rose 52%. And US Treasury collateral was never scarce in aggregate: privately-held marketable Treasuries, net of everything the Fed owned, rose 170% from $5.30trn (end-2008) to $14.33trn (end-2019). Worse for the operator's own framing: Singh's argument, correctly stated, is a reason QE did LESS than the balance sheet suggests, not more.

**Mechanism.** What actually does the work in 2009-2019 is corporate cash flow and its distribution, not a monetary or collateral circuit. Trailing S&P 500 as-reported EPS rose 173.6% against a 186.1% price rise (Dec-2009 to Dec-2019, Shiller), so on a trailing basis the multiple was essentially unchanged (21.8 to 22.8). On the cyclically-adjusted basis roughly 54% of the real price rise is real smoothed-earnings growth and 46% multiple expansion. Greenwald-Lettau-Ludvigson attribute 40% of 1989-2017 real equity growth to reallocation of rewards from labour to shareholders versus 14% to lower interest rates; Farhi-Gourio find the equity premium ROSE 2.18pp while the risk-free rate fell 3.14pp, so the equity discount rate moved only ~1pp. The collateral channel, insofar as it operated, worked in the opposite direction to the thesis: Singh's own aggregate of money-plus-collateral "financial lubrication" was ~$30trn pre-Lehman and ~$30trn at end-2012, flat across the period when the S&P recovered, with the mix shifting to ~80% money, which he argues has LOWER velocity. If a collateral-circuit mechanism is running now, 2009-2019 is not the precedent for it — the observable in that window moved the wrong way.

## Analysis

**1. Singh's argument, established properly (and it points the other way)**

The argument is real and I verified it in the primary papers, not summaries. Singh defines collateral velocity as the volume of secured transactions received by large dealer banks divided by the stock of source collateral (WP/17/113, p.13; WP/13/186 fn.8). QE removes high-quality collateral into central bank balance sheets where, in his phrase, siloed collateral has zero velocity by definition (WP/13/186, fn.5, Aug-2013). His two channels are explicit: QE and regulation constrain collateral flow by (i) reducing dealer balance-sheet space as reserves sit on it, and (ii) reducing the reuse rate, because good collateral is what prices and moves other collateral (WP/17/113, p.13, May-2017).

Crucially, Singh's Box 1 (WP/13/186, p.6) makes the netting claim directly: total "financial lubrication" (US+EA+UK M2 plus bank collateral) was over $30trn pre-Lehman, one-third of it pledged collateral; the post-crisis decline in collateral and its reuse was an estimated $4–5trn; and by end-2012 lubrication had rebounded to approximately $30trn but the mix was roughly 80% money — which has lower velocity and much of which sits as excess reserves. He states plainly that the increase in M2 due to QE does not substitute for the loss in financial collateral.

Read carefully, this is an argument that **QE's net liquidity effect was smaller than the headline**, and that the money-and-collateral aggregate went roughly nowhere from 2007 to 2012 while equities recovered. It is not an argument that a collateral circuit inflated asset prices. If the operator adopts Singh, he must also adopt the deflationary half.

**2. The test: the circuit contracted while assets inflated**

The velocity series, from three IMF papers with different vintages, is consistent and unambiguous:

Set that against equities. Dec-2007 to Dec-2016: pledged collateral volume −39%, velocity −40%; S&P 500 +51.9% (1479.2 to 2246.6). Sharper still, Dec-2007 to Dec-2015 the CAPE was *identical* at 26.0 while velocity fell from 3.0 to 1.9. The correlation fails in level and in change. A mechanism that shrinks by 40% cannot be the proximate cause of an asset boom over the same window. (All equity figures: Shiller ie_data.xls, file vintage Sep-2024.)

**3. The aggregate scarcity premise is false for the US**

QE removed collateral; Treasury supplied vastly more. Marketable Treasury debt held by the public went from $5,774.2bn (31-Dec-2008) to $16,657.9bn (31-Dec-2019) (Treasury MSPD Table 1, fiscaldata API, retrieved this session). SOMA Treasury holdings went from $475.9bn to $2,328.9bn (NY Fed SOMA summary API, same basis, par). Net private float: **$5,298bn to $14,329bn, +170%**. The Fed's share of marketable debt held by the public was 8.2% (2008), peaked at 19.7% (2014), and was 14.0% (2019) — higher than pre-crisis, but the denominator tripled.

Scarcity was real but *specific*, not aggregate: on-the-run tenors, and above all the euro area, where the Eurosystem bought into a shrinking net-issuance pool. The best-identified estimate: buying 1% of a bond's outstanding amount lowers its special repo rate by 0.78bp, with an IV estimate up to six times higher (Arrata, Nguyen, Rahmouni-Rousseau, Vari, IMF WP/18/258 Dec-2018, p.22; published JFE 137(3), 2020). That is a repo-market price effect measured in single-digit basis points per 1% of a bond. It is not an equity-market mechanism.

**4. Relative magnitudes for 2009–2019 — the assigned question**

*(c) Collateral channel: wrong sign, effectively zero as an inflationary force.* See above.

*(a) Discount rate: real, but bounded, and mostly not QE.* The Fed's own preferred estimate is that its holdings depressed the 10-year term premium by 100bp at end-2016, projected to 85bp at end-2017 (Bonis, Ihrig, Wei, FEDS Notes, 20-Apr-2017, Li-Wei preferred-habitat model). Greenlaw, Hamilton, Harris and West (USMPF, Feb-2018) argue that consensus overstates it and that initial effects tended not to persist. Translating even the Fed's own 100bp into equities using the best ZLB-period elasticity — a 25bp unconventional-policy surprise fall in the 10-year yield raises stock prices 0.7% (Rogers, Scotti, Wright, Fed IFDP 1101, Mar-2014; Economic Policy 29(80)) — gives roughly 2.8% on the S&P 500. Against a 186% price rise. Note the same paper finds pre-ZLB the equivalent move was worth ~5%: the equity transmission of rate moves was *weaker*, not stronger, under ZIRP.

*(b) Portfolio composition: where the identified evidence actually is, but narrower than advertised.* Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen (BPEA Fall 2011) explicitly reject the pure duration/portfolio-balance channel on cross-asset evidence — Agencies fell most (10y −199bp vs Treasuries −107bp), and CDS-adjusted Baa yields barely moved, patterns duration cannot generate. What survives is a *safety* channel and a *signalling* channel. Safety is a Treasury-market phenomenon: the convenience yield averages 73bp, 1926–2008 (KVJ, JPE 120(2), 2012). Carpenter, Demiralp, Ihrig and Klee (FEDS 2013-32; JBF 2015) do find the Fed bought chiefly from "households" — a Financial Accounts residual that includes hedge funds — and that these sellers rebalanced toward riskier assets. That is genuine portfolio-rebalancing evidence, and it is the strongest leg the operator has. But it is a flow-composition result, not a magnitude for index levels.

*(d) Broad money: an identity, not yet a mechanism.* M2/NGDP rising from 49.8% to 70.0% is arithmetically the same statement as M2 velocity falling from 2.015 (2006Q2) to 1.435 (2019Q4), −28.8% (FRED M2V, retrieved this session). It restates the fact; it does not supply a channel. Three constraints: (i) M2 rose $6.84trn over 2009–2019 while the Fed's balance sheet rose $1.93trn, so QE can account for at most ~28% of it — bank credit did the rest; (ii) at the ZLB with IOER paid, money and bills are near-perfect substitutes, which is precisely the condition under which open-market operations are irrelevant except through expectations (Eggertsson–Woodford, BPEA 2003, in the spirit of Wallace 1981) — the hot-potato is defused; (iii) option (d) only becomes causal *through* option (b). Money and portfolio composition are not competing explanations; (d) without (b) is bookkeeping.

**5. What actually did the work**

Earnings. Dec-2009 to Dec-2019 the S&P 500 price rose 186.1% and trailing as-reported EPS rose 173.6%; the trailing P/E went 21.8 to 22.8. On the cleaner cyclically-adjusted basis, real price +140.4% splits (log shares) into ~54% real smoothed-earnings growth and ~46% multiple expansion (CAPE 20.3 to 30.3). Of that multiple expansion, a *deliberately generous* arithmetic illustration — full pass-through of the 133bp fall in the 10-year TIPS yield with the equity risk premium and growth held constant — takes CAPE only to 27.8 against an actual 30.3, i.e. about three-quarters of the multiple move, hence at most ~35% of the real price rise. Treat that as identity arithmetic under a stated assumption, not an estimate.

And the assumption is contested by the best structural work. Farhi and Gourio (NBER WP 25282, rev. Feb-2019; BPEA Fall 2018) find that between 1984–2000 and 2001–2016 the risk-free rate fell 3.14pp (2.79% to −0.35%) but the **equity premium rose 2.18pp** (3.07% to 5.25%), so the equity discount rate fell only 0.96pp. Greenwald, Lettau and Ludvigson (JPE 133(4), Apr-2025) decompose the 7.2% annual real per-capita equity growth of 1989–2017: 40% reallocation of rewards to shareholders at labour's expense, 25% output growth, 21% lower risk price, **14% lower interest rates**.

That is the answer. Ranked for 2009–2019: cash flows and factor shares first by a wide margin; discount rate second and bounded; portfolio composition third and narrow; broad money a restatement of the second and third; collateral channel wrong-signed. This does not overturn the prior synthesis so much as sharpen it — the illusion is not even primarily a discount-rate artefact. It is mostly an earnings and profit-share fact.

**Cannot establish.** 1. **Singh's velocity metric is not a measured market aggregate.** It is built from footnote disclosures of 10–18 dealer banks' annual reports (WP/17/113 Annex II). Singh himself writes it "is not intended as an exact metric" (WP/17/113, p.13). It cannot see CCP-netted reuse, non-bank-to-non-bank chains, or non-centrally-cleared bilateral repo. A falling number is therefore consistent with *migration* of reuse rather than *contraction* of it — which is exactly register entry C-015. My finding that the metric fell 3.0→1.8 over 2007–2016 does not establish that collateral reuse in the economy fell; it establishes that reuse *through dealer rehypothecation* fell. The distinction is load-bearing and I cannot close it.

2. **No study identifies the causal effect of QE on the decade-level of equity prices.** Everything available is either an announcement-window elasticity (Rogers-Scotti-Wright, KVJ) or a structural model with strong priors (Li-Wei via Bonis-Ihrig-Wei). My ~2.8% figure is an extrapolation of a high-frequency elasticity to a cumulative term-premium estimate and must not be read as "QE added 2.8% to the S&P 500." It is an order-of-magnitude bound under the assumption that announcement-window elasticities generalise — which Greenlaw-Hamilton-Harris-West (2018) dispute in both directions.

3. **The QE share of the 2009–2019 real-rate decline is not decomposable with what I have.** Real 10-year yields fell 133bp; the Fed's own model attributes ~100bp of term-premium compression to its holdings at the 2016 peak, but term premium is not the same object as the real yield, r* fell globally over the same period, and the two cannot be additively separated from public sources.

4. **Base-date sensitivity is severe and I have not resolved it.** The earnings-versus-multiple split of the 2009–2019 equity rise ranges from ~98% earnings (trailing P/E, Dec-2009 base) to ~54% earnings (CAPE, Dec-2009 base) to substantially less on a Dec-2008 or Mar-2009 base, where trailing earnings were crisis-distorted (Dec-2008 EPS $14.88; Mar-2009 EPS $6.86). I have reported the range rather than picking one, but a reader choosing a different base date will get a different answer and both will be defensible.

5. **I cannot rule out risk-taking channels invisible to announcement windows.** Reach-for-yield effects (Rajan 2005; Hanson-Stein 2015; Di Maggio-Kacperczyk 2017) are documented qualitatively and in specific sectors (money funds, insurers) but I found no credible aggregate magnitude for their contribution to index-level equity valuation, and I did not manufacture one.

6. **Hanson-Stein's 42bp figure is unverified at source.** I could not retrieve the JFE PDF; the figure comes from the paper's abstract as reproduced across multiple secondary pages. Treat as secondary until someone opens the paper.

7. **The euro-area case is genuinely different and I have only sampled it.** Aggregate Treasury collateral abundance in the US does not travel to the euro area, where Bund scarcity and deeply negative special repo rates were real. My verdict is about the US test case; a euro-area-specific answer could differ and would need its own pass.

# The disaggregated quantity theory of credit (Werner 1997/2003/2005) and its modern successors: Bezemer–Ryan-Collins–van Lerven–Zhang, Bezemer–Zhang, Jordà–Schularick–Taylor, Turner — tested against US H.8, FINRA and Z.1 data for the year to July 2026.

**Verdict: `supports_with_qualification`**

**Headline.** The half of Werner's theory everyone cites for asset bubbles is the half that was never econometrically tested. Every replicated result in this literature — Japan, UK, Czech Republic, Spain, cointegration, Granger, general-to-specific — tests credit-for-GDP-transactions against nominal GDP. The asset-price leg (ΔC_F → Δ(P_F·Q_F)) is carried in Werner's own presentations by a two-line co-movement chart of Japanese real-estate lending against land prices, not by a reported coefficient. Worse for the operator: Werner's own stability diagnostic FAILS for the US. Real-circuit bank credit divided by nominal GDP — the ratio his theory says should be stationary — has fallen 17.2% since 2019Q1 (0.391 → 0.324, 2026Q2), and 19.4% on the proxy that excludes real estate. In his framework that means either the proxy is wrong or the mechanism does not hold here. And Mian–Sufi–Verner (JF 2020) found, with identification, that credit supply expansion DOES raise the price of nontradable goods — the wall between the two circuits leaks.

**Mechanism.** STATED PRECISELY ENOUGH TO LOOK FOR: A bank extends credit to a nondepository financial institution (H.8 line "loans to nondepository financial institutions", inside "other loans and leases"; FFIEC Call Report Schedule RC-C item 9). The loan creates a deposit whose first use is the purchase of an ALREADY-EXISTING financial asset. At settlement the deposit transfers from buyer to seller and remains inside the banking system; no claim is placed on current output, so no goods price is bid. The seller's deposit is then available for a further asset purchase. In Werner's notation this is ΔC_F, and it enters Δ(P_F·Q_F) = V_F·ΔC_F rather than Δ(P_R·Y) = V_R·ΔC_R.

THE OBSERVABLE SIGNATURE, all three of which are present in the US to July 2026:
(i) NDFI lending and margin debt growing multiples of nominal GDP growth — margin debt +38.6% y/y, NDFI +19.75% y/y (operator's), nGDP +6.53%;
(ii) the ratio of real-circuit bank credit to nominal GDP falling — 0.391 (2019Q1) to 0.324 (2026Q2);
(iii) asset prices rising with no CPI response — CPI +3.3%.

WHAT ACTUALLY DOES THE WORK, to the extent it is not this: three things compete, and the honest ranking today is (a) internal corporate funds, which the operator has already established fund 100% of capex with a surplus lent onward — this is not credit at all and needs no monetary explanation; (b) discount rates, which the prior synthesis identified and which the modest Favara–Imbs elasticity (0.12 impact, 0.2 peak) is consistent with; (c) the credit circuit, which on the only quantification I can construct bounds at roughly 11% of the past year's US equity market-cap gain even at the full Gabaix–Koijen multiplier. The circuit is real, it is measurable, and it is not yet shown to be the dominant term.

WHERE WERNER IS STRUCTURALLY MIS-SPECIFIED FOR 2026, and where it can be repaired: his theory centres BANK credit creation. The operator's evidence says the marginal balance sheet is a non-bank's. But the NDFI line reconciles this: banks still create the deposits; they have simply moved one step back in the chain, funding the entity that bids for the asset rather than bidding themselves. The modern C_F is not "bank lending against securities" — it is bank lending TO the leveraged buyer of securities. That is the version of the theory worth testing.

## Analysis

**1. What the theory actually says, precisely.**

Werner's move (1992, 1997 Kredit und Kapital 30(2):276–309) is to split the quantity equation by the *use* of credit rather than by the holder of money. Total credit creation C = C_R + C_F, and the two halves obey separate equations: Δ(P_R·Y) = V_R·ΔC_R for the real economy, and Δ(P_F·Q_F) = V_F·ΔC_F for asset markets (verified verbatim from Werner's own OeNB slide deck, "Scientific Macroeconomics & The Quantity Theory of Credit", © 2018). The claimed payoff is that three 1980s anomalies dissolve at once: apparent velocity decline, asset bubbles without CPI inflation, and Japanese capital outflow. This is *exactly* the operator's question, formalised thirty years early, and the operator's counterparty at a Japanese mega bank will recognise the canonical case.

**2. The empirical record, honestly separated.**

The C_R → nGDP leg is genuinely tested and independently replicated. Werner (1997, Japan 1981–95), Voutsinas & Werner (2011, Japan 1984–2008), Werner (2012, Japan 1984–2001), Lyonnet & Werner (2012, UK 1995–2010), Ryan-Collins, Werner & Castle (2016, UK 1965–2012), Bezemer & Werner (2009, Czech Republic), Werner (2014, Spain 1997–2013). Method is Hendry–Mizon general-to-specific reduction: short and long rates, M2+CD or M4, ten-year yields, exchange rates, central-bank asset purchases and a "qualitative easing" term are all eliminated; the disaggregated credit proxy survives. Cointegration holds, Granger causality is unidirectional from credit to nGDP at 1%, and the proxies test strongly exogenous. The implied inverse velocities C_R/nGDP are 0.432–0.709 across seven studies, mean 0.585 (all as tabulated in Clavero 2017, MPRA 76657, Table 3).

The C_F → asset-price leg has no comparable record I could locate. I searched Werner's own publication site, his OeNB and Post-Keynesian presentations, and the Manchester School 2011 note; the asset-side evidence presented is a chart of Japanese real-estate credit growth against nationwide residential land price growth, 1971–2001. The 1997 Kredit und Kapital paper is paywalled and I could not verify whether it contains a land-price regression. **A structurer should be told this asymmetry before being told anything else.**

**3. The methodological objection that matters.**

Clavero (2017) — sympathetic, not hostile — makes the decisive point: C_R is not observable. Werner built the original proxy as bank loans to a hand-picked set of sectors, excluding real estate (7.15% of business lending), without justification; Clavero notes Werner "does not offer a justification; nor do the other authors, apart from mild allusions" (his phrasing). Because the correct proxy is unknown, testing proceeds by "triangulation" — theory-informed search plus empirical refinement — which renders the theory, in his words, close to irrefutable in the strict sense. My own US construction demonstrates the force of this: including real-estate loans in C_R gives an inverse velocity of 0.324 in 2026Q2; excluding them gives 0.146. Same theory, same country, same quarter, factor of 2.2. Note also that Ryan-Collins/Werner/Castle *include* mortgage lending in C_R on wealth-effect grounds — which concedes that asset-directed credit leaks into GDP transactions, dissolving the clean partition the theory rests on.

**4. The successors, which have the panel data Werner lacked.**

Bezemer, Ryan-Collins, van Lerven & Zhang (Socio-Economic Review 21(1) 2023: 437–478) — 17 advanced economies, 1973–2005, JST macro-history database, Pedroni and Westerlund panel cointegration. Credit/GDP roughly 60%→100%; the non-financial-firm share of bank credit roughly 60%→40%. Every credit-policy liberalisation index (credit controls, interest-rate controls, financial-account restrictions, bank privatisation, entry barriers) cointegrates with the falling business share at p<0.007 or better. Note carefully what this establishes: **policy → allocation**, not allocation → asset prices. It documents the debt shift; it does not price it.

Jordà, Schularick & Taylor ("The Great Mortgaging", Economic Policy 31(85), 2016): mortgages rose from ~30% of bank lending in 1900 to ~60% by 2014 across 17 advanced economies. Bezemer, Grydaki & Zhang (Economic Inquiry 54(1), 2016): 46 economies 1990–2011, positive growth effects for credit flows to non-financial business, none for mortgage or other asset-market credit flows. Bezemer & Zhang (2019): the composition of credit, more than its level, predicts post-crisis recession depth. Turner (*Between Debt and the Devil*, 2015) reports that credit financing non-real-estate investment is no more than ~14% of the UK total (secondary attribution; I did not verify against the book).

The only *identified* causal estimate of credit → asset prices in this space is Favara & Imbs (AER 105(3), 2015), instrumenting mortgage supply with US interstate branching deregulations 1994–2005. Impact elasticity of house-price growth to instrumented credit growth: **0.12**, remarkably stable across specifications; peak at two years: **0.2**; effect insignificant after five to six years. Deregulation-induced credit explains virtually all US house-price growth to 2002 and one-third to one-half of 2002–2005 (verified from the authors' May 2014 working-paper text). That is a real result — and it is an order of magnitude smaller per dollar than the Gabaix–Koijen equity multiplier the operator already has.

**5. Against the theory: goods prices do respond.**

Mian, Sufi & Verner (Journal of Finance 75(2), 2020, "How Does Credit Supply Expansion Affect the Real Economy?") find, using the US 1980s Garn–St Germain natural experiment plus a 56-country panel, that credit supply expansion boosts nontradable-sector employment **and the price of nontradable goods**, with limited tradable effects. The household-demand channel dominates the productive-capacity channel. This is a direct, top-journal, identified counterexample to the strong form of the Werner claim. It does not refute the weak form (credit for *pure asset transfer* need not touch CPI), but it shows the boundary is porous exactly where the operator would want it sharp.

**6. Can the measure be built for the US today? Yes, partially — and I built it.**

From H.8 via FRED (verified this session, vintage 2026-08-05 release, monthly SA to 2026-07): total bank credit grew $1,154.1bn in the year to July 2026, +6.22%. Decomposition of that marginal dollar: **"other loans and leases" +$472.3bn (40.9%)** — the line containing the operator's NDFI series, which stands at $2,006bn; securities holdings +$232.1bn (20.1%); C&I +$223.9bn (19.4%); commercial real estate +$97.9bn (8.5%); consumer +$86.9bn (7.5%); residential real estate +$40.9bn (3.5%). **61.0% of marginal US bank credit growth went to categories that are neither households nor non-financial businesses**, against 46.2% of the stock. The stock ratio C_F/C_R (securities plus other loans, over household-plus-NFC lending) has gone 0.596 (2019Q1) → 0.849 (2026Q2), the highest reading in the H.8 record.

FINRA margin statistics (primary file, downloaded and parsed this session): debit balances $1,417,225m July 2026 versus $1,022,548m July 2025, **+38.6% y/y**, against nominal GDP +6.53% (2026Q2) and CPI +3.3% (July 2026). March-to-March the increase was $340.6bn (+38.7%).

**7. The magnitude that disciplines the enthusiasm.**

Z.1 market value of domestic corporate equities: $91.1trn (2025Q1) → $106.9trn (2026Q1), +$15.8trn. Applying the operator's Gabaix–Koijen ~5× multiplier to the full $340.6bn March-to-March margin-debt increase gives ~$1.70trn, roughly **10.8% of the year's market-cap gain**. That is an upper bound and it is generous: margin debt is only the portion of levered equity buying that is measured, but the multiplier applies to *net flow into equities*, and not all margin extension is net new buying. On the housing side the case is weaker still: net home mortgage debt rose $373bn in the year to 2026Q1 (+2.77%) against residential fixed investment running at $1,186bn SAAR (2026Q2) — US net mortgage credit creation is currently smaller than new residential construction. There is no US housing credit circuit at present; whatever is running is a securities-and-NDFI circuit.

**8. Where this leaves the prior synthesis.**

"Discount-rate artefact, not monetary" needs qualifying, not overturning. The composition evidence is strong, constructible and pointing the same way as the operator's other five findings. The *pricing* evidence is thin: one identified elasticity of 0.12–0.2 for housing in 1994–2005, nothing for equities, and a top-journal result that credit expansion does move goods prices. The Werner framework earns its place because it supplies the right measurement — split credit by the use of the deposit created — not because it supplies a verified effect size.

**Cannot establish.** 1. **Werner's asset-price coefficient does not exist in accessible form.** I could not locate a published regression of asset prices on non-GDP credit with reported coefficients, standard errors and diagnostics anywhere in Werner's accessible output. The 1997 Kredit und Kapital paper (30(2):276-309, DOI 10.3790/ccm.30.2.276) is paywalled at Duncker & Humblot and I did not read it. It may contain such an equation. Until someone reads it, the honest statement is: the asset leg is supported by co-movement charts, the GDP leg by cointegration and Granger tests.

2. **H.8 does not record the USE of a loan, and no US source does.** Werner's C_R/C_F split requires knowing what the borrower bought. No US public series answers that. The nearest available splits, none of which is sufficient: FFIEC Call Report Schedule RC-C separates construction and land development from other real estate (use-adjacent); HMDA separates purchase / refinance / home improvement but does NOT flag new-build versus existing property; H.8 has no purpose field at all. The Census/NAR new-versus-existing ratio (13.2% of units) is a transactions ratio, not a credit ratio, and cannot be applied to loan flows without an assumption I decline to make.

3. **The securities line is genuinely ambiguous and I have not resolved it.** Bank purchases of Treasuries at primary auction finance government spending, which is a GDP transaction in Werner's own taxonomy; secondary-market purchases are asset transactions. The public data does not split them. So the 20.1% securities share of marginal credit growth could belong in either circuit, and the honest headline number is the 40.9% "other loans and leases" figure alone, not the 61.0% combination.

4. **Securities-based lending is not publicly aggregated at all.** Non-purpose loans against securities portfolios — the wirehouse/private-bank product that is the natural modern C_F for equities — has no published aggregate. FINRA margin debt covers only Reg-T margin at broker-dealers. This is a material and growing hole in any US construction, and its size is unknown to me.

5. **The counterparty composition of NDFI lending is not published.** H.8 gives one number. Whether the $2,006bn is funding hedge funds taking directional equity risk, market-neutral basis trades, mortgage REITs, BDCs, private-credit warehouses or broker-dealer inventory is unknowable from public data, and the distinction is decisive: only the first is C_F in Werner's sense. FR Y-14 and Y-9C schedules get closer but are not public at counterparty granularity.

6. **Nothing in my US-2026 construction is causally identified.** Every US number I computed is composition or co-movement. The one identified estimate in this literature (Favara-Imbs, 0.12/0.2) is for housing, for 1994-2005, and there is no basis for transferring it to equities or to 2026.

7. **Gross versus net credit flow is unsettled in the theory itself.** Werner's ΔC is the change in the credit stock (net), but the mechanism he describes — a created deposit being spent — is a gross-origination phenomenon. US net home mortgage debt grew $373bn while gross originations ran several times that. Which is the right measure is not resolved in the literature, and the answer changes the housing result materially.

8. **H.8 has structural breaks I did not adjust for.** FAS 166/167 consolidation of securitisation vehicles added a large step to bank credit in 2010Q1, and the 2020-21 reserve/deposit surge inflated securities holdings. My 2010Q1 and 2022Q1 readings of C_R/nGDP and C_F/C_R should be treated as break-contaminated. The 2019Q1-to-2026Q2 comparison, which is the one I lean on, spans no known break but does span the pandemic.

9. **Werner's non-QTC output.** I deliberately did not assess it. His single-bank credit-creation test (Werner 2014, IRFA, Raiffeisenbank Wildenberg) and his later public positions are separable from the disaggregation result and were out of scope. A reader should be told they are separable, not that they are sound.

# Two-price and circuit theories: Minsky's two-price system, Graziani's monetary circuit (finance vs funding), Keynes's finance motive and revolving fund, Tobin's q as the bridge, Borio & Lowe on asset prices and financial imbalances, Krippner on financialisation measurement — and the load-bearing objection: is a financial circuit that does not leak into goods markets coherent?

**Verdict: `undermines`**

**Headline.** The leak is not small. It is roughly 3.8% of PCE at run-rate, and it is visible in the national accounts: the US personal saving rate was 2.7% in June 2026, the third-lowest of 810 monthly observations since 1959 — the only lower readings are 2005-07, the previous asset boom. The circuit does not seal; it drains, and where it drains prices did rise (core services +30.3% since Dec-2019 against core goods +16.2%). Worse for the thesis, the canonical BIS statement of the operator's observation (Borio & Lowe 2002) attributes the goods/asset divergence to supply-side shocks, credibility-induced wage-price stickiness and a non-reacting policy rule — not to segmented monetary circuits. And Minsky's two-price system, correctly read, predicts the leak rather than sealing it: the P_K/P_I gap IS the transmission channel into goods markets, via investment. Exactly one circuit-shaped mechanism survives, and it is narrow: leverage created against, and extinguished on, existing collateral.

**Mechanism.** What actually does the work is three things, none of them a sealed circuit.

(i) MOST "ASSET INFLATION" INVOLVES NO MONEY AT ALL. Of the $31.40trn rise in US household net worth 2023-25, $31.40trn×80.2% was net holding gains — a revaluation of a stock that overwhelmingly did not change hands. Fagereng et al's "saving by holding" is the micro statement of this. A revaluation requires no monetary transaction, so there is nothing to leak and no circuit is needed. The prior synthesis's "discount-rate artefact" survives this lens largely intact.

(ii) THE LEAK THAT DOES OCCUR IS MIS-ADDRESSED, NOT ABSENT. At a 3.2c/$/yr MPC the equity-linked gains imply ~$841bn/yr of extra consumption (3.79% of PCE). It shows up as a 2.7% personal saving rate and a top-decile spending share of 49.2%. It lands in non-tradable services (CRNS find zero tradable-employment response), where prices did rise faster: core services +30.3% vs core goods +16.2% since Dec-2019. Tradable goods prices are pinned by a globally elastic supply curve, exactly Borio & Lowe's supply-side channel plus credibility-induced stickiness.

(iii) THE ONE SURVIVING CIRCUIT MECHANISM, stated precisely enough to look for: bank or dealer credit created against an existing financial asset and extinguished when that asset is resold — margin debt, securities-based lending, prime-brokerage financing, repo against the position — constitutes purchasing power for assets that is never anyone's income and never becomes anyone's income. In Keynes's 1937 terms it is a revolving fund of finance; in Graziani's, initial finance that opens and closes without ever passing through the market for current output. Because it is a revolving fund, the load-bearing observable is TURNOVER, not outstanding stock: the same balance-sheet capacity can support an arbitrarily large flow of asset purchases per period as reuse rate rises. This is why register C-015 matters and why every stock-based metric (repo outstanding, margin balances, NBFI credit) systematically understates the mechanism.

WHERE TO LOOK FOR IT: the ratio of (margin debt + securities-based lending + non-centrally-cleared bilateral repo against equity/corporate collateral) TURNOVER to the outstanding balance, quarter by quarter. FINRA Rule 4521(d) margin debt rose 38.6% y/y to $1.417trn (Jul-2026) — only 2.04% of NFC equity market cap in stock terms, but the +$282bn Jul-2025-to-Apr-2026 flow maps at the Gabaix-Koijen multiplier to ~$1.4trn, roughly a third of the $4.243trn rise in NFC equity market value over the matching quarters. Add net share retirement at the 2025 pace of $304bn/yr and the two together map to ~60% of the appreciation. That is illustrative arithmetic, not an estimate, but it is the right shape of calculation and it is testable.

## Analysis

**1. The objection, met head-on.**

Price the three sealing conditions.

*(a) Low MPC of the seller.* True, and insufficient. Chodorow-Reich, Nenov and Simsek (AER 2021, 111(5):1613-1657) estimate a household MPC of 3.2 cents per dollar of stock wealth per year at a two-year horizon, from US county variation. Applied to this project's established $26.28trn of equity-linked net holding gains 2023-25, the run-rate consumption impulse is ~$841bn/yr: 3.79% of PCE ($22,184.1bn SAAR, Jun-2026) and 2.59% of GDP. That is not a leak; that is a breach. The confirming datum is the aggregate saving rate: 2.7% (Jun-2026), against a 1959-2019 mean of 8.48%, verified this session. Households are not saving out of income because the asset market is saving for them.

Fagereng, Holm, Moll and Natvik (NBER WP 26588, Dec-2019) name the mechanism and, read carefully, it cuts against the thesis. Net saving rates are flat at ~7% across the Norwegian wealth distribution; gross saving rates including capital gains reach ~35% at the top; "saving by holding" accounts for up to 80% of the rise in the wealth-to-income ratio from ~4 to ~7. But holding is a decision not to *sell*. It is not a decision not to *spend*. The wealthy hold the asset and consume out of income — which is precisely a 2.7% saving rate. Di Maggio, Kermani and Majlesi (JF 2020, 75(6):3175-3219) find an MPC out of unrealised capital gains of 13% for the bottom half of the wealth distribution and ~5% for the rest, with dividends far more potent than gains across the whole distribution. Even 5% is not zero, and the base is $26trn.

Distribution corroborates rather than rescues. Moody's Analytics (Zandi, Bloomberg, Sep-2025) put the top income decile at 49.2% of US consumer spending in 2025-Q2, the highest since 1989 and up from ~36% three decades ago. The wealth effect did not vanish. It concentrated — and concentrated spending is still spending.

*(b) A buyer that cannot consume.* Analytically backwards for the largest case. A buyback is a corporation handing cash to a household and destroying a share; it opens the circuit, it does not seal it. Z.1 gives the scale: US non-financial corporate net equity issuance ran -$611.0bn (2023), -$398.0bn (2024), -$304.1bn (2025) — $1.31trn of cash disgorged to shareholders in three years, before dividends. (2026-Q1 flipped to +$124.4bn SAAR, the first positive quarter since 2021-Q2; one observation, worth watching.) The equity market has been a net *source* of cash to households, not a sink.

This exposes a conflation at the heart of the circuit thesis. What is inelastic is the supply of *shares*, not the supply of *money*. Gabaix-Koijen's ~$5 multiplier is a share-scarcity result: a buyback counts as a net inflow because it withdraws float. No money is absorbed by "the stock market" — in a secondary market purchases equal sales identically. Money never went in. Applying the project's own rule, valuation is not money: $31.40trn of net holding gains is a revaluation of a stock that mostly did not trade, and a revaluation requires no monetary circuit to explain it because it involves no monetary transaction. The genuine (b) is contractual retirement saving, but that flows out of labour income, not out of asset sales, and so relocates the question rather than answering it.

*(c) Credit extinguished on resale.* This one works. It is the only mechanism in my lens that is genuinely circuit-shaped. Keynes (Economic Journal 47, 1937) supplies the theoretical object: finance as "a revolving fund of a more or less constant amount," replenished as one commitment is discharged and another opened. A revolving fund of constant size supports an unbounded flow of transactions per period. That is velocity, not quantity — and it is why the register C-015 problem is the right problem: stock measures (repo outstanding, margin balances) are structurally the wrong observable for a revolving-fund mechanism. Graziani's finance/funding distinction is the same cut: *initial finance* is bank credit that opens the circuit; *funding* is the ex-post placement of long-term claims. Margin loans, securities-based lending and repo are initial finance that is created against an existing asset and destroyed on its resale, never passing through the market for current output at all. That is a purchase of an asset with money that was never anyone's income and never becomes anyone's income.

Magnitude, verified this session. FINRA Rule 4521(d) margin debt: $1,417,225mn (Jul-2026), a record $1,502,072mn (Jun-2026), against $1,022,548mn (Jul-2025) — +38.6% y/y. In stock terms this is small: 2.04% of the $69.51trn market value of US non-financial corporate equity. In flow terms it is not. Illustratively, matching windows: margin debt rose +$281.7bn Jul-2025 to Apr-2026 while NFC equity market value rose $4.243trn (2025-Q2 to 2026-Q1). At the Gabaix-Koijen multiplier that flow alone maps to ~$1.4trn, about a third of the appreciation; add three quarters of net share retirement at the 2025 pace and the two together map to ~60%. Treat this as arithmetic, not an estimate — GK's multiplier is fitted to net equity flows generally, margin debt funds more than US NFC equity, and the windows are approximate.

**2. Why the goods price level did not move — and why that is not a circuit story.**

CRNS's most useful result for this project is the one nobody quotes: stock wealth raises employment and payroll in *non-tradable* industries, with no effect in tradables. The leak is real but *mis-addressed*. And the price data show its fingerprint. Verified this session (BLS via FRED, Jul-2026): core goods CPI +0.78% y/y, core services less energy +3.00% y/y; cumulatively since Dec-2019, +16.2% against +30.3%. Asset-fed demand landed in local services, whose prices rose half again as fast as goods, while tradable goods faced a globally elastic supply curve. That is a composition-and-elasticity phenomenon, not monetary segmentation.

Borio and Lowe (BIS WP 114, Jul-2002) said this first and said it without any circuit. Their three reasons why imbalances build in low inflation are: favourable supply-side shocks that push goods prices down and asset prices up simultaneously; monetary-policy credibility that anchors expectations and induces price and wage stickiness, so demand pressure temporarily does not show in inflation; and a policy reaction function that therefore does not tighten. In their account the demand pressure *is there in the real economy* — latent, suppressed by stickiness and elastic supply. Borio's later "excess financial elasticity" (BIS WP 456/457, 2014) is a statement about the absence of a nominal anchor on credit, and he explicitly poses it *against* the excess-saving view — which is the Mian-Straub-Sufi channel. The two leading explanations of the same object are rivals, not complements, and neither is a sealed circuit.

**3. What Minsky and Tobin actually say.**

Minsky's two-price system is the cleanest statement of the operator's *observation* and the sharpest refutation of the operator's *mechanism*. P_K, the demand price of capital assets, is set by expected quasi-rents, the capitalisation rate, liquidity preference and views on acceptable liability structures; P_I, the supply price of current output, by cost plus mark-up. Different forces — yes. But Minsky's point is that the *gap* between them determines investment: when P_K exceeds P_I, new capital assets get produced. The divergence is not a sealed compartment; it is the transmission channel into the goods economy. A theory in which asset prices rise and nothing happens in goods markets is a theory in which Minsky's mechanism has failed.

Tobin's q measures that failure. Computed this session from Z.1 (June-2026 vintage): equity q for US non-financial corporates — market value of equities outstanding over net worth — stood at 1.819 in 2026-Q1, having peaked at 1.943 in 2025-Q3. Long-run mean since 1945-Q4 is 0.966; the pre-1995 mean 0.793. The eight highest readings in 304 quarterly observations are all from 2024-2026. The 2000 dot-com peak was 1.467; the 2021 peak 1.701. Against that, this project has established that net fixed investment rose 5.8% and that the corporate financing gap is negative in four of five recent quarters. The bridge between the two price levels is at its highest reading in eighty-one years and carrying almost no traffic. The anomaly requiring explanation is therefore real-side — Gutiérrez and Philippon (BPEA Fall 2017) attribute roughly a third of the investment gap to intangibles, the rest to concentration, quasi-indexer ownership and payout preference — not monetary.

**4. Where a circuit story is genuinely right: Graziani's non-closure.**

Graziani's circuit fails to close when income recipients hold liquid balances rather than spending on current output: firms cannot recover initial finance, debt accumulates cycle to cycle, and the money stock ratchets up (Graziani 2003, pp.30-31, as reconstructed by Forges Davanzati, Pacella and Patalano). Map this onto the asset economy and it is exactly Mian-Straub-Sufi. But note what it explains and what it does not. Non-closure produces *deficient goods demand and rising indebtedness* — it is a reason goods prices are weak, not a reason asset prices are strong. The asset-price consequence is second-order and residual: with the corporate sector running no external financing need, the leaked saving cannot be absorbed as new corporate liabilities and must be absorbed by revaluation of existing claims plus government debt. That is one circuit that fails to close, not two circuits running in parallel.

Krippner (Socio-Economic Review 3(2), 2005) quantifies less than her reputation suggests for this question. Her measure — portfolio income to cash flow for US non-financial firms, peaking in the late 1980s at ~5x post-war levels, series ending 2001 — documents where corporate profit accrues, not whether purchasing power is segregated between asset and goods markets. It cannot bear the weight the thesis needs.

**Cannot establish.** 1. COLLATERAL VELOCITY REMAINS UNMEASURED, AND MY LENS CANNOT MEASURE IT. Keynes's revolving fund is the correct theoretical object, and it tells you the observable must be a turnover rate, not a stock. No public series reports the turnover rate of pledged collateral. I did not establish what velocity currently is; I established only why every stock-based proxy is the wrong instrument for it. Register C-015 stands and is not closed by this lens.

2. CAPITAL GAINS REALISATIONS 2024-26. I could not retrieve CBO's realised-capital-gains projections this session (cbo.gov returned 403 to both WebFetch and curl). I therefore CANNOT state what fraction of the $26.28trn of equity-linked gains was actually converted to cash, which is the single most decisive number for sizing leak-condition (a). The CBO figure of "over $2trn, 8.7% of GDP" for 2021 appeared in search summaries but I did not verify it against the primary document; do not carry it forward as verified.

3. WHETHER THE 3.2c MPC HOLDS TODAY. CRNS is identified off cross-county variation in a sample predating the current concentration of equity ownership (top 1% now 50.2%). The paper is explicitly robust to MPC heterogeneity ACROSS counties, not across time or across levels of concentration. If the marginal dollar of stock wealth today sits with a household whose MPC is nearer Di Maggio et al's 5% ceiling or nearer zero, my $841bn figure is wrong in either direction. It is a scaling exercise, not an estimate.

4. WHETHER THE GABAIX-KOIJEN MULTIPLIER APPLIES TO MARGIN-FINANCED FLOWS SPECIFICALLY. It is estimated for net equity flows in general. Margin debt also funds non-US and non-equity positions, and a leveraged flow may be differentially transitory. My ~33% and ~60% decompositions are illustrative arithmetic with mismatched windows (monthly margin against quarterly Z.1), not findings. Do not quote them as measurements.

5. NO COUNTERFACTUAL. Nothing here establishes what goods inflation WOULD have been absent the asset boom. The core-services/core-goods split (30.3%/16.2% since Dec-2019) is consistent with the leak landing in non-tradables, but it is also consistent with post-pandemic shelter and wage dynamics that have nothing to do with asset prices. I have shown a fingerprint, not a proof of causation.

6. DIRECTION OF CAUSATION ON THE SAVING RATE. A 2.7% saving rate alongside record holding gains is consistent with (i) a wealth effect, (ii) a distributional shift in whose income is measured, and (iii) measurement problems in the BEA saving residual. I have not separated them.

7. THE 2026-Q1 FLIP TO POSITIVE NET EQUITY ISSUANCE (+$124.4bn SAAR) is one quarter and subject to Z.1 revision. It is not a trend.

8. MINSKY, GRAZIANI AND KEYNES ARE NOT QUANTIFIABLE FRAMEWORKS. Nothing in the two-price system, the circuit, or the finance motive generates a testable magnitude on its own. Every number above comes from outside the theory. Anyone claiming these theories "show" a magnitude is overclaiming.

# The rival explanation argued at full strength: secular decline in r* (Rachel–Smith, Rachel–Summers, Del Negro et al., Farhi–Gourio, Caballero–Farhi–Gourinchas) plus Gabaix–Koijen inelastic markets, tested against the 2021–2026 episode to establish the residual left for a credit or collateral mechanism.

**Verdict: `neutral`**

**Headline.** I was asked to make the financial-circuit thesis unnecessary by arguing that discount rates fell. The data killed my own assignment first: between end-2021 and end-2025 the discount rate applied to US equities ROSE by 266bp on Damodaran's own numbers, and prices rose 43.6% anyway. Worse for the prior synthesis, the discount-rate channel was never the main story even in the period it was supposed to explain — Greenwald, Lettau & Ludvigson (JPE, April 2025) attribute only 14.3% of the 1989–2017 equity rise to interest rates. And on the aggregate Z.1 measure the 2021Q4–2025Q4 rise involved ZERO multiple expansion (−1.3%): prices tracked after-tax profits almost exactly. The residual for a credit mechanism in the CURRENT move is close to zero. The residual is elsewhere — in the failure of a ZIRP-era multiple to mean-revert through a 339bp real-rate normalisation.

**Mechanism.** What does the work in 2021–2026 is NOT a discount-rate mechanism and NOT, on this evidence, a credit mechanism. It is cash flow plus expected cash flow. Corporate profits after tax rose 34.1% (2021Q4–2025Q4) while aggregate nonfinancial corporate equity value rose 32.4% — a 1.3% CONTRACTION in the multiple. The S&P 500's larger 43.6% rise reflects per-share effects (float retired: −$1.90trn cumulative net issuance 2022Q1–2025Q4), index composition including financials that Z.1's nonfinancial aggregate excludes, and a +403bp rise in the consensus five-year earnings growth forecast (6.47% → 10.50%). The deep driver of the profit level is Greenwald–Lettau–Ludvigson's factor-share reallocation — 40.2% of the entire 1989–2017 equity rise, versus 14.3% for interest rates — and Cho & Williams (NY Fed, April 2026) tie the labour-share decline to the same secular forces that lowered r*, giving the non-monetary account a cash-flow route that needs no discount-rate move at all.

Where a financial-circuit mechanism would still have to operate, stated precisely enough to look for: (a) in the ERP, not the risk-free rate. The 2012→2021 fall in the required return on equities was 179bp, of which only 25bp came from the Treasury yield and 154bp from implied-ERP compression. A collateral mechanism raises risk-bearing capacity, which shows up as risk-price compression — GLL's "orthogonal risk price" term, 20.8% of the 1989–2017 move and 28.1% of the ME/NVA rise since 1989. (b) In level persistence rather than flow. The aggregate equity/after-tax-profits multiple recovered from 14.98 (2022Q4) to 19.93 (2025Q4) while the 10-year real yield rose, and now sits within 1.3% of its ZIRP peak after a 339bp real-rate normalisation. A mechanism that supports a valuation LEVEL through a discount-rate shock is balance-sheet-shaped, not news-shaped. (c) Through Gabaix–Koijen rather than instead of it: GK explicitly leave flow origin unexplained, so a credit-financed flow inherits the full $3–8 multiplier and, being mechanically less price-elastic, plausibly more.

## Analysis

**1. The rival account, built properly — and its own quantitative self-limitation.**

Rachel & Smith (BoE Staff WP 571, Dec 2015; IJCB 13(3), Sep 2017) is the strongest version. Global long real rates fell ~450bp since the 1980s; they account for ~400bp. Saving-side: demographics 90bp, within-country inequality 45bp, EM government precautionary saving 25bp (~150bp total). Investment-side: falling relative price of capital goods 50bp, retreat from public investment 20bp, widening spread between the risk-free rate and the return on capital 70bp (~140bp). Plus ~100bp from slower expected trend growth. Residual ~50bp. Rachel & Summers (BPEA Spring 2019) extend it: the observed neutral rate fell ~300bp over a generation, but the *private-sector* neutral rate fell as much as ~700bp, with roughly 400bp offset by fiscal expansion. Del Negro, Giannone, Giannoni & Tambalotti (BPEA Spring 2017) locate the post-1990s decline chiefly in a rising convenience yield on safe and liquid assets rather than in growth. Caballero, Farhi & Gourinchas (JEP 31(3), 2017) supply the supply-side counterpart: safe-asset issuance has not kept pace with global demand.

This account is empirically vindicated on its own terms. The NY Fed's current estimate (Cho & Williams, Liberty Street Economics, 15 April 2026) puts R* at roughly 1%, down from 2.5–4% in 2000 — almost exactly Rachel & Smith's 2015 forecast that the global neutral rate would settle near 1%. The secular-forces literature made a prediction about r* and it held.

The problem is that this vindication does not transfer to asset prices, and the literature itself says so. Farhi & Gourio (BPEA Fall 2018) document the awkward fact that while safe real rates trended down for thirty years, the return on private capital was stable or rising, the wedge widened, and stock market valuation ratios rose only moderately. Their attribution runs to market power, unmeasured intangibles and *rising* risk premia — not to a uniform fall in the discount rate applied to equities.

**2. The decomposition that settles it.**

Greenwald, Lettau & Ludvigson, "How the Wealth Was Won" (JPE vol. 133 no. 4, April 2025; electronically published 29 Jan 2025), Table 2 Panel B, is the direct test. Over 1989–2017 the real per-capita value of corporate equity grew 429.7%. The additive decomposition: factor-share reallocation 40.16%, real per-capita output growth 24.75%, orthogonal risk price 20.81%, risk-free rate 14.29%. Their 1952–88 comparison inverts: output growth explained 108% of a much smaller rise, and factor shares contributed negatively.

So the entire discount-rate channel — risk price plus risk-free rate — is 35.1% of the 1989–2017 move, and the risk-free rate alone is 14.3%. The single largest contributor, at 40.2%, is a reallocation of rewards from labour to shareholders. This is a real, non-monetary, non-discount-rate mechanism, and it is bigger than growth and bigger than rates combined. The prior synthesis's conclusion that the illusion is "a discount-rate artefact, not a monetary one" is therefore wrong in its positive half as well as arguable in its negative half. It is predominantly a factor-share artefact.

Note that Cho & Williams (April 2026) close the loop: a 1pp higher R* is associated with a 0.044 higher log trend labour share (~2.5pp at sample means). The same secular forces that push r* down push the labour share down and the profit share up. The rival account thus reaches high equity values by two routes, and the cash-flow route is the larger one.

**3. The 2021–2026 episode breaks the discount-rate story on sign.**

10-year TIPS (FRED DFII10): −1.04% on 31 Dec 2021 (series minimum −1.19% on 3 Aug 2021) against +2.35% on 19 Aug 2026 — a 339bp rise in the real risk-free rate. S&P 500: 4,766.18 (31 Dec 2021) → 6,845.50 (31 Dec 2025) → 7,641.16 (20 Aug 2026).

Damodaran's own dataset (histimpl.xls, last saved 7 Jan 2026) makes it exact. Implied ERP (FCFE) was 4.24% at end-2021 and 4.23% at end-2025, against a 1960–2025 mean of 4.2477% — no compression. But the 10-year Treasury went 1.51% → 4.18%. The required nominal return on equities therefore rose from 5.75% to 8.41%: **+266bp**. Prices rose 43.6% into a discount rate that rose 266bp.

I verified in this session what absorbs it. Reconstructing his implied-ERP solve — five years of analyst-forecast growth then a terminal value with stable growth set equal to the risk-free rate — reproduces his published series to within 0–52bp and exactly (to the basis point) for 2019. Two consequences. First, his ERP is by construction near-insensitive to parallel shifts in r and g, so "the ERP did not compress" is substantially a property of the estimator, not independent evidence about risk appetite. Second, the residual absorber is visible: his analyst five-year growth estimate went from 6.47% (end-2021) to 10.50% (end-2025), +403bp. Growth expectations rose more than the discount rate did.

**4. The aggregate measure: no multiple expansion at all.**

Z.1 nonfinancial corporate equity liabilities (FRED NCBEILQ027S) went $54.26trn (2021Q4) → $71.86trn (2025Q4), +32.4%. Corporate profits after tax (CPATAX) went $2.69trn → $3.61trn, +34.1%. The aggregate multiple therefore fell 1.3%. Prices tracked profits one-for-one.

Contrast ZIRP. 2012Q1 → 2021Q4: equity +218.8%, profits +52.9%, nominal GDP +54.4% — multiple expansion **+108.6%**, the ratio going 9.68 → 20.19.

That is the finding. The re-rating the operator observed is real, it is large, and it happened in 2012–2021. It has not been extended since. The residual for a credit or collateral mechanism *in the 2021–2026 move* is approximately zero.

**5. Where the residual actually is — and it is not where either side put it.**

Two places.

First, the composition of the ZIRP re-rating. On Damodaran's data the 2012→2021 fall in the required return was 179bp, of which the Treasury yield supplied only 25bp (1.76% → 1.51%) and the implied ERP supplied 154bp (5.78% → 4.24%). Even under ZIRP the discount-rate action was risk-premium compression, not the risk-free rate. Expanded risk-bearing capacity is precisely what a balance-sheet or collateral mechanism produces. GLL's "orthogonal risk price" channel is the same object.

Second, persistence. The multiple stood at 19.93 (2025Q4) and 19.18 (2026Q1), against 20.19 at the ZIRP peak and a 1989–2019 range of roughly 9–16 outside the dot-com episode. It fell to 14.98 in 2022Q4 and recovered to 19.93 while the real rate went *up* 35bp. A ZIRP-era valuation survived a 339bp real-rate normalisation. Neither the r* literature nor GLL predicts that. A level-sustaining, balance-sheet-capacity mechanism is exactly the shape of thing that would.

**6. Gabaix–Koijen is not a rival. It is a shared amplifier.**

NBER WP 28967 (June 2021): $1 into equities raises aggregate market value ~$5 (range $3–8); macro price elasticity ~0.2. For buybacks specifically the coefficient is ~$2 per $1, and they flag it as tentative.

The decisive sentence is methodological: they postpone to future research the question of what determines flows in the first place. GK is agnostic about flow origin. A credit-financed dollar and a saving-financed dollar are the same dollar in their framework. So inelastic markets do not displace the circuit thesis — they multiply whichever account supplies the flow. If anything they strengthen it, since a levered buyer is mechanically more price-inelastic than an unlevered one.

Applying their coefficient to measured supply: cumulative nonfinancial corporate net equity retirement 2022Q1–2025Q4 (FRED NCBCEBQ027S) was −$1.90trn. At $2 that is ~$3.8trn of market value (~7% of the 2021Q4 base); at $5, ~$9.5trn (~18%). This is my arithmetic applying their coefficient, not their result.

**7. A correction to the brief.**

The instruction treats "80.2% of the 2023–25 net worth rise was holding gains" as prima facie evidence for the rival account. It is not evidence for either. Holding gains are the explanandum. Both accounts predict prices rise faster than saving; GK predicts it as an identity.

Similarly, Caballero–Farhi–Gourinchas is listed as a rival, but a shortage of *safe assets* is a shortage of *collateral* described in different vocabulary. Safe assets are the assets that get pledged. CFG is not orthogonal to the circuit thesis; it is one of its premises stated in general-equilibrium language.

**Cannot establish.** 1. **The growth-versus-risk-appetite split cannot be identified.** Damodaran's implied ERP hard-codes terminal growth equal to the risk-free rate — I verified this by reconstruction (0–52bp match, exact for 2019). His terminal value is therefore near-invariant to parallel shifts in r and g, so a stable ERP across the 2021–2025 rate rise is substantially an estimator property. It cannot arbitrate between "expected growth rose 403bp" and "risk appetite expanded." No available series separates them.

2. **Whether the +403bp rise in consensus growth forecasts is warranted.** If those forecasts are wrong, what looks like a cash-flow explanation is a valuation the forecasts merely rationalise, and the question of what funds the marginal buyer reopens. This is unresolvable ex ante.

3. **Collateral velocity now.** Per register entry C-015 this remains unmeasured. It is the circuit account's central quantity, and its absence is a data gap, not a refutation and not a confirmation. Nothing in my lens closes it.

4. **The credit-financed share of equity flows.** Gabaix & Koijen explicitly postpone the determinants of flows. Without splitting flows by funding source, the $5 multiplier is silent on the circuit thesis in both directions — it neither supports nor undermines it.

5. **The S&P 500 / Z.1 wedge cannot be fully attributed.** The index rose 43.6% while the nonfinancial corporate aggregate rose 32.4%. Float retirement, financial-sector inclusion in the index but not in NCBEILQ027S, and mega-cap concentration all contribute; I did not decompose them and should not be read as having done so.

6. **Causation, anywhere.** Every relationship above is observational. Rachel–Smith's own accounting leaves ~50bp of 450bp unexplained; GLL's credible set for the 1989–2017 risk-price contribution spans zero.

7. **The GK-multiplier / saving-share coincidence is not an identity.** Household net worth and equity market capitalisation are different measurement bases; per project rules the two figures must not be treated as the same quantity. The numerical agreement is suggestive only.

8. **The counterfactual multiple.** My statement that a reversion to the 2012Q1 ratio of 9.68 would imply ~$34.9trn rather than $71.86trn is arithmetic on a counterfactual, not a measured residual, and carries no claim that reversion was due.
