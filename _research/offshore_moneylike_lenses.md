# WHO IS DOING THE LENDING — decomposition of the offshore dollar expansion by bank nationality and booking location (BIS LBS by nationality, LBS by residence/reporting country, CBS ultimate-risk basis, BoJ FSR and BoJ Review), with the funding side separated from the lending side.

**Headline.** The operator's instinct is not merely right, it is measurable — and it partly breaks the project's headline framing. Splitting the four-quarter expansion into claims and liabilities on BIS's own break/FX-adjusted flow series shows banks outside the US grew USD claims by $2,040bn but USD liabilities by only $1,650bn: $391bn (19%) of the expansion has no dollar liability behind it and must be FX-swap funded. Japan is 47% of that residual on its own ($182bn; only 46% of its claim growth was liability-matched) and Japan plus China are 89% of it. The UK is the single largest booking location for new offshore dollar credit (+$419bn, 20.5% of the total) but runs a 92%-matched book. So the "who" and the "how" are different countries: London books the volume, Tokyo carries the funding gap. The BoJ says the mechanism outright — major banks' domestic yen loan-to-deposit surplus is swapped into dollars to fund overseas foreign-currency lending (FSR April 2026, footnote 16).

## Mechanics

The binding constraint the operator identifies is real and it produces four distinguishable balance-sheet cases. The LBS records all four identically at the aggregate, which is why the gross flow cannot be read as money creation.

CASE A — genuine offshore dollar creation (London/eurodollar model). Bank in London writes a $100m dollar loan. Entry: Dr Loans (USD) 100 / Cr Customer deposit (USD) 100. Both legs on the London bank's own books. A new dollar-denominated deposit exists that did not exist before, is a liability of a non-US bank, and appears in no monetary aggregate. No Fed account is touched at inception — this is the sense in which a non-US bank *can* create dollars. The constraint arrives at settlement: when the borrower pays a counterparty banking elsewhere, the London bank must deliver central bank money it cannot issue. Dr Customer deposit 100 / Cr Due from US correspondent 100. It must therefore pre-fund or borrow a settlement balance. Creation is possible; retention of the deposit is what makes it survive. In LBS this shows as claims +100 and liabilities +100 at the same reporting country — the 92%-matched UK profile is consistent with this (and with Case D).

CASE B — Japanese model, intermediation not creation. MUFG Tokyo writes a $100m dollar loan. It has no dollar deposit franchise to credit. It raises the dollars in the swap market: spot leg, Dr USD nostro 100 / Cr JPY cash 100-equivalent — an exchange of existing assets, not an issuance. The forward leg (obligation to redeliver dollars and receive yen) is a derivative, off-balance-sheet at inception apart from fair value. Then Dr USD loan 100 / Cr USD nostro 100. Net effect on the LBS: dollar claims +100, dollar liabilities +0. No new dollars exist anywhere in the world; the dollars were supplied by the swap counterparty — a US money fund, a reserve manager, another bank — out of an existing stock. The yen side is funded by the domestic deposit surplus the BoJ names in FSR footnote 16. This is precisely the $182bn Japanese residual and the $1,752bn Japan-located net stock. It is a currency-transformation trade wearing the costume of credit creation.

CASE C — deposit gathering and re-lending (Hong Kong, Cayman, Bahrain). A Hong Kong bank receives a $100m dollar time deposit whose dollars were created elsewhere. Dr Due from US correspondent 100 / Cr Customer deposit (USD) 100. It then lends: Dr Loan 100 / Cr Due from correspondent 100. Existing dollars are transferred and re-lent. LBS shows liabilities rising at least as fast as claims — Hong Kong at 127% matched and Cayman at 162% are net *importers* of dollar funding, redistributing it to the systems that book the assets. These centres are the plumbing, not the source.

CASE D — pure interbank recycling. Bank A (Tokyo) places $100m with Bank B (London). A: Dr Interbank claim 100 / Cr USD nostro 100. B: Dr USD nostro 100 / Cr Interbank liability 100. Aggregate LBS: claims +100 and liabilities +100, and zero new credit to the non-bank economy. Because `L_CP_SECTOR` is suppressed to 'A' in the foreign-currency cut (verified this session), there is no way to strip this out. The gross $1.90trn contains an unmeasurable amount of it.

WHY THE OFFSHORE SYSTEM CANNOT SIMPLY EXPAND. Every offshore dollar deposit is, at one remove, a claim on the US banking system, because final settlement occurs only on Fedwire across accounts no foreign bank holds. Expansion is capped by three things: access to correspondent settlement balances; the willingness of dollar holders to leave balances offshore rather than repatriating them to a US bank; and, for Case B, the capacity and price of the swap market. That last item is why the cross-currency basis is the single price that governs the whole structure — for a Japanese bank the basis is not a spread, it is the marginal cost of the liability it cannot issue. When the basis widens, Japanese dollar lending is quantity-constrained in a way that UK dollar lending is not.

## Analysis

## What the BIS will and will not let you cut

Before any number: I tested the cube rather than assuming it. The result constrains everything below and confirms the project's stated limits, with one addition.

The `L_CURR_TYPE` dimension (which is what makes the headline series "offshore" — USD as a *foreign* currency means the booking office is not in the US) **does not intersect with `L_PARENT_CTY`**. Querying available parent countries returns exactly one value, `5J` (all countries), for `L_CURR_TYPE` in ('F','D'). Only under `L_CURR_TYPE=A` do 14 parent countries appear. **There is therefore no published series for "offshore USD claims by bank nationality."** Any nationality decomposition necessarily includes US-located offices. I verified the same suppression for sector (`L_CP_SECTOR`: only 'A') and counterparty country (`L_CP_COUNTRY`: only '5J'), which reproduces the project's established limits, and I add that these hold even when the reporting country is pinned.

One dimension *is* open and the project has not used it: `L_REP_CTY` returns **48 booking locations** for the headline foreign-currency cut, and `L_POS_TYPE` returns local ('R') as well as cross-border ('N'). That is the whole basis of this note.

A separate hard limit: **the consolidated banking statistics have no currency dimension at all** (`CURR_TYPE_BOOK` takes only TO1 and LC1). CBS cannot answer "which banking system lends dollars." It can only rank total international footprint.

## Nationality (LBS/N, 2026-Q1, retrieved 2026-08-21)

Cross-border USD claims, all reporting locations, all currency-types — total $21,797bn, +$2,375bn over four quarters (+12.2%). This total reconciles exactly against the residence cut: $21,797bn − $17,863bn = **$3,934bn booked by offices located inside the US** (derived by subtraction; BIS does not publish the US-located cut, which returned no data).

By parent nationality: US $3,880bn (+20.2%), **Japan $3,618bn (+$258bn, +7.7%)**, France $2,132bn (+15.6%), UK $2,124bn (+16.4%), Canada $2,084bn (+14.2%), China $1,555bn (+9.4%), Germany $1,096bn (+2.1%), Switzerland $960bn, Spain $483bn (+26.2%), Netherlands $391bn, Italy $191bn (+27.8%), Sweden $128bn, Belgium $42bn.

Two things matter here. First, **Japan is the largest non-US national banking system in cross-border dollar claims, but it is growing slower than the aggregate** — 7.7% against 12.2%. The fast growers are Spain, Italy, the US, the UK and France. Second, only 13 nationalities are published: the named systems sum to $18,684bn, leaving **$3,113bn (14.3%) unallocated by nationality**. Taiwan, Singapore, Korea, Australia and India — precisely the systems the lens asks about — are absent from the USD nationality cut entirely. CBS shows Singapore-headquartered banks with $954bn and Taiwanese with $534bn of foreign claims, so the missing mass is real, not trivial.

Netting claims against liabilities by nationality gives the funding picture: **Japan +$1,953bn, China +$818bn, Canada +$568bn** are the net cross-border dollar creditors; **France −$304bn, the US −$585bn, Sweden −$115bn** are net borrowers. Over four quarters the world net position widened $628bn, of which **Japan +$166bn, China +$151bn and Canada +$129bn are 71%**.

## Booking location — where the credit is actually written

Cross-border USD claims of banks outside the US, $17,863bn, +$1,900bn (+11.9%): **UK $3,298bn (+$444bn, +15.6%)**, Japan $3,095bn (+$262bn), France $1,786bn (+$271bn, +17.9%), Canada $1,446bn, Hong Kong $1,345bn (+13.4%), China $977bn, Germany $952bn, Switzerland $558bn, Taiwan $466bn, Australia $324bn (+25.0%), Spain $307bn (+29.8%). Then the centres: Cayman $294bn, Luxembourg $228bn, Jersey $128bn, Macau $118bn (+30.9%), Bahrain $106bn, Bahamas $85bn, Panama $76bn, Guernsey $53bn, Isle of Man $23bn, Bermuda $10bn.

**Offshore financial centres are a smaller share than the framing usually assumes.** On the broadest definition (including Hong Kong and Luxembourg) they are $2,465bn, **13.8% of the total and 11.9% of the four-quarter flow**. Excluding Hong Kong, $1,120bn (6.3%); excluding Hong Kong and Luxembourg, $892bn (5.0%). The offshore dollar system is overwhelmingly booked in **major onshore jurisdictions with real prudential supervisors** — the UK, Japan, France, Canada, Germany. This cuts against the "regulatory blind spot" reading: the booking locations are visible to the PRA, the FSA/BoJ, the ACPR and OSFI. What is invisible is the *currency* detail, not the *location*.

Suppression: **Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, Curaçao and Russia return NaN with `OBS_CONF=F`** in the USD cut. Singapore publishes $810bn of all-currency cross-border claims (2026-Q1) but zero of its dollar book. Named locations sum to $17,173bn against the $17,863bn total, so **$690bn (3.9%) is unallocated by location** — that is the floor on the suppression, and the OFC share above is correspondingly a floor.

## The funding side — the actual answer to the question

Using BIS's break-and-FX-adjusted flow series (`L_MEASURE=F`) for claims *and* liabilities, `L_POS_TYPE=A` so local foreign-currency positions are included, four quarters to 2026-Q1, banks located outside the US:

- Claims **+$2,040bn**, liabilities **+$1,650bn**, residual **+$391bn — 19% unfunded on balance sheet.**

By location, "matched %" = liability flow / claim flow: **Japan 46%** (+$334bn claims, +$152bn liabilities, **$182bn unfunded**); **China −51%** (+$110bn claims while liabilities *fell* $56bn, **$166bn unfunded**); Italy 28%, Macau 53%, Ireland 54%, Taiwan 60%, Australia 75%. Against: **UK 92%, France 92%, Germany 87%, Canada 103%, Hong Kong 127%, Cayman 162%.**

Japan is 47% of the global residual; **Japan and China together are 89%**. The UK and French books — which produced 35% of all new offshore dollar claims — are essentially matched: they are intermediating, not running a currency-funding gap. Hong Kong and Cayman took in *more* dollar liabilities than claims; they are net dollar *gatherers* feeding the system.

The stock levels make the point permanent rather than cyclical. Japan-located offices, total USD positions: claims $3,514bn, liabilities $1,763bn, **net +$1,752bn**. Even after crediting Tokyo's $707bn of local dollar deposits, $1.75trn of dollar assets sit against no dollar liability. That net position was $533bn in 2007-Q4 and has risen through every cycle: $930bn (2019-Q4), $1,454bn (2024-Q4), $1,752bn (2026-Q1). **The entire rest-of-world-ex-US net dollar position is only $947bn — Japan is 185% of it.** Every other banking system in aggregate is net short dollars on balance sheet; Japan is the single massive long.

## Primary confirmation of the mechanism

The BoJ states it directly. Financial System Report, April 2026, footnote 16: part of the domestic yen loan-to-deposit gap at major banks is used as a funding source for foreign-currency-denominated overseas loans, via FX and currency swaps. Chart IV-3-14 ("Loan-to-funding gap among major banks", internationally active banks, data to 2025-Q4) plots loans against deposits, corporate bonds and medium-to-long-term FX and currency swaps on a scale to $1.2trn. Chart IV-3-15 decomposes dollar funding cost into FX and currency swaps, repos and deposits (median, major banks, February 2026).

BoJ Review 2025-E-8 (July 2025) is blunter: Japanese majors do not hold a stable retail deposit base in the United States and cover the shortfall with short-term FX swaps, medium-to-long-term currency swaps and corporate bonds. Its BOX Chart 1 plots loan-to-deposit ratios by currency reaching ~400–500% (Korean won), and argues that where deposits are structurally scarce it is rational to fund with swaps and repo rather than bid for deposits.

## Cannot establish

**Verified non-existent this session, not merely un-found:**

1. **Offshore USD credit by bank nationality.** The `L_CURR_TYPE` × `L_PARENT_CTY` intersection returns only `5J` for both 'F' and 'D'. There is no series for "USD claims of Japanese/French/Chinese banks located outside the US." Every nationality number in this note includes US-located offices — a Japanese bank's New York branch lending cross-border to a non-US resident is inside the $3,618bn. The size of that contamination is bounded at $3,934bn system-wide but cannot be allocated by nationality.

2. **Counterparty sector for the offshore dollar cut.** `L_CP_SECTOR` returns only 'A'. Interbank recycling cannot be separated from credit to the non-bank economy. This is the single largest analytical gap and it is why the $1.90trn is an upper bound.

3. **Counterparty country.** `L_CP_COUNTRY` returns only `5J`. Who borrows the offshore dollars is unavailable in this cut.

4. **Any currency breakdown in the consolidated banking statistics.** `CURR_TYPE_BOOK` takes only TO1 (all) and LC1 (local). CBS ultimate-risk data cannot be used for dollar-specific nationality analysis at all — a limit worth stating explicitly because CBS is the natural place one would look.

5. **Taiwan, Singapore, Korea, Australia, India as bank nationalities in the USD LBS cut.** Not published. Only 13 nationalities exist, covering 85.7% of the total.

6. **Singapore's dollar book entirely.** Confidentiality-flagged (`OBS_CONF=F`, NaN) across all position types, while its all-currency total ($810bn) is published. Malaysia, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, Curaçao and Russia likewise. The OFC share of 13.8% is therefore a floor, not an estimate.

7. **Decomposition of the $391bn unfunded residual into FX swaps vs currency swaps vs equity vs statistical unallocation.** The LBS cannot distinguish these — it records the absence of a liability, not the identity of the off-balance-sheet substitute. The BoJ charts confirm the *mechanism* qualitatively for Japan but publish no underlying series. I did not find, and do not believe there exists, a published FX-swap notional broken down by bank nationality at quarterly frequency; the BIS Triennial (next vintage due 2026) gives market-wide turnover, not bank-nationality outstanding positions.

8. **Japanese banks' dollar-specific funding gap in yen terms, from the FSA.** I found no FSA series independent of the BoJ Joint Survey; the BoJ Review describes a "Joint Survey on Foreign Currency Liquidity Risk Management" with the FSA whose granular outputs are supervisory and unpublished.

**Not attempted, flagged as another researcher's ground:** the BIS Global Liquidity Indicators (dollar credit to non-banks outside the US, ~$14.7trn at end-March 2026 per the BIS release) — the borrower side. My WS_GLI key probes failed and I did not spend further budget there.

## Challenges to project context

**1. "The only channel that is unambiguously money creation" — the data does not support "unambiguously," and the measurement to settle it does not exist.**

This is the central challenge and it is empirical, not semantic. Splitting the same BIS adjusted-flow series into claims and liabilities shows 19% of the four-quarter expansion ($391bn of $2,040bn) has **no corresponding dollar liability**. That portion is not money creation under any definition — it is an existing dollar acquired in the swap market and on-lent, with the funding leg sitting off-balance-sheet as a forward. For Japan the unfunded share is 54%; for China, liabilities *fell* while claims rose. The BoJ states the mechanism in its own April 2026 report: yen deposits swapped into dollars.

The other 81% *is* liability-matched, but matched claims and liabilities are equally consistent with (a) genuine deposit creation and (b) pure interbank recycling, which creates nothing at the system level. Distinguishing them requires the counterparty-sector dimension, which I verified is suppressed to 'A'. So the honest statement is: **$1.90trn is a gross upper bound on offshore dollar money creation. The true figure is smaller by an unmeasurable amount, and at least $391bn of it is definitively not creation.** The project should carry the claims-and-liabilities pair, not the claims number alone.

**2. The framing that this sits "outside every regulator's view" is too strong on location, though right on currency.**

Offshore financial centres are 13.8% of the stock on the widest definition, 6.3% excluding Hong Kong, 5.0% excluding Hong Kong and Luxembourg. The overwhelming majority is booked in the UK, Japan, France, Canada and Germany — jurisdictions with the PRA, the FSA/BoJ, the ACPR, OSFI and BaFin, all of whom see these balance sheets in supervisory detail (the BoJ/FSA Joint Survey on Foreign Currency Liquidity Risk Management collects transaction-level dollar deposit data from Japanese G-SIBs). What is missing is not supervision but *published currency-disaggregated statistics*. That is a disclosure gap, not a regulatory blind spot, and the distinction matters for what one would conclude from it.

**3. The stock figures in the project context understate the system by using cross-border only.**

$17.86trn claims / $14.11trn liabilities is the cross-border cut. Adding local foreign-currency positions gives **$21.11trn claims / $20.17trn liabilities** for banks outside the US. The net position collapses from +$3.75trn to **+$0.95trn** once the $5.17trn of local dollar deposits is counted. The offshore dollar system is far closer to self-funded in aggregate than the cross-border cut implies — and the residual imbalance is almost entirely one country. Quoting the cross-border net without the local offset overstates the aggregate funding fragility by roughly four times while *understating* how concentrated it is.

**4. A correction to the lens brief's own premise.** The brief asks which systems are "expanding dollar books — Japanese, Canadian, French, UK, Chinese, Taiwanese, Singaporean." Japan is expanding *slowest* of the large systems (+7.7% nationality, +9.3% by location, against +11.9% aggregate). Japan is not the growth story; it is the *funding-structure* story. The growth is UK, French, Spanish, Italian and Australian. And Singapore cannot be assessed at all — its dollar book is fully suppressed.

**5. Minor: the project's rule "never difference the stock series" is right in general but I tested it here.** For USD-denominated positions there is no FX translation effect, and for the four quarters to 2026-Q1 the break effect is negligible: differenced stocks give +$2,049bn/+$1,658bn against BIS adjusted flows of +$2,040bn/+$1,650bn. The rule should stand, but this particular window is robust either way, which strengthens the funding-gap finding rather than resting it on a single construction.

# The funding side — enumerating, sizing and rating the stability of every source of dollars available to a bank without a Federal Reserve account, and identifying which source financed the current expansion.

**Headline.** The offshore dollar book's marginal funding is not FX swaps — it is non-bank financial institution wholesale deposits (+$590.2bn of the +$1,747.4bn four-quarter adjusted funding to 2026-Q1) and intragroup transfers (+$414.3bn), with only $390.5bn of the $2,040.1bn asset expansion unmatched by any reported dollar liability; meanwhile the gross off-balance-sheet dollar obligation of non-banks outside the US has reached $35.9trn at end-2025 on the BIS's own method, up 38% since mid-2022, with $15.6trn of the USD-leg increase in H1 2025 alone.

## Mechanics

**Origination (offshore dollar loan).** Bank L, located in London, lends $100 to a corporate. Entries at Bank L: Dr Loans (USD) 100 / Cr Customer deposits (USD) 100. No dollars have been obtained. A dollar deposit — a money-like liability outside every monetary aggregate — now exists. This is the money creation the project's headline measures.

**Drain.** The borrower pays a supplier banking at Bank P in Paris. Bank L must settle. Two cases. (i) If Bank P is willing to hold a claim on Bank L, settlement is an interbank book entry inside the offshore system: Dr Customer deposits 100 / Cr Due to Bank P 100. No US settlement asset moves. This is why "banks, total" is $9,882.7bn — 50.6% — of cross-border USD liabilities, and why $6,204.9bn of that is intragroup: the offshore system settles a large share of its own payments internally. (ii) If Bank P will not, Bank L must pay over Fedwire/CHIPS, drawing on its correspondent balance: Dr Customer deposits 100 / Cr Due from US correspondent 100. Bank L's cross-border claim on a US bank falls. The aggregate stock of that buffer is $2,845.8bn (claims on US banks) inside $8,547.0bn of total cross-border claims on US residents (2026-Q1).

**Replenishment — the funding entries, in order of the marginal amounts observed.**

1. *NBFI deposit / repo.* A Cayman fund or a US money fund places dollars. Bank L: Dr Due from US correspondent 100 / Cr Deposit from NBFI 100 (or Cr Repo, with securities pledged). Stock $5,047.3bn; four-quarter adjusted flow +$590.2bn. Very short; MMF assets maturing within seven days were $3,581.1bn of $8,388.3bn in May 2026.

2. *Intragroup.* Head office or the New York branch ships dollars. Bank L: Dr Due from US correspondent 100 / Cr Due to related office 100. Stock $6,204.9bn; flow +$414.3bn. From the US side this is H.8's net due to related foreign offices, +$803.8bn (5 Aug 2026); from the BIS side, offshore offices' net claim on their US affiliates is $795.5bn ($2,180.2bn claims less $1,384.7bn liabilities). Two independent sources agree to within 1%.

3. *Local dollar deposits.* A Hong Kong or Gulf resident places dollars at Bank L's own jurisdiction office. This never appears in cross-border data. Stock $5,173.8bn; four-quarter adjusted flow +$420.6bn. Stickiest of the wholesale sources; not covered by any deposit insurance the depositor can rely on.

4. *Corporate and official deposits.* Non-financial corporates $1,393.3bn (flow +$67.9bn); central banks / reserve managers $769.5bn (flow +$79.2bn); government $80.3bn. Together 11.4% of the stock and 9.4% of the flow. Small and inert.

5. *Unsecured wholesale paper.* Bank L issues a CD or CP into US money markets: Dr Due from US correspondent 100 / Cr Debt securities issued 100. Foreign financial CP outstanding in the US is $369.5bn (19 Aug 2026); prime MMF holdings of CDs, TDs and financial CP total $482.8bn (May 2026). Under 2% of the book. Structurally dead since the 2016 and 2023 MMF reforms.

6. *FX swap.* Bank L pays €92 spot, receives $100 spot, and contracts to reverse. On balance sheet: Dr Due from US correspondent (USD) 100 / Cr Due from EUR counterparty — i.e. the euro asset falls, the dollar asset rises. **No dollar liability is booked.** The obligation to return the dollars sits off balance sheet as the forward leg, carried only at fair value near zero. Consequence, and this is the testable identity: dollars sourced through swaps make on-balance-sheet dollar claims exceed on-balance-sheet dollar liabilities by the amount so sourced. For banks outside the US that excess is $946.7bn at 2026-Q1 (claims $21,112.4bn, liabilities $20,165.6bn), against −$101.0bn at 2019-Q4. Four-quarter widening: +$390.5bn on a $2,040.1bn asset expansion.

7. *Fed swap line.* Foreign central bank draws on the Fed and on-lends to Bank L: Dr Due from central bank (USD) 100 / Cr Deposit from central bank 100. Outstanding $123mn. Not in use.

**Why the identity in (6) must be read with caution.** LBS "total liabilities" excludes capital and, on the all-reporter cut where instrument detail exists, the claims-minus-liabilities excess is entirely a securities-and-derivatives artefact: cross-border USD loans and deposits are $14,118.5bn of claims against $15,823.8bn of liabilities (a *negative* $1,705.3bn), while "other instruments" run +$3,983.6bn. On four-quarter flows the loans-and-deposits gap widened only $40.3bn. BIS does not publish instrument detail for the foreign-currency (offshore) cut, so the swap-funding residual for banks outside the US cannot be cleaned of this contamination with published data.

## Analysis

**1. The premise needs one correction before the numbers make sense.** A bank outside the US does create a dollar deposit when it writes a dollar loan. Debit "loan to customer, USD", credit "customer deposit, USD" — the entry is identical to a US bank's. What the offshore bank lacks is not the ability to create the liability but the ability to *settle* it when the depositor pays someone at another bank. Settlement of a wholesale dollar payment runs through CHIPS/Fedwire and terminates in a Fed account. The offshore bank therefore needs a correspondent balance, not a prior deposit. This is why the offshore system's dollar assets are so heavily concentrated on US counterparties: banks located outside the US hold $8,547.0bn of cross-border USD claims on US residents against $4,290.7bn of cross-border USD liabilities to them (2026-Q1, BIS LBS) — a net claim on the United States of $4,256.3bn. Of the asset side, $2,845.8bn is claims on US banks. That is the settlement layer. Separately, foreign-related institutions inside the US held $1,149.2bn of cash assets (H.8, week ending 5 Aug 2026) against total Fed reserve balances of $2,935.3bn (H.4.1, 19 Aug 2026) — foreign banks' US offices hold on the order of two fifths of the reserve stock on 13.6% of US commercial-bank assets. The constraint binds at the drain, not at origination.

**2. The funded-liability decomposition.** For banks located outside the United States — the BIS foreign-currency residence cut, Q.S.*.A.USD.F.5J.A.5A.A.5J.*, which is the exact counterpart to the project's headline claims series — the complete reported dollar book at 2026-Q1 is: **claims $21,112.4bn, liabilities $20,165.6bn**. The liability side splits: cross-border $14,115.0bn (70.0%), **local liabilities booked in the bank's own jurisdiction in dollars $5,173.8bn (25.7%)**, unallocated by position type $876.8bn (4.3%). That second line is the answer to the "where are the deposits" question that most commentary misses: a quarter of the offshore dollar book is funded by dollar deposits taken in-country — Hong Kong, Singapore, London, the Gulf — not cross-border.

By counterparty, BIS publishes the sector split only on the all-currency-type basis, which covers all reporting banks ($19,518.5bn cross-border, of which roughly $5,403.5bn is booked by banks located in the US). On that basis: related offices (intragroup) $6,204.9bn (31.8%); unrelated banks $2,908.3bn (14.9%, derived as banks-total less related offices less central banks); **NBFIs $5,047.3bn (25.9%)**; non-financial corporates $1,393.3bn (7.1%); central banks $769.5bn (3.9%); general government $80.3bn (0.4%); unallocated $1,991.7bn (10.2%).

**3. Which source is doing the marginal work.** Over the four quarters to 2026-Q1, break- and FX-adjusted flows of cross-border USD liabilities (all reporters) were **+$1,747.4bn**, distributed: banks +$793.6bn (of which related offices +$414.3bn, central banks +$79.2bn); **NBFIs +$590.2bn**; non-financial corporates +$67.9bn; government +$16.4bn; unallocated +$237.3bn. Corporate deposits contributed 3.9% of the marginal funding. Retail is invisible in these statistics and immaterial cross-border. **The marginal on-balance-sheet dollar funding is wholesale: NBFI deposits and repo, plus intragroup redistribution.** NBFI dollar funding has risen 91.2% since 2019-Q4 ($2,639.6bn → $5,047.3bn); nothing else in the liability structure has moved comparably.

Geographically the NBFI money is: US residents $1,838.5bn, UK $890.6bn, **Cayman Islands $882.4bn**, Luxembourg $163.3bn, Japan $35.2bn (2026-Q1, cross-border USD liabilities to NBFIs by counterparty country). Roughly a third of the fastest-growing funding source in the global dollar system sits in two offshore fund domiciles.

**4. Where the funding is not coming from.** (a) *Unsecured wholesale into US money markets is now a rounding error.* Total US commercial paper outstanding was $1,441.2bn on 19 Aug 2026, of which foreign financial CP $369.5bn — 1.8% of the offshore dollar book. Prime MMFs held $203.1bn of CDs, $105.0bn of non-negotiable time deposits and $174.7bn of financial-company CP in May 2026 (SEC N-MFP), i.e. $482.8bn of direct unsecured bank paper across the whole prime complex, which is itself only $1,372.2bn of an $8,388.3bn MMF industry. The 2016 and 2023 MMF reforms did what they were designed to do: institutional prime is 3% of MMF assets (Fed FSR, May 2026). *MMF reform is no longer a meaningful constraint on offshore dollar credit, because the channel it constrains has already been abandoned.* (b) *Central bank swap lines are at $123mn* (H.4.1, 19 Aug 2026), unchanged from a year earlier — test operations, exactly as the project context states. (c) *Official reserve deposits are small and stable*: $769.5bn cross-border, +$79.2bn over four quarters. The dollar was 57.13% of allocated reserves at 2026-Q1 (IMF COFER), but reserve managers hold that overwhelmingly in securities, not bank deposits.

**5. Repo.** MMF repo totalled $3,004.2bn in May 2026 (centrally cleared $1,177.1bn, non-cleared $1,827.1bn); the Fed's runnable-liabilities repo line was $5,887bn at 2025-Q4 and grew 19.1% year on year — the fastest-growing component of Table 4.1 by a wide margin. Non-US dealers are large borrowers here, but the counterparty-domicile split is no longer published: the Federal Reserve's Enhanced Financial Accounts table on MMF holdings by country of issuance is frozen at 30 April 2021, when non-US counterparties were 58.7% of MMF repo ($1,235.9bn total: USA $510.7bn, France $226.7bn, Canada $175.5bn, Japan $162.1bn, UK $89.9bn). Five years of a fast-growing channel are unmeasured in public data.

**6. FX swaps — enormous, but not, on the evidence, the marginal net funding.** The gross numbers are larger than the December 2022 BIS article implies. Replicating Borio–McCauley–McGuire exactly on current BIS OTC derivatives data: USD-leg FX forwards, FX swaps and currency swaps outstanding were **$113,496.9bn at end-December 2025**, against $85,472.7bn at end-June 2022 (their "$85 trillion") and $71,355.8bn at end-2019. Positions with customers (other financial institutions + non-financial customers) were $71,777.2bn; halving as they do gives **$35,888.6bn of off-balance-sheet dollar debt owed by non-banks outside the US**, versus their $26.0trn at mid-2022 and $17.0trn at end-2016 — +37.9% in three and a half years. The step change was **H1 2025: +$15,568.9bn on the USD leg in six months**, coincident with the April 2025 dollar sell-off and the re-hedging of chronically underhedged foreign portfolios (Japanese life hedge ratios roughly 60%→40%, Dutch pension funds 30%→25%, per BIS QR December 2025 citing BoJ and DNB). 75.9% of the total book matured within one year at end-2025.

But the funding claim needs care, and here I part company with the obvious reading. The McGuire–von Peter identity says on-balance-sheet dollar assets in excess of dollar liabilities must be swap-funded. For banks outside the US that excess is **+$946.7bn at 2026-Q1**, up from −$101.0bn at 2019-Q4 and +$556.2bn a year ago; the four-quarter widening is **+$390.5bn**, i.e. 19% of the $2,040.1bn adjusted expansion of the dollar book had no reported dollar liability behind it. That is the honest maximum for marginal swap funding. It is *not* $3.75trn: the cross-border-only gap of $17,862.8bn − $14,115.0bn is offset by a $2,016.7bn local-currency-of-location deficit. And it may be far less than $390.5bn: on the all-reporter cut, where instrument detail exists, the entire widening sits in "other instruments" (securities and derivatives), while on loans and deposits — the actual credit-and-deposit book — four-quarter adjusted claims rose $1,426.3bn against liabilities of $1,386.0bn, a wedge of only $40.3bn. BIS does not publish instrument detail for the offshore cut, so the decisive test cannot be run.

**7. What this means for risk.** The rollover profile is severe regardless. MMF assets maturing in one business day or less were $2,431.0bn and within seven days $3,581.1bn of $8,388.3bn (May 2026). Three quarters of a $113.5trn USD swap book rolls inside a year. Intragroup funding of $6,204.9bn is stable only while the group is; the $795.5bn net claim of offshore offices on their US affiliates (corroborated by H.8's $803.8bn net due to related foreign offices) reverses in a stress, as it did in 2008. The vulnerability is concentrated in an NBFI deposit base that is one third US money funds and one third Cayman and UK vehicles, and in a hedging book whose size is a function of a hedge ratio that moved 20 points in three years.

## Cannot establish

**1. Whether FX swaps funded the marginal offshore dollar credit.** The decisive test is whether cross-border USD *loans and deposits* claims of banks outside the US grew faster than the corresponding liabilities. BIS publishes the instrument split (all instruments vs loans and deposits) only for the all-currency-type cut, which mixes in banks located in the US, and publishes no instrument split at all for the foreign-currency (offshore) cut — every attempt at Q.S.{C,L}.G.USD.F.5J.A.5A.A.5J.* returns 404. The offshore-only residual of $946.7bn therefore cannot be cleaned of securities and derivatives composition effects, which on the cut where the split does exist account for the entire gap. This is a one-dimension addition to an existing BIS table.

**2. The counterparty sector and country composition of the offshore dollar book.** As the project context notes and I independently confirmed by query, the foreign-currency residence cut supports no counterparty sector and no counterparty country dimension. Every sector figure I report is on the all-reporter basis, which includes roughly $5,403.5bn of cross-border USD liabilities booked by banks located in the US (derived as the difference between the all-currency-type and foreign-currency aggregates, and therefore also contaminated by dollarised reporting jurisdictions such as Panama and by unclassified-currency positions).

**3. Where US money-fund cash goes by country of counterparty.** The Federal Reserve's Enhanced Financial Accounts table on MMF holdings by country of issuance is frozen at 30 April 2021. The OFR Money Market Fund Monitor renders its country and counterparty series through a JavaScript client; no CSV or JSON endpoint is reachable without a browser session (data.financialresearch.gov returns 403 "Missing Authentication Token"; the chart pages contain no data URL). So the split of the $3,004.2bn MMF repo book and the $482.8bn unsecured bank-paper book between US and non-US borrowers cannot be stated on 2026 data. The 2021 vintage (58.7% non-US) is the last published figure.

**4. The current level of the cross-currency basis.** BIS QR December 2025 Graph 5.C plots the turnover-weighted three-month USD premium on a scale from 0 to −100bp but publishes no values, and I found no primary-source numeric series. The price of swap-based dollar funding — the single most informative real-time indicator of whether the swap channel is doing marginal work — is therefore not stated here.

**5. Maturity of offshore dollar liabilities.** LBS has no maturity dimension on the liability side. Remaining-maturity data exist in the consolidated banking statistics for *claims* only. The stability rating I give each funding source rests on instrument type and counterparty sector, not on measured tenor.

**6. Whether the Fed's "uninsured deposits" line captures deposits at US branches of foreign banks.** FSR Table 4.1 cites only FFIEC 031 (Call Report) as the source for that $7,608bn line. US branches and agencies of foreign banks file FFIEC 002, not 031, which implies the $1,563.0bn of deposits at foreign-related institutions (H.8, 5 Aug 2026) is largely outside the measure. I could not confirm this from the FSR's methodology, and state it as an inference.

**7. The Federal Reserve has published no updated quantitative treatment of non-US bank dollar funding since May 2021.** The May 2026 FSR's only reference on the subject points back to the box "Vulnerabilities in Global U.S. Dollar Funding Markets" in the May 2021 report. There is no US official-sector series for the offshore dollar funding gap.

**8. Chinese banks.** As the project context notes, they are structurally understated in LBS. The nationality series shows Chinese-headquartered banks with $1,554.5bn of cross-border USD claims against $736.4bn of liabilities and a four-quarter adjusted claims flow of +$133.5bn against *negative* liability flow of -$17.4bn — the largest proportional funding gap of any nationality. Whether that is a real off-balance-sheet position or a reporting artefact cannot be determined from published data.

## Challenges to project context

Three challenges.

(1) **The framing that a non-US bank "cannot conjure dollars the way a US bank conjures a domestic deposit" is half right and, as stated, misleading.** A bank in London writing a dollar loan credits a dollar deposit at itself — the accounting entry is identical, and the deposit is money by any functional test. What it lacks is a Fed account, which binds at *settlement*, not at *origination*. The empirical signature of this is that offshore banks are net creditors to the United States by $4,256.3bn in dollars (cross-border USD claims on US residents $8,547.0bn vs liabilities $4,290.7bn, 2026-Q1) and that foreign-related institutions in the US hold $1,149.2bn of cash assets against $2,935.3bn of total Fed reserve balances. They pre-position settlement capacity; they do not pre-fund loans. If the project's mechanism section says the offshore book requires dollars to be obtained before credit is written, that is wrong as accounting and should be rewritten as a settlement/liquidity constraint.

(2) **The companion stocks in the context ($17.86trn claims, $14.11trn liabilities) invite a $3.75trn "funding gap" that does not exist.** Both are cross-border only. Adding local dollar positions booked in the banks' own jurisdictions — $3,157.1bn claims, $5,173.8bn liabilities — collapses the gap to $946.7bn on the full reported dollar book ($21,112.4bn vs $20,165.6bn). Anyone differencing the two published stocks will overstate the unfunded position roughly fourfold. The local-USD-liability line, $5,173.8bn or 25.7% of the offshore dollar book, is itself a major finding that the cross-border framing suppresses entirely.

(3) **I would not assert that FX swaps are doing the marginal funding work, and the lens brief anticipates that conclusion.** The gross evidence is overwhelming ($113.5trn USD leg, $35.9trn non-bank missing debt, +$15.6trn in H1 2025). The *net funding* evidence is not. The on-balance-sheet gap for banks outside the US widened only $390.5bn over four quarters against a $2,040.1bn expansion, and on the one cut where BIS publishes instrument detail the entire equivalent widening sits in securities and derivatives rather than in loans and deposits (four-quarter adjusted L&D claims +$1,426.3bn vs L&D liabilities +$1,386.0bn — a $40.3bn wedge). The defensible statement is: the marginal *net* funding was NBFI wholesale deposits and repo (+$590.2bn) plus intragroup (+$414.3bn); FX swaps are the dominant *gross* exposure and the dominant rollover risk, and are growing far faster than the balance sheet, but the published data do not show them financing the incremental credit.

# The collateral and balance-sheet constraint: what actually binds an offshore dollar book, ranked — regulatory (leverage/LCR/NSFR/ring-fencing), collateral (Treasuries, FIMA, repo), and settlement (CLS, correspondent, Fedwire via US branches).

**Headline.** The offshore dollar book is 95.5% funded by on-balance-sheet dollar liabilities — the aggregate synthetic gap is only $947bn at 2026Q1 — and essentially all of it sits in two banking systems: banks located in Japan run a +$1,752bn dollar funding gap and banks in China +$691bn, against net dollar surpluses everywhere else. The binding constraint is NOT the NSFR: Basel has no currency-specific stable funding requirement at all, and the foreign-currency LCR is explicitly "not a standard but a monitoring tool" with no minimum threshold (BCBS d238 ¶212). The constraint is the price of yen-dollar swap funding and the settlement/ring-fencing chokepoint — and the US branch network is a net *importer* of $834bn from its own foreign offices, not a supplier to them.

## Mechanics

Four cases, entry by entry.

CASE A — offshore bank writes a dollar loan to its own depositor. Bank A (located outside the US): Dr Loan (USD) 100 / Cr Customer deposit (USD) 100. No Fed account touched, no correspondent involved, no collateral required. This is money creation by the identical double entry a US bank uses. In BIS LBS this appears as +100 USD claims and +100 USD liabilities; the funding gap is unchanged. The operator's premise that a non-US bank "cannot conjure dollars" is too strong here: it can, and $5,174bn of local (same-country) USD liabilities at non-US banks at 2026Q1 is the visible stock of exactly this.

CASE B — the depositor pays the money away. Bank A: Dr Customer deposit 100 / Cr Due to Bank B 100. Settlement occurs over Fedwire: A's US correspondent (frequently A's own New York branch) is debited 100 of Fed reserves; B's is credited. Only now does the Fed account bind — and only for the multilateral net, not the gross. This is where the operator's constraint actually lives: not in origination, in settlement.

CASE C — Bank A has no dollar deposit to lose and funds synthetically. Near leg: Dr USD nostro 100 / Cr JPY cash ~15,000. Forward leg: a derivative recognised at fair value ≈ 0 at inception. Then Dr USD loan 100 / Cr USD nostro 100. Net balance-sheet effect: USD assets +100, USD liabilities +0. No dollar debt instrument is recorded anywhere — the forward obligation to deliver 100 dollars sits in the derivative book. In LBS this is +100 claims, +0 liabilities: the funding gap widens by 100. That is precisely what the +$390.5bn four-quarter gap widening to 2026Q1 measures, and what Japan's +$1,752bn stock gap measures. Regulatory consequence: NSFR sees only the replacement cost (0% ASF on the net liability, 20% of gross negative RC at 100% RSF); capital sees RC plus add-on; BCBS-IOSCO requires no initial margin on a physically settled FX swap. The JPY 15,000 principal handed over IS the collateral, at full value with no haircut and no margin account.

CASE D — the group funds through its US branch. US branch of A: Dr Reserves at the Fed 100 / Cr Due to head office 100. This is H.8 Table 11 line 38, "Net due to related foreign offices," and at week ending 2026-08-05 it stands at $834.4bn — positive, i.e. the US offices OWE their foreign parents. The dollars flow offshore-to-onshore and terminate as $1,214.4bn of cash assets at the Fed. Head office is not drawing dollars out of the US branch to fund the offshore book; it is pushing dollars in.

CASE E — the official sector converts collateral. Under FIMA the entry would be: NY Fed Dr Repurchase agreements–foreign official / Cr Foreign official deposit. At 2026-08-19 that line is $0. The mirror entry that IS being made runs the other way: foreign officials lend $364,589mn to the Fed via reverse repo, and their custody holdings at the NY Fed — the collateral that would back any FIMA draw — fell $295,632mn over the year to $2,864,974mn.

## Analysis

**1. The funding side, measured.** The operator's instinct is right in principle and, in aggregate, already satisfied in practice. Using the BIS LBS foreign-currency residence cut (L_CURR_TYPE=F, i.e. USD positions of banks located outside the US), all positions (cross-border + local), 2026Q1: USD claims $21.112trn, USD liabilities $20.166trn. The on-balance-sheet net dollar position is **+$946.7bn — 4.5% of claims**. Ninety-five and a half percent of the offshore dollar book is funded by real dollar liabilities. The offshore system did gather the deposits.

But the aggregate is a lie of composition. Decomposed by the residence of the bank office (2026Q1, all positions, $trn): **Japan claims 3.514 / liabilities 1.763 → gap +1.752. China 0.977 / 0.286 → +0.691.** Against these: United Kingdom −0.259, Switzerland −0.239, France −0.184, Australia −0.169, Hong Kong −0.139, Sweden −0.116, Canada −0.061. Japan plus China is +$2,443bn of shortfall; the rest of the world nets −$1,496bn of surplus. Two banking systems carry the entire unfunded position of the global offshore dollar system, and both must close it synthetically — dollars bought spot against yen or renminbi and sold forward, or via cross-currency basis swaps.

Japan's gap is not new but it is at an all-time high and accelerating: +$686bn (2014Q4), +$1,020bn (2020Q1), +$1,420bn (2024Q1), **+$1,752bn (2026Q1)**. Crucially, the Japan series carries OBS_STATUS=A — no break flags, no pre-break values — across the whole 2024Q1–2026Q1 window, so its level *and* its changes are clean. The all-reporters aggregate carries OBS_STATUS=B in every quarter with pre-break restatements, so I used the BIS break-adjusted flow series (L_MEASURE=F) for changes rather than differencing stocks.

On that adjusted-flow basis, four quarters to 2026Q1, all reporting countries: **USD claims +$2,040.1bn, USD liabilities +$1,649.5bn, gap widening +$390.5bn.** Japan contributed +$181.8bn of the widening (claims +$334.3bn, liabilities +$152.4bn); China +$165.6bn (claims +$109.6bn, liabilities **−$56.1bn** — Chinese banks grew the dollar book while shrinking dollar funding). Japan and China are 89% of the world's increase in unfunded offshore dollar credit.

**2. The regulatory constraint — the hypothesis is wrong.** I was asked to nail down the NSFR treatment of short-dated FX swap funding as the likely binding constraint. It is not, and the reason is more fundamental than the treatment itself: **Basel contains no currency-specific stable funding requirement whatsoever.** BCBS d295 (Oct 2014) ¶50 applies the NSFR "to all internationally active banks on a consolidated basis"; a full-text search of d295 returns zero currency-mismatch provisions — the only currency references are trade-date payables. ¶51 asks banks to "actively monitor and control" liquidity by legal entity and currency, which is a supervisory expectation, not a ratio. On the LCR side, BCBS d238 (Jan 2013) ¶209 states the LCR is required in one single currency; ¶212 is explicit that **the foreign-currency LCR "is not a standard but a monitoring tool" with no internationally defined minimum threshold**, with a 5%-of-total-liabilities materiality trigger at ¶211. A non-US bank can therefore run an arbitrarily large USD funding gap and be fully LCR- and NSFR-compliant.

Where NSFR touches FX swaps at all, it touches replacement cost, not notional. d295 ¶25(c) assigns **0% ASF** to NSFR derivative liabilities net of derivative assets; ¶43(d) assigns 100% RSF to **20% of gross negative replacement cost** before deducting posted variation margin (national discretion may floor this at 5%). A $1trn FX swap book carrying $20bn of negative mark consumes roughly $4bn of RSF. Against notional, it is a rounding error — which is precisely the BIS "missing debt" point restated in regulatory arithmetic. The one respect in which it bites is direction: the negative replacement cost of a dollar-borrowing swap book grows exactly when the dollar strengthens, so the RSF add-on is procyclical against dollar borrowers and tightens in the stress it should cushion.

**3. Collateral — the swap principal is the collateral, and no margin is posted.** Under the BCBS-IOSCO margin framework, physically settled FX forwards and swaps are **exempt from initial margin**; only variation margin applies (per BCBS supervisory guidance on FX settlement risk). So the "collateral" for synthetic dollar funding is the yen principal delivered at the near leg — a full-value, haircut-free exchange rather than a posted margin pool. This answers the operator's dichotomy directly: for the FX swap channel, deposits and collateral are the same object.

The official-sector collateral channel is idle. Federal Reserve H.4.1, release 2026-08-20 (as of Wed 2026-08-19): **FIMA repo — "Repurchase agreements: Foreign official" — is $0mn.** Central bank liquidity swaps total $123mn (test draws). Meanwhile foreign official and international accounts hold **$364,589mn in the Fed's reverse repo pool** — they are net dollar *lenders* to the Fed. And the collateral pool itself is shrinking: securities held in custody for foreign official and international accounts were **$2,864,974mn** on Wednesday 2026-08-19 ($2,870,870mn weekly average; marketable USTs $2,592,082mn), **down $295,632mn year over year**. Conversion capacity exists and is unused; the underlying stock is contracting.

Private collateral capacity is the opposite story. OFR Short-term Funding Monitor, retrieved this session: tri-party repo $2,405.97bn (2026-08-18), DVP repo $2,750.68bn and GCF $242.98bn (2026-08-19) — roughly $5.4trn a day of Treasury repo. The eSLR final rule takes effect 2026-04-01 but relieves US GSIBs only, not FBO IHCs. The forward-dated squeeze is the SEC Treasury clearing mandate: cash by 2026-12-31, repo by 2027-06-30.

**4. The "no Fed account" framing partly dissolves — in the opposite direction.** Foreign banks' US offices are Fed account holders and dealers. Of the **26 primary dealers** on the NY Fed's list (retrieved 2026-08-21), **17 are foreign-owned**, and three — Bank of Montreal Chicago Branch, Bank of Nova Scotia New York Agency, Société Générale New York Branch — *are* the Fed-account-holding branch. Five are Japanese: Daiwa, Mizuho, Nomura, SMBC Nikko (added 2025-01-31) and **MUFG Securities Americas (added 2026-01-15)**.

But the flow runs inward. H.8 Table 11, week ending 2026-08-05 (release 2026-08-14): foreign-related institutions in the US hold total assets $3,527.6bn, cash assets $1,214.4bn, and are **net due TO related foreign offices $834.4bn** — the offshore group is pushing $834bn of dollars into its own US branches, which park them at the Fed. Year over year the branch network rotated out of reserves into credit: cash −$262.5bn, loans and leases +$184.1bn, C&I +$87.3bn, loans to nondepository financial institutions +$63.7bn to $522.1bn. The US branch is a settlement and reserve-holding node, not a funding source for the offshore book.

**5. Ring-fencing and settlement — the real chokepoint.** US LCR/NSFR (12 CFR 249.1(b)) apply to Board-regulated institutions — GSIB BHCs and DIs, Category II/III/IV — and **not to US branches or agencies of foreign banks**. Their only US liquidity requirement is Reg YY §252.157's 14-day branch liquidity buffer. The ring-fence is thin, but it exists, and it is aimed at exactly the intragroup artery. The BIS QR June 2026 special feature (Conway, Drehmann, Lovell, McGuire, Shirakami) quantifies that artery for the first time: of **$14,186bn of gross FX obligations settled per average day in April 2025**, PvP covered $5,157bn (36%) and **intragroup settlement covered $4,950bn (35%)**. The BIS names the failure mode explicitly — liquidity trapped in an entity by ring-fencing or a payment moratorium, disrupting the entire internal payment sequence. Residual: $2,167bn netted down to $337bn, $496bn under timing controls, and **$1,416bn (10%) settled gross bilateral with no mitigation**, of which $347bn was PvP-eligible anyway and 57% of the ineligible remainder failed because the counterparty had no PvP access.

**Ranked, what limits the offshore dollar book:** (1) the supply of gatherable dollar liabilities — the actual binding constraint, and it has not bound; (2) for Japan and China specifically, the price and tenor of FX swap dollars, i.e. the cross-currency basis, a price constraint not a regulatory one; (3) intraday settlement and the intragroup payment chain, with ring-fencing as the tail risk; (4) counterparty credit lines and large-exposure limits on the swap providers; (5) home-country consolidated leverage ratios — and Japan's is loosened, with the BoJ-deposit exclusion made permanent 2024-03-31; (6) the NSFR, a distant sixth and currency-blind; (7) official collateral conversion, which is not constraining because it is not being used.

## Cannot establish

1. **The instrument split of offshore dollar liabilities does not exist.** The BIS publishes no instrument dimension on the foreign-currency (L_CURR_TYPE=F) cut. I tested L_INSTR = G (loans and deposits) and D (debt securities) against every position type (cross-border, local, all) and every one returns HTTP 404 — only L_INSTR=A is published. So the $20.166trn of offshore dollar liabilities at 2026Q1 CANNOT be split into deposits (money) versus debt securities and repo (not money) from LBS. Any figure purporting to state offshore dollar deposit money is an estimate, not a measurement.

2. **The foreign-currency cut has no nationality dimension either.** I queried the parent-country dimension with a wildcard and exactly one value is published: 5J (all). So the funding gap cannot be attributed to a banking system by ownership — only by the residence of the booking office. Japan's +$1,752bn is offices located in Japan, which includes foreign banks' Tokyo branches and excludes Japanese banks' London, New York and Cayman books. The UK's −$259bn and the Cayman Islands' +$0.0bn are net of whatever Japanese and other groups book there. Part of Japan's measured gap may be Tokyo funding its own overseas branches, which is a real dollar-raising requirement in Tokyo but not a claim on ultimate borrowers.

3. **Singapore is invisible.** On the same cut, SG, SA, ID, MY, TR, RU and CW return NaN under confidentiality. The $946.7bn aggregate gap therefore excludes Singapore entirely — one of the largest offshore dollar booking centres. The true global gap is unknown and the sign of the omission is not determinable.

4. **There is no published currency-specific LCR or NSFR for any banking system.** Because the foreign-currency LCR is a monitoring tool (BCBS d238 ¶212) with no defined minimum, no jurisdiction publishes bank-level or system-level USD LCR. It is impossible to say from public data how much USD HQLA any non-US banking system holds against its 30-day USD outflows.

5. **The counterparty of the dollar shortfall cannot be identified.** BIS OTC derivatives statistics give the FX notional ($155trn at end-June 2025, ~$100trn under one year) but not who is short dollars against which currency at which maturity. There is no published series mapping the $1,752bn Japanese gap to the institutions supplying those dollars.

6. **The end-December 2025 BIS OTC derivatives release could not be located.** I checked otc_hy2604/2605/2606 — all 404. The latest confirmed release remains end-June 2025, published 2025-12-08. Do not assume a 2026 vintage exists for FX notionals.

7. **The current level of the 3-month USD/JPY cross-currency basis is not established here.** The BoJ FSR (April 2026) shows dollar funding premiums "generally at low levels" to end-March 2026, with a temporary widening around year-end. I could not obtain a primary quote for August 2026 — Bloomberg-sourced levels are behind a terminal and I will not substitute a plausible number.

8. **FFIEC 002 aggregate detail was not obtained.** I used H.8 Table 11, which covers foreign-related institutions (branches, agencies, Edge Act corporations and IBFs) on a weekly sample basis. A quarterly FFIEC 002 aggregation of US branches and agencies alone — which would permit a currency split of branch assets that H.8 does not offer — was not retrieved.

9. **The size of the FX swap book actually used to close the Japanese gap is not directly observable.** The $1,752bn is a residual: the amount of dollar assets not funded by dollar liabilities. It could in principle be left unhedged (open FX risk) rather than swapped. I have no measurement of the hedged share; the BoJ FSR asserts stable funding coverage but its chart aggregates deposits, bonds and >1yr swaps into a single "stable" bar without publishing the split.

## Challenges to project context

**1. The claim that offshore dollar creation is "outside every monetary aggregate in the world — not euro-area M3 (euro-denominated, resident-based), not Japanese M3" is wrong on both counts, verified against the primary methodology this session.**
- ECB: "Holdings by euro area residents of liquid assets denominated in foreign currency can be close substitutes for euro-denominated assets. Therefore, the monetary aggregates include such assets if they are held with MFIs located in the euro area." (ECB, "The ECB's definition of euro area monetary aggregates", retrieved 2026-08-21). Euro-area M3 is resident-based but it is NOT euro-denominated-only. A dollar deposit held by a German corporate at a German bank IS in M3.
- BoJ: the Guide to Japan's Money Stock Statistics (October 2025) lists "Foreign Currency Deposits" as a named component of "Time Deposits etc." inside both M2 and M3.
The correct, narrower statement is: dollar deposits at non-US banks are outside all monetary aggregates when held by *non-residents* of the bank's location country. That maps to the cross-border USD liabilities of $14.115trn (2026Q1), not to the full $20.166trn. Local USD liabilities of $5.174trn are partly captured by the local aggregate. This matters because it shrinks the "invisible money" claim by up to a quarter and gives it a defensible boundary.

**2. The headline conflates claims growth with money creation.** "+$1.90trn offshore dollar credit creation" is an asset-side number. Money is the liability side. On the all-positions foreign-currency cut, break-adjusted, four quarters to 2026Q1: claims +$2,040.1bn but liabilities only +$1,649.5bn. Roughly 81% of the credit extension was matched by new dollar liability creation; the residual $390.5bn was funded by converting other currencies through the swap market and created no dollar liability at all. And LBS liabilities include debt securities and repo, which are not money and which the F cut refuses to separate. So offshore dollar money creation over the year is AT MOST $1.65trn, is probably materially less, and is not measurable more precisely from published data. Reporting the claims number as the money-creation number overstates it.

**3. The lens brief's own hypothesis — that the NSFR treatment of short-dated FX swap funding "is a hard constraint on synthetic dollar funding and may be the binding one" — is wrong, and the reason matters more than the answer.** It is not that the NSFR treats FX swaps leniently; it is that Basel has NO currency-specific stable funding requirement whatsoever. BCBS d295 contains zero currency-mismatch provisions and applies the ratio consolidated in one currency (¶50). The foreign-currency LCR is explicitly "not a standard but a monitoring tool" (d238 ¶212). Where the NSFR touches FX swaps it does so through replacement cost (0% ASF on the net derivative liability, ¶25(c); 100% RSF on 20% of gross negative RC, ¶43(d)), never through notional — which is exactly why the dollar obligation is missing from the ratio as well as from the balance sheet. Do not build the report around NSFR as the binding constraint.

**4. "A bank writing a dollar loan outside the US must fund it. It cannot conjure dollars the way a US bank conjures a domestic deposit" is overstated at origination and correct only at settlement.** When an offshore bank lends dollars to a customer who holds a dollar account at the same bank, the double entry is identical to a US bank's and no Fed account is touched. The stock of exactly this is measurable: $5.174trn of local (same-country) USD liabilities at non-US banks, 2026Q1. The Fed account binds on the *net* transfer out, not on origination — and even then, the settlement is usually done by the group's own US branch, which is a Fed account holder. Framing the constraint as origination rather than settlement will lead the operator to the wrong hedges.

**5. The "no Fed account" framing should be inverted, not merely qualified.** Foreign banks' US offices are Fed account holders and 17 of 26 primary dealers are foreign-owned (three of them literally branches or agencies). But H.8 shows the net flow runs the wrong way for the funding story: US foreign-related institutions are net due TO related foreign offices $834.4bn (week ending 2026-08-05). Head office funds the branch, not the reverse. Any narrative in which offshore books are funded out of US branch access to the Fed is contradicted by the data.

**6. Add a caution the context does not carry: never treat the aggregate offshore funding gap as representative.** The $946.7bn aggregate is the residual of +$2,443bn (Japan and China) against −$1,496bn (everyone else). A shock to yen-dollar or renminbi-dollar swap pricing does not hit the offshore system proportionately; it hits two banking systems, one of which is the operator's employer.

# WHO IS TAKING THE CREDIT — counterparty sector, region and instrument decomposition of offshore USD credit creation (BIS LBS counterparty-sector cuts, BIS global liquidity indicators, BIS QR March 2026 Box C, Fed FEDS Notes on the basis trade)

**Headline.** The borrower is not the EME corporate — it is the fund complex. Of the $2,375.5bn break- and FX-adjusted expansion in cross-border USD bank claims over the four quarters to 2026Q1, non-bank financial institutions took $854.0bn (35.9% of the total, 64.9% of everything lent to non-banks), growing 17.2% y/y; EMDE non-financial corporates took $8.7bn (0.4%), and EMDE non-financial sectors as a whole have had zero or negative dollar credit growth for five straight quarters. Emerging Asia's dollar credit has now contracted for fifteen consecutive quarters. The single largest recipient region is the United States itself ($974.6bn, 41.0% of the entire expansion), followed by Cayman Islands NBFIs ($249.2bn, 10.5%). This channel connects directly to the private-credit/fund-finance/basis-trade map, not to the real economy — and it is a bank-balance-sheet phenomenon, not a bond-market one, which is precisely why it qualifies as money creation.

## Mechanics

Take the modal transaction behind the $854bn: a London or Tokyo-headquartered bank's London branch books a USD reverse repo or a term facility to a Cayman-domiciled fund.

Entry 1 — the credit. Lending bank, London: DR reverse repo / loan to Cayman NBFI (asset, USD). CR customer deposit, Cayman NBFI (liability, USD). This is the money creation the operator is asking about. It is a genuine dollar deposit, it is a liability of a bank with no Fed account, and it appears in no monetary aggregate anywhere. At this instant the operator's constraint is NOT binding: the bank has conjured the dollar, exactly as a US bank would.

Entry 2 — the constraint bites at settlement, not at origination. The fund does not hold the deposit. It buys a Treasury from a US primary dealer. Lending bank: DR customer deposit (liability extinguished). CR due to correspondent / reserve balance at its US correspondent (asset extinguished). The correspondent's reserve account at the Fed is debited and the dealer's bank's is credited. The offshore bank has now converted a self-created deposit into a claim on Fed money it does not have. That is the moment it must fund. This is why 41% of the expansion landing on US residents matters mechanically: credit that leaves the offshore circuit cannot be funded by its own deposit creation.

Entry 3 — the funding leg. Three options, and the LBS liability side is where the other researchers should look: (a) borrow USD interbank offshore — cross-border USD claims on banks grew $913.0bn over the same four quarters, +10.52%, which is the interbank leg of exactly this; (b) issue USD CP/CD into US money funds — this is the direct join to Fed FSR Table 4.1, where MMF assets of $7,746bn and CP of $1,368bn are on the other side of the ledger; (c) an FX swap: sell yen spot for dollars, buy them back forward. Under (c) the entry is DR USD nostro / CR JPY nostro plus an off-balance-sheet forward — the dollar liability is off-balance-sheet and does not appear in the LBS at all. The bank has a dollar liability that no dollar statistic records.

Entry 4 — the closed-loop case, which is where money creation is fully self-sustaining. If the fund's dollars instead pay another offshore entity that banks in the same offshore system — a Cayman fund paying a Luxembourg fund that deposits with a Frankfurt bank — no Fed reserve moves. The deposit simply changes owner within the eurodollar circuit. This is the pure case the operator's instinct says should be impossible, and it is not impossible: it is limited only by how much of the payment chain stays outside US-resident counterparties. The 87.9% advanced-economy / 39.2% US-resident split of the claims stock is the best available proxy for how large that closed loop is, and the honest reading is that it is large but shrinking as US counterparties take a rising share.

Entry 5 — the collateral point the operator raised. For the Cayman block the collateral IS the asset being financed: the fund pledges the Treasury it just bought back to the bank under the repo. Collateral is created by the same transaction that creates the credit. There is no pre-existing deposit or collateral requirement in any binding sense — only a settlement-timing requirement. That is why the constraint shows up as a funding-market phenomenon (repo rates, FX swap bases, quarter-end) rather than as a credit-origination constraint.

## Analysis

## 1. The sector arithmetic, verified

Working in BIS LBS cross-border USD claims (dataflow WS_LBS_D_PUB, all reporting countries, all currency types — see §5 for why the project's foreign-currency cut cannot be used here), the four-quarter FX- and break-adjusted flow to 2026Q1 decomposes as follows (USD bn, my summation of MEASURE=F quarterly flows, never differenced stocks):

| | Flow | % of total |
|---|---|---|
| All sectors | 2,375.5 | 100.0 |
| Banks (interbank) | 913.0 | 38.4 |
| Non-banks | 1,315.0 | 55.4 |
| — NBFIs | **854.0** | **35.9** |
| — Non-financial sectors | 455.8 | 19.2 |
| —— of which NFCs | 233.3 | 9.8 |
| —— of which government | 213.2 | 9.0 |

Annual growth at 2026Q1 (BIS-calculated, MEASURE=G): all sectors +12.23%, banks +10.52%, non-banks +12.92%, **NBFIs +17.18%**, non-financial sectors +9.43%, NFCs +8.86%, government +15.58%. NBFI growth has accelerated from +7.77% at 2024Q4 to +17.18%; NFC growth troughed at +4.15% in 2025Q1 and has only recovered to +8.86%.

So the answer to the operator's lens question is unambiguous and quantified: **NBFIs are 36% of the total acceleration and 65% of everything lent to non-banks.** Two thirds of the non-bank story is funds, vehicles and financial intermediaries; one third is the real economy, and half of that third is sovereigns.

## 2. Geography — this credit is not going offshore in any meaningful sense

Stock at 2026Q1, cross-border USD claims by counterparty residence (total $21,796.9bn):

- United States: $8,547.0bn (39.2%)
- Advanced economies: $19,159.6bn (87.9%)
- EMDEs: $2,333.9bn (10.7%)
- Cayman Islands: $1,755.3bn (8.1%)

Within the $5,829.2bn of USD claims on NBFIs: United States $2,190.1bn (37.6%), Cayman $1,309.3bn (22.5%), United Kingdom $768.1bn (13.2%), Luxembourg $193.9bn, Ireland $189.7bn. Those five are 79.8% of the total. Advanced economies are 93.1%; EMDEs are 3.8%.

Flows tell the same story more sharply. Of the $2,375.5bn four-quarter expansion, US residents took $974.6bn (41.0%) and all EMDEs took $126.9bn (5.3%). Of the $854.0bn NBFI flow, advanced economies took $777.8bn (91%) and EMDEs $40.1bn (4.7%); the US alone took $328.6bn and Cayman $249.2bn — together 68% of the NBFI flow.

This matters for the project's framing. "Offshore dollar credit creation" accurately describes *where the credit is created* (non-US banks, no Fed account — the operator's crux). It badly describes *where the credit lands*. Two fifths of the expansion is a claim on a US resident.

## 3. Emerging markets are deleveraging in dollars, with one exception

The BIS global liquidity indicators (WS_GLI) — the closest published direct read on dollar credit to non-banks outside the US — put the stock at $14,742.8bn at 2026Q1, +7.30% y/y, of which EMDEs $4,495.5bn (30.5%). Regional growth at 2026Q1:

- Emerging Africa & Middle East: **+17.23%** ($1,638.3bn)
- Emerging Europe: +7.22% ($398.2bn)
- Emerging Latin America & Caribbean: +6.27% ($1,374.1bn)
- Emerging Asia & Pacific: **−2.32%** ($1,084.8bn)

Emerging Asia has printed negative dollar credit growth every quarter since 2022Q3 — fifteen consecutive quarters. China −7.05%, India −5.18% at 2026Q1. Against that, Saudi Arabia +25.04%, Argentina +24.18%.

In the LBS the same picture is starker still: cross-border USD claims on EMDE *non-financial sectors* grew −0.51%, −0.45%, −1.81%, −0.50%, +0.39% over the five quarters to 2026Q1. EMDE NFCs +2.12%. EMDE governments +21.53%, but from a small $102.1bn base. The classic 2010-2015 "EME dollar borrowing" transmission channel is not merely absent from this episode — for the largest EME region it is running in reverse.

## 4. Bank credit, not bond credit — which is what makes it money creation

Decomposing the GLI $14,742.8bn: international debt securities $8,059.8bn (+6.59%), bank loans $6,683.0bn (+8.18%). Bank loans are the faster leg and have accelerated harder (from +1.37% at 2024Q4 to a peak +9.89% at 2025Q4).

More telling for the lens: of the $8,059.8bn of USD international debt securities owed by non-banks outside the US, $4,436.8bn is owed by non-financial sectors (+8.78% y/y). The residual — non-bank *financial* issuers — is $3,623.0bn and, by implied decomposition of the two growth rates, is growing roughly **+3.9%**. So NBFIs are *not* driving dollar bond issuance. The NBFI surge is entirely a bank balance-sheet event.

That distinction is the crux for this project. Bond issuance to an NBFI transfers existing deposits; a bank loan or reverse repo to an NBFI creates a new deposit. The channel accelerating is the one that creates money.

## 5. What these NBFIs actually are — as far as anyone can say

The BIS itself is explicit that the sectoral breakdown "is too coarse to distinguish among NBFIs" (BIS Quarterly Review, March 2026, Box C, Aldasoro and Hardy). It infers activity from geography, and its readings are:

- **US banks ↔ Cayman**: securitisation and prime brokerage.
- **Japanese banks ↔ Caribbean centres**: holdings of securities issued by securitisation vehicles, specifically CLOs. Note this is a *securities holding*, not a loan — relevant to the operator's own institution. Japan is the third-largest creditor to NBFIs globally: $1,366.5bn of cross-border claims on NBFIs (all currencies) at 2026Q1, behind the UK ($2,579.9bn) and the US ($2,025.4bn), ahead of France ($1,165.3bn) and Germany ($1,106.0bn).
- **US ↔ UK**: central clearing — CCPs for derivatives and repo. A meaningful share of "credit to NBFIs in the UK" is exposure to clearing houses, not credit to a leveraged borrower.
- **Euro-area, Canadian, Australian banks ↔ Ireland/Luxembourg/Netherlands**: investment funds and asset managers.

The Cayman block can be corroborated from the US side. Federal Reserve FEDS Notes (15 October 2025) finds Cayman-domiciled hedge funds held $1.85trn of Treasuries at end-2024, up $1trn since 2022, absorbing 37% of net note and bond issuance over Jan-2022 to Dec-2024, and that adjusted for sponsored DVP repo Cayman is the largest foreign holder of Treasuries — exceeding China, Japan and the UK combined. A later FEDS Note (22 June 2026, data to September 2025) puts hedge fund gross Treasury exposures at $4.0trn, the basis trade at ~$830bn (double the early-2020 peak), swap-spread arbitrage at ~$305bn, and hedge fund repo borrowing at $3.0trn, with the top 50 funds holding ~90% of exposures.

Cayman NBFI claims are consistent with this: $1,309.3bn total, of which $1,136.0bn (86.8%) is loans-and-deposits, growing +24.63% y/y at 2026Q1 after +44.77% at 2025Q4. Globally, loans-and-deposits claims on NBFIs grew +18.74%.

The unavoidable caveat: BIS reporting guidelines (Table 2.2) put **repurchase agreements inside "loans/deposits"**, together with the cash leg of securities lending. So the $4,158.3bn of USD loan-and-deposit claims on NBFIs cannot be split between reverse repo financing a basis trade, a subscription line to a private-credit fund, a NAV loan, and a warehouse facility. Direct read-across to the project's money-like-liabilities table: securities-lending cash collateral ($1,201bn there) and repo ($5,887bn) are the *mirror* of part of this asset block.

## 6. The residence/nationality distortion — measurable in bonds, not in banking

BIS QR March 2026 restates international debt securities by ultimate parent. For advanced-economy issuers, NBFI IDS falls from $12,369bn (immediate issuer) to $8,559bn (ultimate parent) while NFCs rise from $3,830bn to $6,363bn — 41% of AE NFC bond debt is issued through NBFI affiliates. For EME issuers, NBFI IDS falls from $1,126bn to $399bn and NFCs rise from $804bn to $1,509bn — 48% via NBFI affiliates.

Roughly a third of apparent AE "NBFI borrowing" and two thirds of apparent EME "NBFI borrowing" in bonds is really a corporate borrowing through an SPV. If a comparable distortion applies to the banking data, the NBFI share of dollar credit is overstated — but the BIS states plainly that bank-NBFI links "are recorded on a residence basis and do not identify NBFIs' nationality," so the correction cannot be made. Working against that: the Cayman/US/UK concentration and the loan-and-deposit composition point to genuine leveraged financial borrowers, not conduits for operating companies.

## Cannot establish

**1. No NBFI sub-sector, anywhere.** The BIS states it directly: the sectoral breakdown "is too coarse to distinguish among NBFIs" (QR March 2026, Box C). Hedge funds, money funds, insurers, pension funds, securitisation vehicles, CCPs and private-credit funds are one undifferentiated bucket. The split of the $854bn NBFI flow between basis-trade repo, fund finance (subscription lines, NAV loans), CLO warehouse lines and CCP exposure cannot be established from published data. Geography is the only inference available and it is an inference.

**2. Repo cannot be separated from lending.** BIS guidelines put repurchase agreements and securities-lending cash legs inside "loans/deposits" (Table 2.2, verified this session). Instrument codes for repo (N), debt securities (D) and credit (B) return no data at the sector cut — I tested each. So within the $4,158.3bn of USD loan-and-deposit claims on NBFIs, and the $1,136.0bn on Cayman NBFIs specifically, secured financing of a leveraged arb book is indistinguishable from an unsecured facility to a fund.

**3. Currency × reporting country × counterparty sector is not jointly published.** Newly verified this session and an extension of the project's known limits: with DENOM=USD you may have counterparty sector but only REP_CTY=5A (all reporting countries); with a named reporting country you may have sector but only DENOM=TO1 (all currencies). Consequence: **the sector decomposition in this report cannot be restricted to non-US-resident banks.** The $2,375.5bn total includes US-resident banks' cross-border USD claims; the project's foreign-currency headline ($1,899.7bn, verified this session) excludes them, and that cut has no sector dimension at all — I confirmed the API rejects it. The residual, ~$476bn, is the approximate US-resident contribution. Best available bound: US-resident banks hold $2,025.4bn of the world's $11,736.4bn cross-border NBFI claims (all currencies), i.e. ~17%, so ~83% of the NBFI creditor base is non-US-resident. That is a bound, not a decomposition.

**4. No nationality view for bank claims on NBFIs.** The BIS restates bond issuance by ultimate parent and the correction is large (a third of AE, two thirds of EME apparent NBFI bond borrowing is really corporates through SPVs). For banking, the BIS states the links "are recorded on a residence basis and do not identify NBFIs' nationality." So I cannot tell you how much of the $854bn NBFI flow is an operating company borrowing through a financial subsidiary. This is the single largest unquantifiable risk to my headline.

**5. Trade finance is unmeasurable and probably negligible.** No BIS series decomposes cross-border bank credit into trade finance; the LBS has no such instrument or purpose dimension. The only current primary read is US-specific (Fed FEDS Notes, 8 May 2026), which puts US commercial letters of credit at roughly $15bn in 2025 on a long declining trend — four orders of magnitude below the flows in question. I will not put a number on global dollar trade finance because none exists. That the operator's lens listed it as a candidate category is reasonable; the data says it cannot be one of the drivers.

**6. Confidentiality holes, confirmed at the sector cut.** Hong Kong and China return NaN (OBS_CONF=Q) for cross-border claims on NBFIs; Singapore returns no series at all. This is the project's known confidentiality limit reproducing exactly in the counterparty-sector data, and it bites hardest where Asian NBFI activity would sit.

**7. Break in the sector series at 2023Q1.** The NFC and government stock series step up discontinuously at 2023Q1 (NFC $1,588bn → $2,271bn; government $561bn → $1,015bn) — a reporting expansion, not lending. All growth and flow figures I report are BIS break-adjusted; no stock level should be compared across that quarter.

## Challenges to project context

**1. The word "offshore" is doing misleading work, and this is the finding most worth acting on.** The project treats this channel as dollar credit created outside the US and, by implication, circulating outside it. Creation, yes — non-US banks with no Fed account. Destination, no: 41.0% of the four-quarter expansion ($974.6bn) is a claim on a US resident, and US residents hold 39.2% of the cross-border USD claims stock. Adding Cayman ($1,755.3bn stock; $249.2bn of NBFI flow), the share landing on entities in or immediately adjacent to the US financial system is over half. The dominant use of this credit is financing US asset markets — Treasuries above all, via the basis trade. It is offshore-created, onshore-deployed. That changes the mechanical story materially: credit whose proceeds pay US residents forces the creating bank onto Fed money at settlement, so the funding constraint the operator identified binds much harder than a pure eurodollar-loop model implies.

**2. The BIS's own headline figure for the NBFI share does not reconcile with its published data.** QR March 2026 Box C says banks' claims on NBFIs rose "from 7% of overall lending in 2015 to 24% in 2025." I computed both from the API: 16.9% (2015Q4) → 24.6% (2026Q1) on all cross-border claims, or 14.3% → 25.6% on loans and deposits. The end point matches; the start point does not, on either basis, and 7% sits below the y-axis floor of their own accompanying chart (10.0). Their footnote — "based on a varying number of reporting countries in respective quarters" — is the plausible cause. The practical consequence: the rise is real and large but roughly half as dramatic as the BIS sentence implies (+7.7pp or +11.3pp, not +17pp). Do not carry the 7% figure into project outputs.

**3. The project's nine-channel map should reclassify this channel as adjacent to, not separate from, the private-credit and fund-finance map.** Two thirds of everything lent to non-banks in this expansion went to NBFIs. The counterparty concentration (US, Cayman, UK, Luxembourg, Ireland = 79.8% of the NBFI stock) is the fund and securitisation complex. If the project holds offshore dollar credit creation and the private-credit/NBFI map as distinct channels, it is double-mapping the same balance sheets and understating the correlation between them in a stress scenario.

**4. The EME framing implied by the lens brief should be retired for this episode, not merely qualified.** The brief anticipated splits between EME sovereigns, EME corporates and AE NBFIs. The data does not support treating EME borrowers as a meaningful component: 5.3% of the flow, and EME non-financial sectors have had zero-to-negative dollar credit growth for five quarters while emerging Asia is in its fifteenth consecutive quarter of contraction. The only live EME story is the Gulf (+17.23%, Saudi Arabia +25.04%) and EMDE sovereigns (+21.53% on a $102.1bn base) — a commodity-fiscal story, not a dollar-funding-vulnerability story.

**5. One caution on the project's "outside every monetary aggregate" claim.** It holds on the liability side and I have not disturbed it. But note that when the borrower is a US resident, the asset created is inside US credit aggregates (Z.1 records it as rest-of-world lending to US sectors) even though the deposit created is outside every money measure. The channel is invisible as money and visible as credit — which is a sharper and more defensible statement than "invisible" alone.

# Japan and China: the two least-visible drivers of offshore dollar credit creation, and the funding side that binds them

**Headline.** On one consistent basis (BIS LBS by bank nationality, USD, cross-border, 2026Q1), Japanese banks run a +$1,953bn and Chinese banks a +$818bn excess of dollar claims over dollar liabilities — together $2,771bn against a whole-system net of only $2,278bn. Every other named banking system is roughly flat or net dollar-LONG (US −$585bn, France −$304bn). Japan and China are not merely large participants in offshore dollar credit; they ARE the offshore dollar system's structural short, and neither funds it with dollar deposits: Japan funds it off-balance-sheet through FX and currency swaps against yen, China funds it from an onshore FX deposit base ($1.02trn, PBoC, Sep-2025) that appears nowhere in any BIS statistic.

## Mechanics

**Japan — the swap-funded entry chain (why the funding is invisible on the balance sheet).**

1. Tokyo HQ holds yen deposits as a liability; weak domestic loan demand leaves the asset side in BoJ reserves and JGBs. This is the "loan-to-deposit gap" the BoJ names in FSR April 2026 fn.16.
2. FX swap, spot leg: the bank delivers ¥ and receives $. Entries at the Japanese bank: **Dr USD nostro (asset) / Cr JPY reserves-at-BoJ (asset down)**. No dollar liability is booked. The forward leg — the obligation to redeliver dollars and take back yen — is a derivative, carried at fair value only, with the notional off-balance-sheet.
3. This is the crux: the funding shows up in LBS as *the absence of a dollar liability*, which is exactly why claims exceed liabilities by $1,601bn on the all-positions perimeter. **The $1.6trn gap IS the swap book.** It is not a data error; it is the correct representation of off-balance-sheet funding.
4. Overseas branch lends: **Dr USD loan to borrower / Cr USD deposit of borrower at the branch**. Here, and only here, is genuine eurodollar money creation — a new dollar deposit outside US M2, created by a bank with no Fed account for that entity.
5. The constraint bites at settlement. When the borrower spends the deposit away from the branch, the branch must settle in Fed money. Japanese megabanks solve this through NY branches holding Fed master accounts — so they are not wholly outside the Fed. Their NY local book (CBS local claims in local currency, **$1,290.6bn** globally at 2026Q1) is excluded from LBS on *both* sides, which is why the LBS gap and the true net dollar need are not the same number.
6. Counterparty to the swap: someone long dollars wanting yen — FX reserve managers, non-Japanese banks (BIS: their net yen borrowing via FX derivatives tripled since 2017), dealers. Japanese life insurers are on the *same* side as the banks (also buying dollars, hedged), so the marginal yen-taker is foreign. That is the concentration risk.

**China — the deposit-funded entry chain (why the funding is outside the statistical perimeter).**

1. A Chinese exporter is paid in dollars and deposits them at, say, BoC Shanghai. Entries: **Dr claim on US correspondent (asset) / Cr FX deposit of exporter (liability)**. This is a *local* position in *foreign* currency, inside mainland China. **It is reported to no BIS statistic** — China's LBS submission is SAFE's external-position return, and local positions are by construction excluded. PBoC sizes the pool at **$1.02trn** (Sep-2025).
2. Onshore FX loans absorb only **$554.4bn**. The ~$470bn surplus is the raw material.
3. Intragroup transfer to the offshore arm: **Dr placement with BoC Hong Kong / Cr FX deposit funding**. At this point, and only at this point, the position becomes visible — as a cross-border USD claim of a CN-parent bank.
4. Offshore arm lends to a Belt-and-Road or Asian borrower: **Dr USD loan / Cr USD deposit**. Offshore dollar credit created.
5. Net effect in the statistics: CN-parent cross-border USD claims $1,554.5bn against liabilities $736.4bn, a **$818.1bn gap that has no visible funding counterpart** — because the funding never crossed a border. Chinese banks are not short dollars; they are long onshore dollars that the international statistics cannot see.

**The contrast, stated plainly.** The operator's dichotomy — "deposits or collateral" — maps exactly onto the two countries. **China uses deposits** (onshore, invisible). **Japan uses collateral** (yen assets exchanged through an FX swap, off-balance-sheet). Both produce offshore dollar credit that lands outside every monetary aggregate. Only one of them is refinancing-risk-bearing: China's funding is a sticky domestic deposit base; Japan's is a rollable derivative whose price is the cross-currency basis and whose counterparty is foreign.

## Analysis

**The operator's premise is correct, and the data lets us say exactly which constraint binds where.**

A bank outside the US cannot conjure a dollar the way a US bank conjures a domestic deposit. It must acquire an existing claim on the US banking system first. The BIS locational banking statistics let you observe the resulting imbalance directly, because they report both dollar claims and dollar liabilities. Where claims exceed liabilities, the difference must be funded by something that is not a dollar liability — i.e. off-balance-sheet, or outside the statistical perimeter.

I put every published bank nationality on one basis (LBS nationality, USD, cross-border, 2026Q1, series `Q.S.{C,L}.A.USD.A.<PARENT>.A.5A.A.5J.N`). Net dollar-short: **Japan +$1,953bn, China +$818bn, Canada +$568bn**. Net dollar-long: **US −$585bn, France −$304bn, Sweden −$115bn, Germany −$43bn**. Switzerland is exactly flat. The system total is +$2,278bn; Japan and China alone are +$2,771bn. Read that carefully: the rest of the offshore system, in aggregate, is a net collector of dollar deposits that it on-lends. The dollar *shortage* is concentrated in two national banking systems, and they solve it in two completely different ways.

**Japan: the swap-funded model, and it is still widening under BoJ normalisation.**

On the fuller perimeter (cross-border plus local foreign-currency), Japanese-headquartered banks hold **$4,054.9bn of USD claims against $2,453.8bn of USD liabilities — a gap of $1,601.1bn** at 2026Q1. I built the liability side as cross-border ($1,664.1bn) plus local-FCY ($789.7bn); BIS does not publish an all-positions USD liability series for a single nationality, so that sum is my construction, not a published series.

The time series is the finding. The gap was **$512.8bn at 2008Q4**, $994.9bn at 2019Q4, **$1,280.9bn at 2023Q4**, and $1,601.1bn at 2026Q1. It has tripled since the GFC — and, critically, **+$320bn of that widening has come in the nine quarters since 2023Q4**, which is precisely the BoJ normalisation window. I verified the policy path from BIS CBPOL (`M.JP`): −0.10% through Feb-2024, 0.05% Apr-2024, 0.25% Aug-2024, 0.50% Feb-2025, 0.75% Dec-2025, **1.00% by Jun-2026**.

The naive expectation — BoJ hikes, yen carry unwinds, Japanese banks retrench from dollars — is wrong, and the covered-interest-parity algebra says why. The all-in cost of dollars via an FX swap is the USD rate *plus the basis*; the yen leg cancels. Raising the yen policy rate does not raise the dollar cost. What it does do is compress the USD/JPY differential, which **lowers the cost of hedging** foreign assets back into yen — making FX-hedged dollar asset accumulation *more* attractive, not less. The result is more dollar demand through swaps, and a wider funding gap. The flows corroborate: Japanese USD claims grew in seven of the last ten quarters while Japanese USD cross-border liabilities shrank in six of ten (2026Q1: claims +$102.9bn, liabilities −$3.1bn).

The BoJ says the mechanism itself, in its own words. Financial System Report April 2026, footnote 16, states that part of major banks' loan-to-deposit gap funds "foreign currency-denominated overseas loans, such as FX and currency swaps." That is the yen deposit base being swapped into the dollar loan book, stated by the central bank. Chart IV-3-14 names "Medium- to long-term FX and currency swaps" as a distinct funding block alongside corporate bonds and deposits, on a scale topping out near $1.2trn (internationally active banks, 2025Q4). Chart IV-3-15 decomposes major banks' dollar funding cost into three legs: FX and currency swaps, repos, deposits.

Market structure confirms the dependence. In the April 2025 BIS Triennial, **FX swaps were 55.6% of Japan's FX turnover against 42.2% globally**, on daily turnover of $440.2bn — Japan is the most swap-intensive major centre (BoJ Review 2026-E-8, May 2026). CGFS 65 established the structural cause and it still holds: Japanese banks lack a US retail deposit base, so they migrated from short FX swaps toward longer cross-currency swaps to reduce rollover risk.

One honest qualification against a Japan-centric narrative: **Japan is a stock story, not the marginal flow story.** Japanese banks' USD cross-border claims grew 7.7% y/y in the four quarters to 2026Q1 — *below* the 12.2% system rate. Spain (25.0%), Italy (27.8%), the US (19.9%), the UK (16.4%) and France (15.6%) all grew faster. On the residence cut that generates the project's headline, Japan-located banks contributed $262.2bn of the $1,899.7bn flow (13.8%), Hong Kong $159.2bn (8.4%), China $109.6bn (5.8%) — **Japan+HK+China = 27.9% of the flow and 30.3% of the $17.86trn stock**. Large, dominant in *level* and in *net short*, but not the accelerator.

**China: the deposit-funded model, and it is invisible by construction.**

Chinese banks' USD cross-border claims fell for eight straight quarters (2022Q4–2024Q2, trough $1,332.5bn), then turned: **$1,554.5bn at 2026Q1, +$222bn from the trough, +9.4% y/y**. The Fed's May-2025 FEDS Note ("Chinese Banks' Dollar Lending Decline", data to 2024Q2, finding a $267bn drop) captured exactly the trough and is now stale on direction.

But the currency substitution the Fed identified is real and continuing: the **USD share of Chinese banks' cross-border claims has fallen from 61.1% (2015Q4) to 46.6% (2026Q1)**. Total cross-border claims rose $594bn between 2024Q4 and 2026Q1 while USD claims rose only $166bn. Both things are true — absolute dollar re-expansion inside a structural de-dollarisation. AidData puts total developing-world debt to China at $1.1–1.5trn nominal, with a documented shift from policy banks (CDB, CHEXIM) toward state commercial banks and from USD infrastructure lending toward RMB rescue lending.

The funding side is where China is genuinely unobservable. Chinese banks' USD cross-border liabilities have *fallen* from $1,074.4bn (2021Q4) to $736.4bn (2026Q1) while claims returned to record levels — the gap doubled from $494bn to $818bn. It is not funded in the eurodollar interbank market. It is funded onshore: **PBoC reports $1.02trn of foreign-currency deposits at Chinese financial institutions (+20% y/y) against only $554.4bn of foreign-currency loans (end-Sep-2025)** — a ~$470bn onshore FX surplus, chiefly exporters' dollar receipts, recycled offshore through the group.

None of that is in the BIS. I verified the blind spot mechanically rather than asserting it. Querying China as an LBS reporting country, the "all positions" figure equals the "cross-border" figure to the decimal — $977,162.449mn for claims, $286,004.985mn for liabilities. **China reports exactly zero local foreign-currency positions.** Compare Japan (local-FCY USD claims $417.6bn) or Hong Kong (local-FCY USD claims $334.5bn, local-FCY USD liabilities $946.3bn). And those two BIS numbers match SAFE's published banking-sector external position for end-March 2026 (USD assets $977.2bn, USD liabilities $286.0bn) to the dollar — confirming that China's LBS submission simply *is* SAFE's external-position return, which is external-only by definition. There is no suppression to complain about; the concept excludes the domestic FX market entirely.

Hong Kong is where the Chinese offshore dollar book actually sits, and it is only partly visible. HK-located banks hold $1,344.7bn of USD cross-border claims and $334.5bn local-FCY, funded by $825.9bn cross-border and $946.3bn local-FCY liabilities — HK is a genuine dollar *deposit collector* (HKMA reports HK$9,191.4bn ≈ US$1,178bn of USD customer deposits at Jun-2026). HK banks' mainland-related lending is HK$4,133.4bn ≈ US$530bn (Mar-2026). But you cannot separate Chinese-owned from other banks within it.

**The measurement wall, quantified.** LBS nationality data is published *only* at the global aggregate. I tested `L_PARENT_CTY=JP` against ten reporting countries: all return no data. You cannot see how much of London's $3,297.5bn USD book is Japanese banks. Nationality × location does not exist. Nor does counterparty sector or counterparty country on any USD-currency cut — I confirmed the project's existing finding independently for both the Japan and global aggregates (only `CP_SECTOR=A`, `CP_COUNTRY=5J` exist). And the residual is large: summing all thirteen published nationalities leaves **$3,115bn of USD cross-border claims (14.3%) and $3,413bn of liabilities (17.5%) attributable to no published nationality**, plus $265bn (11.1%) of the four-quarter flow. Singapore returns NaN on the residence cut, as the project context states.

**The money-like liability link, and a bound on it.** US prime MMFs are the classic dollar funding channel for Japanese banks — BIS WP 708 documents that Japanese and Canadian banks were the largest MMF recipients and that Japanese banks pay a measurable repo premium to US MMFs. But the channel is too small to be the answer. ICI reports total MMF assets of **$7.93trn at 19-Aug-2026, of which prime funds are only $1.238trn** (government $6.541trn, tax-exempt $149.3bn). Even if Japanese banks took a quarter of all prime assets, that is ~$310bn against a $1,953bn cross-border gap. **The MMF channel cannot fund the Japanese dollar book; the swap market must, and does.** That is the single most useful implication of putting the two datasets side by side.

## Cannot establish

Nationality × location does not exist in the public data. London's $3,297.5bn USD book cannot be decomposed by owner. This is the single largest structural gap for this question.

So it cannot be established whether Japanese banks' dollar growth is credit to non-banks (real money creation) or interbank/intragroup recycling. The project's existing finding on this is correct.

## Challenges to project context

**1. The headline understates the offshore dollar book by ~$3.2trn, because it uses a cross-border-only perimeter.** The project headline cites $17.86trn of cross-border USD claims. On the fuller perimeter that actually corresponds to "offshore dollar credit" — cross-border *plus* local positions in foreign currency, i.e. `L_POS_TYPE=A` — the stock is **$21.112trn** and the four-quarter flow is **$2.040trn**. The $3.157trn difference is genuine eurodollar lending booked locally in London, Hong Kong and elsewhere; it is not double-counting. Note the direction of the correction on the growth rate, though: the fuller measure grows **+10.7% y/y, not +11.9%**. So the level is meaningfully larger and the growth rate slightly *less* dramatic. Both belong in the headline. I am not suggesting the existing figure is wrong — it is exactly reproducible (I verified $1,899,703mn, four quarters 2025Q2–2026Q1) — but the perimeter choice should be stated explicitly, because a reader will assume "offshore dollar credit" includes the local eurodollar book, and it does not.

**2. "Of banks located outside the US" is true but not for the reason it appears.** The series uses `L_REP_CTY=5A`, which is *All reporting countries* — the US included. The US drops out mechanically because `L_CURR_TYPE=F` means "currency foreign to the bank's location", and USD is domestic for a US-located bank. The exclusion is a by-product of the currency-type filter, not a country filter. Worth stating precisely, because it also means Puerto Rico and any other USD-domestic location drops out, and it means the filter would break if applied to a different currency.

**3. The China caveat in the project context needs sharpening.** The context says "Chinese banks are structurally understated." That is right but under-specified, and the reason is not confidentiality suppression. China reports **zero local foreign-currency positions, by construction** — I verified that its all-positions and cross-border figures are identical to the decimal, and that both match SAFE's published external-position return exactly. China's LBS submission simply *is* SAFE's external-position return, and "external" excludes the onshore FX market by definition. That onshore market is $1.02trn of FX deposits against $554bn of FX loans (PBoC, Sep-2025). This is a definitional exclusion, not a data-quality problem, and it is not fixable by the BIS.

**4. The unattributed residual is larger than the named China problem.** $3,115bn of USD cross-border claims (14.3%) and $3,413bn of liabilities (17.5%) belong to no published nationality at 2026Q1. Whatever is worrying about Chinese under-coverage ($1.55trn of *published* claims), the anonymous residual is twice as large.

**5. On the money-like liabilities figure, a vintage update and a bound.** The context cites Fed FSR Table 4.1 MMFs at $7,746bn (2025Q4). ICI reports total MMF assets of **$7.93trn at 19-Aug-2026** — consistent and current. But the composition matters for this project's thesis: **prime funds are only $1.238trn**; government funds are $6.541trn. Since only prime funds materially fund non-US banks through CDs, CP and repo, the MMF component of the $27trn money-like liability stock is *mostly not* a funding channel for offshore dollar credit creation. It funds the Treasury and the sponsored-repo market. Any narrative linking the $7.7–7.9trn MMF number to offshore dollar credit needs to run through the $1.24trn prime subset, and even that cannot cover the Japanese gap alone.

**6. A correction to the likely instinct on Japan.** If the project is inclined to treat Japan as the accelerator behind the +11.9% headline, the data says otherwise: Japanese banks' USD claims grew **7.7% y/y, below the 12.2% system rate**, and Japan-located banks contributed 13.8% of the flow. Japan's importance here is not growth — it is that Japan is 86% of the entire system's net dollar short. That is a *level and structure* finding, not a *flow* finding, and conflating them would be an error the operator would spot immediately.

# Decomposition of the Fed FSR's $27,033bn "runnable money-like liabilities": component-by-component growth contributions, independent rebuildable series for each, and reconstruction of the $3,223bn unnamed residual.

**Headline.** Repo, not money funds, is the largest single contributor to the +12.0% (3.91pp of 12.0 vs 3.69pp for MMFs) and the fastest-growing component (+19.1%) — and $749bn of the apparent increase in the headline is not growth at all but an upward restatement of the unnamed "other" bucket between the April 2025 and May 2026 Financial Stability Reports (published-to-published the number rose 15.6%, not 12.0%). The restatement is almost certainly in offshore money market funds, which arithmetic suggests is the largest single item inside "other."

## Mechanics

The operator's premise is right but needs one correction: a non-US bank CAN create a dollar deposit ex nihilo; what it cannot do is settle the resulting payment.

**The loan.** Tokyo bank's London branch books a $100m loan. Dr Loans to customer $100m / Cr Customer USD deposit $100m. Money created, dollar-denominated, on a balance sheet with no Fed account. It appears in BIS LBS as a cross-border/local USD claim and liability of a bank located outside the US; it appears in no monetary aggregate anywhere (excluded from US M2 by residence/reporter definition, from euro-area M3 by currency and residence, from Japanese M3 by currency).

**The constraint.** The deposit is drawn to pay a third party. If the payee banks with another non-US dollar bank, settlement occurs across correspondent nostros and, at the limit, nets within the offshore system: no Fed reserves move. Offshore dollar credit is therefore not reserve-constrained loan-by-loan — it is constrained by (a) net leakage to the US banking system, and (b) the price of covering that leakage. This is why offshore dollar credit can compound at +11.9% while US reserves shrink. Reserves are needed only at the margin.

**Covering the leakage, entry by entry.**

*(a) Unsecured wholesale — the dying channel.* US prime MMF buys the branch's CD. MMF: Dr CD $100m / Cr MMF shares $100m — the money-like liability is the fund share, created in the US. Bank: Dr Nostro at US correspondent $100m / Cr CD issued $100m. Requires a buyer of unsecured foreign-bank paper. Institutional prime MMFs are that buyer and are down to $246bn (ICI, 19 Aug 2026), 3.1% of the industry. Residual capacity shows up as foreign-bank CP: $336bn, Dec 2025.

*(b) Secured — the growing channel.* Branch pledges USTs to a government MMF or via FICC sponsored repo. Bank: Dr Cash $100m / Cr Repo payable $100m; collateral stays on balance sheet, encumbered. MMF: Dr Reverse repo $100m / Cr MMF shares $100m. The binding input is **eligible collateral**, not a depositor. Government MMFs (82% of the industry, $6,375bn) are legally confined to government paper and repo, so their growth can only reach a foreign bank through this entry. MMF repo with foreign financial institutions: $950bn, July 2026. Rest-of-world repo borrowing in Z.1: $2,052bn at 2025:Q4, +25.9%.

*(c) FX swap.* Branch has yen, needs dollars. Spot: Dr USD cash / Cr JPY cash; forward leg booked as a derivative. No dollar deposit is created by the swap — the dollars are *borrowed*, and the ultimate USD lender is normally a US MMF or a reserve manager reached through the dealer. The cross-currency basis is the price of the same scarcity as (a) and (b). A structurer will recognise that a widening JPY basis and a tightening in MMF repo-with-FFI are the same constraint priced in two places.

*(d) Conduit.* An ABCP conduit sponsored by the bank issues paper against receivables; the bank supplies the liquidity backstop. Conduit: Dr Assets / Cr ABCP. Bank: contingent commitment, drawn only on failure. ABCP outstanding $506bn (July 2026), +37% from Dec 2024. This is the purest form of the operator's point: credit created against collateral with no deposit anywhere in the chain until the ABCP is bought — by a money fund.

*(e) Interoffice.* The group's US branch or IHC borrows fed funds/repo in New York (foreign banking offices in US: $471.9bn repo liabilities, 2025:Q4) or runs down its Fed reserves and lends interoffice to head office. This is the only route by which actual Fed reserves reach the offshore book.

**The link to the $27trn.** Every one of (a)–(d) terminates in a US-side money-like liability: an MMF share, a repo payable held by an MMF, a CP holding, or cash collateral from a securities loan. The $27,033bn is, functionally, the inventory of US-side claims available to fund the offshore book. The single largest change in that inventory since 2022 was not new money — it was the $2.34trn migration out of the Fed's RRP, a facility that absorbed cash and created nothing, into private secured credit. That reallocation is the mechanical bridge between Table 4.1 and the BIS LBS +$1.90trn. It is also why the marginal dollar is now getting harder: the RRP is empty ($3bn, Jan 2026), so the next increment of offshore dollar funding must come from genuinely new money-like liabilities or from higher collateral velocity, not from a stock reshuffle.

**2. The $749bn that is not growth.**

The April 2025 FSR published 2024:Q4 total runnable money-like liabilities at **23,388**. The May 2026 report's stated 12.0% implies a 2024:Q4 base of **24,126–24,147** (rounding-bounded). Published-to-published, 23,388 → 27,033 is **+15.6%**. The Fed's 12.0% is therefore measured against a base restated upward by **$738–759bn**.

Component-level: uninsured deposits, MMFs and securities lending reconcile to within $10bn; repo +23; CP was revised **down** $104bn (published 1,323, implied 1,219 — a DTCC-sourced revision). The entire restatement sits in the unnamed bucket: "other" at 2024:Q4 was **2,181** as published, **2,980–3,020** as implied. That is an **$799–839bn (≈+38%) upward restatement of a category the Fed never publishes as a series.** The list of what is in "other" is *word-for-word identical* across the 2024, 2025 and 2026 vintages (VRDOs, federal funds, funding-agreement-backed securities, private liquidity funds, offshore MMFs, STIFs, LGIPs, stablecoins), so this is re-measurement of existing components, not a new instrument. This is materially larger than prior-vintage revisions (+$208bn at 2023:Q4, −$42bn at 2022:Q4). Anyone who says "$27trn, fastest growth since X" against a history of published headlines is comparing across a break.

**3. What is inside "other," and the offshore link.**

Bottom-up at 2025:Q4, using what is publicly retrievable: offshore money funds — Crane Data puts the European/offshore complex at $1,670bn (13 May 2026), of which USD 52.4% = ~$875bn; private liquidity funds $374bn (SEC Private Fund Statistics, 2025:Q1, the latest published — the series runs ~15 months late and 2025:Q4 does not exist yet); stablecoins $320bn (FSR/DeFiLlama, 2025:Q4); federal funds $44bn (Z.1 F4.1.s, lines 4+7). That identifies $1,613bn, leaving $1,610bn for VRDOs, STIFs, LGIPs and FABS — implausibly large for those four. If instead the Fed counts the **full** offshore money-fund complex ($1,670bn, all currencies, USD-equivalent), identified rises to $2,408bn and the residual falls to $815bn, which VRDOs + STIFs + LGIPs + FABS can plausibly fill. Note the difference between the two treatments is **$795bn against an observed restatement of ~$819bn**. That is a hypothesis, not a finding — it cannot be confirmed without the Fed's underlying iMoneyNet series — but it is the only candidate large enough, and it implies **offshore MMFs are the single largest item in "other," bigger than every named component except MMFs, uninsured deposits and repo.**

That matters for this project specifically: offshore money funds are dollar money-like liabilities *created outside the US*, whose assets are the CP, CDs and repo of non-US banks. Central Bank of Ireland data (rebuildable, monthly): Irish-resident MMF total assets **€1,011.9bn at 30 June 2026**, of which USD-denominated loans-and-securities **€451.2bn = 44.9%** (€155.4bn of USD "loans" — deposits and repo placed with banks — plus €296.8bn of USD securities). That is the Dublin leg of the loop.

**4. Money funds: the RRP unwind is the whole story, and it has run out.**

OFR MMF Monitor (monthly, N-MFP): MMF repo with the Federal Reserve fell from **$2,340bn (Dec 2022) → $969bn (Dec 2023) → $382bn (Dec 2024) → $73bn (Dec 2025) → $3bn (Jan 2026) → $7bn (Jun 2026)**. Total MMF repo barely moved over the same span ($2,977bn → $2,994bn); the composition inverted. Repo with FICC rose $110bn → $1,298bn; with domestic financial institutions $160bn → $720bn; **with foreign financial institutions $367bn → $897bn, and $950bn by July 2026.** So: the growth in MMF AUM did not fund new credit — the *reallocation out of the RRP* did. About $2.34trn of cash migrated from a sterile Fed facility into private secured credit, of which roughly $950bn now sits directly against non-US banks.

The composition question in the lens has a sharp answer. ICI (19 Aug 2026): total $7,928.5bn — government $6,541.2bn (retail 1,977.2 / institutional 4,563.9), prime $1,238.1bn (retail **992.0** / institutional **246.1**), tax-exempt $149.3bn. Institutional prime is 3.1% of the industry. The 2023 SEC reform's mandatory liquidity fees (effective 2 Oct 2024) effectively killed it. Institutional prime funds were historically the main US buyers of foreign-bank CP and CDs. Government funds, which are 82% of the industry, **cannot** buy foreign bank paper — but they can repo with foreign banks against Treasuries and agencies. **The composition of the US money-fund industry has therefore mechanically converted non-US banks' dollar funding from unsecured to collateralised.** Empirically: MMF repo with foreign financials $950bn vs foreign-financial CP $344bn (both July 2026). The operator's "deposits or collateral" dichotomy is resolved, in the data, in favour of collateral.

**6. Commercial paper.**

Fed CP release (DTCC), monthly NSA, last obs July 2026, $bn. Total 1,174 (Dec-24) → 1,323 (Dec-25) → **1,454 (Jul-26)**. Two things drive it. **7. Uninsured deposits.**

FDIC QBP (Table I-C, Q4 2025, $m): estimated uninsured domestic deposits **8,138,545 at 2025:Q4, +6.98% y/y**, versus a trough of 7,200,848 at 2024:Q2 and a pre-SVB peak of **8,172,253 at 2022:Q4**. The level is back to **within 0.4% of the peak** — fully recovered. **8. What is decelerating.**

Post-2025:Q4 the composition is rotating. MMFs: 7,746 → 7,928.5 (ICI, 19 Aug 2026), **+2.4% in ~7.7 months** against +13.0% in 2025 — the MMF engine has essentially stopped, because the RRP it was draining is empty. Stablecoins: 320 (2025:Q4) → **308.4 (DeFiLlama, 21 Aug 2026), −3.6%** — contracting, and the smallest component; the popular narrative that stablecoins are driving this is wrong by an order of magnitude. Meanwhile repo ex-Fed +6.7% in a single quarter to 2026:Q1, total CP +9.9% and ABCP +16.3% through July 2026. The growth is migrating from the fund-share channel to the collateral channel.

## Cannot establish

**1. Securities lending cash collateral ($1,201bn, +13.8%, 5.0% of the rise) has no free rebuildable series.** The Fed sources it to the RMA Securities Lending Report and S&P Securities Finance, both proprietary and both unavailable to me. ISLA publishes on-loan and lendable balances but not a US cash-collateral reinvestment stock on the Fed's basis. 4.4% of the $27trn and 5.0% of its growth cannot be independently verified. Note also the Fed's own footnote: the long-run average for this line runs only to 2025:Q3, i.e. the series is reported with a lag inside the same table.

**2. The "other" bucket is not published as a series.** It exists only as a residual and as a shaded band in Figure 4.1. Its $819bn restatement therefore cannot be attributed to a named component from public data. My attribution to offshore money market funds is an inference resting on (i) no other component is large enough, and (ii) the $795bn gap between counting only the USD sleeve of the offshore complex and counting the whole complex is arithmetically close to the observed $819bn. Confirming it requires the Fed's iMoneyNet Offshore Money Fund Analyzer series, which is proprietary. **Do not present this as established.**

**3. VRDOs, short-term investment funds, local government investment pools and funding-agreement-backed securities have no retrievable primary series.** SIFMA's US Municipal VRDO Update is member-restricted; the Fed switched VRDOs to Bloomberg from 2019:Q1, which is itself a documented break inside the history. STIF data is filed with the OCC monthly under 12 CFR 9.18 but is not published in aggregate. There is no consolidated LGIP statistic. Together these four must account for roughly $815bn–$1,610bn of "other" depending on the offshore MMF treatment, and none of it is verifiable.

**4. The repo component cannot be reconciled to Z.1.** FSR says $5,887bn at 2025:Q4. Z.1 F4.1.s (2026:Q1 vintage) gives total repo+FF liabilities of $6,464.2bn, $6,002.0bn excluding the central bank, and $5,957.6bn excluding both the central bank and fed funds. The nearest cut is still $70bn away, and the FSR would have been built on the March 2026 Z.1 vintage which the June release has since revised. I could not close this gap.

**5. Private liquidity funds are unusable at the required vintage.** SEC Private Fund Statistics runs roughly 15 months behind; 2025:Q1 ($374bn) is the latest published as of August 2026. There is no 2025:Q4 figure, and I did not fabricate one — the $374bn used in the bottom-up is a 2025:Q1 number carried forward, and it is labelled as such.

**6. The MMF growth decomposition asked for in the lens cannot be done cleanly.** Published data does not attribute MMF flows to motive. I can establish as an accounting fact that $2.34trn left the ON RRP between Dec 2022 and Jan 2026 and that the counterparty composition of MMF repo inverted; I cannot separate "rate differential against bank deposits" from "institutional cash migration" as competing causes, because no source decomposes flows that way. Any such split would be an assertion.

**7. No US-side series carries a currency-of-lending or destination dimension.** Nothing in Table 4.1, Z.1, the CP release or the OFR monitor tells you what share of these liabilities ultimately funds dollar credit booked outside the United States. The bridge from the $27trn to the BIS LBS +$1.90trn is mechanically coherent and directionally supported by three measured quantities (ROW repo borrowing +25.9%, MMF repo with foreign financials $950bn, non-US bank CP +24.5%), but it is an inference from counterparty residence, not a measured flow. It cannot be closed with published data.

**8. Federal funds is reported inconsistently.** Z.1 F4.1.s footnote 2 says the fed funds lines exclude term federal funds, while line 39 (FHLB) includes them. The $44.3bn figure is therefore a floor, not a total.

## Challenges to project context

**1. "Repo $5,887bn" is understated as a driver; it is the single biggest one.** The context lists MMFs first and treats the $27trn as an MMF-led story. On the arithmetic, repo contributes 3.91pp of the 12.0% versus 3.69pp for MMFs, grows fastest of any component (+19.1%), and accounts for 32.6% of the increase. Further, most of the government-MMF contribution (3.06pp) is itself repo — government funds are legally confined to government paper and repo, and the whole change in their asset mix since 2022 is the RRP-to-private-repo rotation. Properly attributed, the collateral channel is well over half the story.

**2. The "+12.0% y/y against a +5.4% 2003-25 average" comparison is not clean, and the context should carry the caveat.** The April 2025 FSR published 2024:Q4 at 23,388. The May 2026 report's 12.0% implies a base of 24,126–24,147. Published-to-published the headline rose 15.6%; $738–759bn of that is restatement. The restatement is $799–839bn concentrated entirely in the unnamed "other" bucket, whose component list is word-for-word identical across the 2024, 2025 and 2026 vintages — so this is re-measurement of existing components, not a new instrument entering. The context correctly says the series is "definitionally non-stationary"; it should now say **by how much and where**, because at ~3.2% of the total this restatement is comparable in size to a quarter of the reported annual growth.

**3. "Z.1 L.207" is a dead identifier.** The Z.1 released 11 June 2026 renumbered every table and reclassified federal funds and security repurchase agreements as a subcategory of total loans. The repo tables are now F4.1.t (flows) and F4.1.s (stocks outstanding). The release also retired the word "levels" in favour of "stocks outstanding" and renamed "monetary authority" to "central bank". Any project note or downstream citation using L.207 should be corrected.

**4. The repo series does not add up, by a growing margin.** Z.1 F4.1.s reports a discrepancy (assets minus liabilities) of −$1,775.0bn at 2025:Q4, up from −$686.1bn at 2023. The repo leg of the $27trn rests on a measurement that fails to balance by an amount that has grown $1.09trn in two years. This belongs alongside the existing "basis-mixed" caveat and is arguably more serious than it.

**5. The uninsured-deposit line needs two corrections.** First, the FSR's $7,608bn is not the FDIC number: FDIC estimated uninsured domestic deposits were $8,138,545m at 2025:Q4, a $531bn gap, and the growth rates differ (7.7% vs 6.98%). The May 2026 FSR narrows its source to Call Report Form FFIEC 031, a change of wording from the 2025 report. Second — and this contradicts the received reading of the FSR's own language — **on the FDIC basis the level of uninsured deposits has fully recovered from SVB**, sitting 0.4% below the 2022:Q4 peak of $8,172,253m after a trough of $7,200,848m in 2024:Q2. It is only the *share* of deposits (42.92% vs 44.30%) that remains below peak. "Significantly below elevated 2023 levels" is true of the ratio and false of the stock.

**6. Stablecoins are not driving anything and are now shrinking.** $320bn at 2025:Q4 per the FSR, $308.4bn on a live DeFiLlama query on 21 August 2026 — down 3.6%. They are 1.2% of the total and a negative contributor in 2026. The context lists them as a recent entrant to the series, which is right, but any framing that treats them as a growth engine is wrong by an order of magnitude. Tether plus USDC are 83% of supply.

**7. The largest item in "other" is very likely offshore money market funds — which, if right, is the most important single fact for this project.** Bottom-up, the only candidate large enough to absorb an $819bn restatement is the offshore money-fund complex ($1,670bn total, 52.4% USD, Crane Data, 13 May 2026). If the Fed counts the full complex rather than the USD sleeve, offshore MMFs are the largest item inside "other" and the fourth-largest money-like liability overall. That would mean the $27trn already contains, unlabelled, a large block of **dollar money-like liabilities created outside the United States** — which is precisely the nine-channel map's offshore credit channel appearing on the other side of the ledger. The two headline findings of this project may be partially the same object viewed from opposite ends. This is a hypothesis with supporting arithmetic, not a verified fact, and the Fed's iMoneyNet series would be needed to settle it.

**8. The forward run-rate contradicts the "fastest since March 2020" momentum framing.** The MMF engine has stalled: 7,746 (2025:Q4) → 7,928.5 (ICI, 19 Aug 2026), +2.4% in 7.7 months against +13.0% in 2025, because the ON RRP it was draining is empty ($3bn in Jan 2026, from $2,340bn in Dec 2022). What is still accelerating is the collateral side: repo ex-Fed +6.7% in the single quarter to 2026:Q1, total CP +9.9% and ABCP +16.3% through July 2026. The composition of money-like liability creation is rotating from fund shares to collateral, and any narrative built on the 2025 MMF surge is already stale.

# The theory of money-like liability creation — the creation function, its estimated elasticities, and which term is currently moving. Offshore extension: why a bank without a Fed account must manufacture safety from collateral rather than from a reserve account.

**Headline.** The government-safe-asset term enters the creation function twice with opposite signs, and in the current regime the positive one dominates: Treasury supply is no longer crowding private money out (Krishnamurthy–Vissing-Jorgensen's -0.5), it is crowding it in as collateral. 58% of the 2025 increase in the Fed's $27,033bn "runnable money-like liabilities" came from two lines — repo (+19.1%) and government MMFs (+13.1%) — whose asset side is overwhelmingly Treasury collateral, at the same time as bills outstanding rose $993bn (+16.6% y/y to $6,988.9bn, 31 Jul 2026, MSPD Table 1, verified this session). That is not private money creation filling a government under-supply. It is government safe-asset supply being re-wrapped, twice-counted along the MMF→repo chain, and the same inversion is what makes the offshore dollar system collateral-funded rather than deposit-funded.

## Mechanics

**Entry by entry, three cases.**

*(a) A US bank writes a dollar loan.* Dr Loans / Cr Deposits. Money created ex nihilo; the reserve requirement is zero and the binding constraints are capital, LCR outflow assumptions, and the payment leakage when the deposit leaves. The bank holds a Fed account, so settlement is internal to the Fed's ledger.

*(b) A non-US bank writes a dollar loan.* It cannot do (a), because "dollars" for it means a claim on a US-resident bank, and it cannot issue that claim. Two sub-cases, and only one is creation:

 *(b1) Creation.* Dr USD loan / Cr USD deposit, both on the offshore bank's own books. The borrower spends. If the payee banks at another offshore institution that shares the same US correspondent, the correspondent books Dr Bank A nostro / Cr Bank B nostro and **no Fed reserve balance moves**. The offshore system's dollar deposits have risen with no change in the US monetary base. This is the eurodollar multiplier and it is genuinely money creation outside every published aggregate. Its constraint is not reserves. It is whether the payee accepts an offshore dollar deposit as an information-insensitive claim — which is a Gorton condition, not a Basel one. If the payee banks in the US, the correspondent debits Bank A's nostro and moves a reserve to the payee's US bank: leakage, and Bank A must replace the funding.

 *(b2) Transfer.* Bank A acquires an existing dollar claim before or as it lends. Repo: Dr USD cash-at-correspondent / Cr Repo payable, with a Treasury pledged out — the MMF's share in the fund becomes the money-like claim, and the dollar deposit merely changed hands from the MMF's custodian to Bank A. CD/CP: same, unsecured. Intragroup: Dr Nostro / Cr Due to head office — the group's aggregate dollar position is unchanged, only its location. FX swap: Dr USD cash / Cr USD payable forward, with a JPY leg posted — the counterparty gave up an existing dollar deposit, and Bank A has written a dated dollar obligation, not a dollar. **In every b2 case the offshore dollar deposit stock is unchanged; only the claim's holder and its money-likeness change.**

*(c) The chain that makes the Fed's $27,033bn a gross sum, not a money stock.* A government MMF's liability is a share, counted in the $7,746bn MMF line. Its asset is a Treasury bill (counted in Θ, not in Table 4.1) or a repo — and that repo is *also* a liability of a dealer, counted in the $5,887bn repo line. At 31 Jul 2026 MMFs held $2,925.3bn of repo (OFR). So roughly $2.9trn — ~11% of Table 4.1's total — is one economic exposure counted as two money-like liabilities at two entities. The Fed is not wrong to do this (both are runnable), but the sum is a run-risk aggregate, not a quantity of money.

*(d) The settlement floor.* Whatever the offshore system does, its final settlement asset is a Fed reserve balance held by a US-resident office. Foreign-related institutions in the US held $1,222.7bn of cash assets at Jul 2026 against $3,557.7bn of total assets; system reserve balances were $2,935.3bn on 19 Aug 2026 (H.4.1). The offshore ledger can expand freely above that floor and cannot settle net through it.

## Analysis

**1. The creation function, stated.**

The literature converges on a supply-and-demand system for information-insensitive claims. Write private money-like liabilities as

  M^p = Φ(C, θ, σ) · λ(D(i, W), Θ)

where D is non-financial demand for claims that can be traded without adverse selection (Gorton & Pennacchi 1990 JF; Gorton "no questions asked"), Θ is government supply of safe/liquid assets, λ is the resulting liquidity/safety premium (the price of moneyness), and Φ is intermediaries' capacity to manufacture safety — collateral C, haircuts/structure θ, bounded by regulation, and destroyed by uncertainty σ (Moreira & Savov 2017 JF 72:2381–2432).

The estimated elasticities, with vintages:

- **Price of moneyness vs government supply.** KVJ (JPE 2012, 120(2):233–267): Treasury yields depressed 73bp on average 1926–2008. In the 2013 working-paper version of KVJ (JFE 2015, 118(3):571–600), regressing spreads on log(Debt/GDP) with controls gives −0.554 for high-grade CP–bills, −1.96 for low-grade CP–bills, −0.888 for the P2–P1 safety spread (t=−4.34), −1.884 for the FDIC-insured CD–bill liquidity spread (t=−1.71). A one-s.d. fall in debt/GDP from 0.44 to 0.24 widens the Aaa–Treasury premium 44bp.
- **Quantity crowding-out.** KVJ's headline: a $1 increase in Treasury supply reduces the financial sector's *net* short-term debt by ~$0.50 and its net long-term lending by ~$0.50 (coefficients "around −0.5", 1% significance; WP sample 1914–2011, published sample 1875–2014).
- **But with the opposite sign on the collateralised leg.** In the same table, government supply *crowds in* checkable deposits (+0.22) and crowds in the financial sector's holdings of government-supplied assets by more (+0.45), so the deposit coverage ratio rises with a coefficient of 1.15 (t>2). KVJ's own model predicts both signs: Treasuries are simultaneously a substitute for private money and the backing that lets intermediaries write it.
- **Quantity response to money demand.** Sunderam (RFS 2015, 28(4):939–977), weekly, Jan 2001–Jun 2007: a 50bp (2 s.d.) widening in the ABCP–bill spread forecasts +0.5% ABCP outstanding. Extrapolated, the 21bp higher average ABCP–bill spread in Jun 2004–Jul 2007 vs Jan 2001–Jun 2004 implies ~30pp more issuance — roughly half the 70% ABCP growth over that window.
- **Price vs the short rate.** Nagel (QJE 2016, 131(4):1927–1971): the T-bill liquidity premium is proportional to the opportunity cost of money. Repo–bill spread +5.36bp per 100bp of fed funds (s.e. 0.99, May 1991–Jun 2007); CD–bill +6.69bp; bankers'-acceptance–bill +10.12bp in the long sample. Critically, Nagel's column (7) shows the Greenwood–Hanson–Stein bill-supply effect collapses once the short rate is included: the T-bill-supply coefficient falls an order of magnitude from −95.35 (s.e. 27.83) to −9.51 (s.e. 12.00), while the fed funds coefficient survives at 11.01 (s.e. 1.25). **The supply channel and the opportunity-cost channel are not both identified.** Anyone using KVJ's crowding-out to forecast is implicitly rejecting Nagel.
- **The null on the level.** Gorton, Lewellen & Metrick (NBER WP 17777, Jan 2012): the safe-asset share of *total US assets* is a constant 33.2% (s.e. 0.003) every year since 1952; financial-sector liabilities 26.7%. If demand for information-insensitive debt is a stable share of the risky-asset pool, the interesting object is never the level — it is the composition.

**2. Which term is moving. All the classical terms point the wrong way.**

Θ is expanding violently. Bills outstanding $6,988.9bn at 31 Jul 2026 vs $5,995.9bn a year earlier: +$993.0bn, +16.6%; bills 22.22% of marketable debt vs 20.70%; total marketable +$2,485.1bn to $31,455.1bn (MSPD Table 1, pulled from the Fiscal Data API this session). Under KVJ that should have subtracted roughly $1.2trn from private net short-term debt.

The price of moneyness is compressed. On 19 Aug 2026 the 4-week bill printed 3.62% bank-discount / 3.68% coupon-equivalent against TGCR 3.60%, SOFR 3.62%, EFFR 3.63% (Treasury daily bill rates; NY Fed reference rates). Bills are not rich to secured overnight money; the bill curve is upward-sloping to 3.98% at 52 weeks. And the opportunity cost of money has fallen ~170bp from the 2023–24 peak of 5.33%, which under Nagel's coefficients mechanically compresses the bill liquidity premium ~9–11bp.

The official money supply to non-banks has been withdrawn entirely. MMF repo with the Federal Reserve: $2,062.9bn (Jun 2022) → $6.8bn (Jun 2026); the ON RRP took $225mn on 20 Aug 2026 (OFR MMF data release; NY Fed operations API). That is a −$2.06trn shock to Θ *as held by money funds* running against the +$2.5trn in marketable issuance.

Yet M^p grew 12.0% y/y to $27,033bn at 2025Q4 against a 1997–2025 average of 5.4% (Fed FSR, May 2026, Table 4.1 — note the vintage is the **May** report, published 8 May 2026, not April).

**3. What actually did the work: Φ, the manufacturing term.**

Decomposing the $2,897bn increase in Table 4.1 (my arithmetic from the reported component growth rates): repo contributed ~$944bn (32.6%), domestic MMFs ~$891bn (30.8%, of which government MMFs alone ~$738bn), uninsured deposits ~$544bn (18.8%), CP ~$149bn, securities lending ~$146bn. Repo plus government MMFs = ~58% of the increase.

Both are Treasury-collateral pass-throughs. From OFR's N-MFP-based release at 31 Jul 2026: MMF total investments $8,411.9bn, of which Treasuries $3,452.6bn, repo $2,925.3bn, agency $1,214.8bn — 90.2% government paper or collateralised claims. **Bank-related assets (financial CP, CDs, deposits) are $467.4bn, 5.6% of the total, and falling: $546.1bn (Jul 2024) → $515.2bn (Jul 2025) → $467.4bn (Jul 2026).** The single largest private money-production line in the world is now almost entirely a repackaging of government collateral, not a claim on bank credit.

Regulation, the bound on Φ, was explicitly loosened. The eSLR final rule (adopted Nov 2025, Federal Register 1 Dec 2025, effective 1 Apr 2026) cuts the GSIB holding-company requirement to 3% plus half the Method 1 surcharge and the subsidiary-bank requirement from 6% to 4%; agencies estimated a **$219bn (28%) reduction in Tier 1 leverage capital requirements at the major subsidiary depositories**. That is the KVJ/Stein bound on collateral intermediation being relaxed by a quarter.

**4. The offshore extension — why the operator's instinct is right and where it stops.**

A bank outside the US has no reserve account. Its Θ is rationed: no ON RRP, no IOR except through a US branch, FIMA repo only for official accounts, swap lines only for a small standing set of central banks. So offshore, the λ(D, Θ) term is smaller and Φ(C, θ) must carry proportionally more of the load. That is the theoretical reason offshore dollar credit is collateral- and swap-funded rather than deposit-funded.

The funding legs, each with its own creation status:
- **Offshore dollar deposit taken from a non-bank.** This *is* creation, and the system-level constraint is not reserves but whether the payee will hold an offshore dollar deposit as money — Gorton's information-insensitivity condition, offshore. When two offshore banks share a correspondent, no Fed reserve moves and the expansion is entirely internal to the offshore ledger.
- **Unsecured CD/CP to money funds.** Shrinking: $467.4bn of US MMF bank-related assets. Offshore MMFs, ~$2.2trn AUM at end-2025 (Fed FSR May 2026), sit inside the $3,223bn "other" line — roughly 68% of it. This is *transfer*, not creation.
- **Repo with US money funds.** $950.5bn to foreign financial institutions at 31 Jul 2026, up from $365.8bn in Jul 2022 (+160%) — OFR series MMF-MMF_RP_wFFI-M. Transfer, collateralised.
- **Intragroup.** US offices of foreign banks are net *borrowers* from head office: net due to related foreign offices $842.5bn (H.8 Table 10, 14 Aug 2026 release, Jul 2026 average), vs $945.5bn a year earlier. Their cash assets — the offshore system's settlement base — are $1,222.7bn, down from $1,443.3bn. Transfer.
- **FX swap.** $155trn of FX derivatives notional at end-Jun 2025, +19% y/y, of which $100trn forwards and swaps maturing within a year (BIS, published 8 Dec 2025). An FX swap does not create a dollar; it borrows one against a currency leg and books a forward obligation. Pure Φ, maximal rollover fragility, and Moreira & Savov's σ-shock hits it first.

**5. The tension the literature has not resolved.** Nagel says the price of moneyness is set by the short rate, not by supply; KVJ and Greenwood–Hanson–Stein say supply. Stein (QJE 2012) says a higher policy rate taxes private money creation; Nagel and Sunderam imply a higher rate raises the premium and therefore the incentive to issue. The 2022–26 record cuts both ways: money-like liabilities exploded while rates rose 2022–23, and kept growing 12% while rates fell ~170bp. Neither sign survives. That is why I locate the action in Φ rather than in λ.

## Cannot establish

**1. Whether offshore dollar credit growth is creation or transfer.** The distinction turns entirely on whether the offsetting entry is a new offshore dollar deposit held as money by a non-bank, or an existing dollar claim acquired via repo, CD, intragroup or FX swap. The BIS LBS foreign-currency-by-residence cut has no counterparty sector, so the split cannot be computed. No published series resolves it. I would not defend calling the +$1.90trn "money creation" without it — at minimum the claim should be stated as an upper bound.

**2. The composition of the $3,223bn "other" line beyond one sentence.** The FSR states offshore MMFs are ~$2.2trn at end-2025 and stablecoins ~$320bn; the residual ~$700bn spread across VRDOs, fed funds, funding-agreement-backed securities, private liquidity funds, STIFs and LGIPs is not published as a table. It cannot be decomposed from public sources.

**3. The current cross-currency basis.** I could not obtain a primary, free, dated quote for the 3-month JPY/USD or EUR/USD basis as of August 2026. The operator has this on his own screens and it is the single most informative price for the Φ term offshore; I decline to supply a number I have not seen.

**4. BIS OTC derivatives beyond end-June 2025.** As of 21 Aug 2026 the latest published commentary is still the 8 Dec 2025 release covering end-June 2025. The end-December 2025 and end-June 2026 FX swap stocks are not available.

**5. No estimated elasticity of *offshore* money-like issuance to anything.** KVJ, Sunderam, Nagel and Greenwood-Hanson-Stein are all US-only, all pre-2008 or pre-2015 samples. There is no published regression of offshore dollar money-like liabilities on Treasury supply, on the fed funds rate, or on the cross-currency basis. The creation function I state above is a US-estimated function applied offshore by analogy. That is a genuine gap and it is the most valuable unfilled piece of work in this area.

**6. The Nagel-vs-KVJ identification problem is unresolved in the literature and I cannot resolve it here.** Whether the +$993bn of bill supply should have compressed the money premium (KVJ/GHS) or whether the premium is set by the 3.63% policy rate regardless (Nagel) determines the sign of the forecast. Both papers are correctly executed on overlapping data and reach incompatible conclusions.

**7. Whether the eSLR relaxation reaches foreign banking organisations.** The final rule applies to US GSIBs and their subsidiary depositories. FBO intermediate holding companies are subject to the SLR under Category II/III standards but not the eSLR buffers, and I did not establish what, if anything, changed for them. Since the $950.5bn of MMF repo with foreign financial institutions is transacted largely with US affiliates of foreign groups, this matters and is unverified.

**8. Sunderam's test has never been re-run.** No post-2020 replication on repo, government MMF shares, or offshore instruments exists. The "shadow banking responds to money demand" result is a 2001-2007 ABCP finding being carried, on faith, into a market whose instruments have entirely changed.

## Challenges to project context

**1. "$27,033bn of runnable money-like liabilities" is a run-risk aggregate, not a quantity of privately created money, and the project context treats it as the latter.** Three problems, in order of severity.

(a) *It double-counts along the intermediation chain.* A government MMF's share is counted in the $7,746bn MMF line; the repo that fund holds is separately counted in the $5,887bn repo line as the dealer's liability. At 31 Jul 2026 money funds held $2,925.3bn of repo (OFR, verified this session) — roughly 11% of the $27,033bn total is one exposure wearing two money-like hats. The Fed is not wrong to count both, since both can run; but the sum is not additive as *money*.

(b) *Most of the 2025 increase is government safe-asset supply in a private wrapper, not private safety manufacture.* Repo (+19.1%) and government MMFs (+13.1%) supplied ~58% of the $2,897bn increase. Of OFR-measured MMF assets, 90.2% are Treasuries, agencies, or repo; only 5.6% are unsecured bank claims. Calling this "private money creation" inverts what it is: a claim on the Treasury, intermediated. Netting it against the $993bn increase in bill supply the same year is not optional — it is the same object counted on both sides of the ledger.

(c) *The 12.0% growth rate is less anomalous than it reads.* Gorton, Lewellen & Metrick's null is that safe assets are a constant 33.2% of *total assets*, not of GDP. Household net worth rose ~8.1% y/y to $182.98trn (2026Q1). So the safe-asset share did breach its historical constant — by roughly 4pp of growth, not 7pp against nominal GDP. The Fed's own 86%-of-GDP framing uses a denominator GLM explicitly rejected. Real, but a quarter smaller than the headline implies.

**2. "The only channel that is unambiguously money creation" is the claim I would most want to defend before repeating.** The operator's own instinct is the objection. Offshore dollar credit is funded through legs that are overwhelmingly *transfers* of existing dollar claims: repo ($950.5bn from US money funds alone), unsecured CD/CP (shrinking, $467.4bn of US MMF bank-related assets), intragroup ($842.5bn net due to related foreign offices), and above all FX swaps ($100trn of sub-one-year forwards and swaps at end-Jun 2025). Only the leg where a non-bank accepts a newly written offshore dollar deposit as money is creation, and the BIS foreign-currency-by-residence cut cannot isolate it. The +$1.90trn is an upper bound on creation, and given the funding mix it may be a loose one.

**3. Vintage correction.** The Financial Stability Report carrying Table 4.1 at 2025Q4 is the **May 2026** report, published 8 May 2026 — not April. Minor, but the project rule is to note the vintage.

**4. The KVJ crowding-out sign is being applied backwards, and the project context does not flag it.** KVJ's -0.5 is a net coefficient over 1875-2014, a period in which most private money was backed by private assets. Their own table has +0.22 on checkable deposits and +1.15 on the deposit coverage ratio: Treasury supply crowds *in* the money-like claims that are Treasury-backed. In a regime where 90% of money-fund assets and the fastest-growing money-like line are Treasury-collateralised, the composition has shifted far enough that the net sign plausibly flips positive. That is a falsifiable claim and, if right, it means the standard "the Treasury is under-supplying so private money fills the gap" story runs exactly the wrong way in 2026: the Treasury is over-supplying, and private money is growing *because of* it.

# What is driving it right now — the 2024-2026 window. Ranked attribution of the +12.0% y/y in runnable money-like liabilities across six candidate drivers, and the funding-side link to offshore USD credit creation.

**Headline.** The marginal offshore dollar is not a deposit and not real-economy credit — it is collateral. BIS LBS at end-March 2026 shows USD cross-border credit +$793bn in Q1 alone, the largest quarterly expansion since the onset of Covid, +12% y/y, of which credit to non-bank financial institutions was +$651bn of the +$1.1trn to all non-banks, borrowers located in the US, UK, Cayman Islands and Japan. That is the same population as the Cayman hedge-fund Treasury complex, and it is the mirror image of the Fed FSR's repo line (+19.1%, the single largest contributor at 3.91pp of the 12.0%). Second finding: roughly 1.5pp of the 12.0% is not money creation at all but RRP reclassification — tri-party repo ex-Fed rose +$376bn in 2025 while the ON RRP fell -$367bn, near one-for-one — and that source is now exhausted (RRP $0.225bn, 2026-08-20).

## Mechanics

Take the representative transaction: a Tokyo- or London-headquartered bank books a $1bn dollar reverse repo to a Cayman-domiciled fund running the cash-futures basis trade.

Leg 1 — the fund. Buys $1bn of on-the-run Treasuries, sells the corresponding future short. Books: Dr Treasury securities $1bn / Cr Repo payable $1bn. It has no dollars of its own; the position is 100% financed.

Leg 2 — the offshore bank, asset side. Dr Reverse repo (securities purchased under agreement to resell) $1bn / Cr Due to funding counterparty $1bn. In BIS LBS this appears as a cross-border USD claim on a non-bank financial institution resident in the Cayman Islands. That is exactly the +$651bn NBFI line at 2026:Q1.

Leg 3 — the offshore bank, funding side. It has no Fed account, so it cannot conjure the dollar. It re-hypothecates the received collateral and repos it out. Two routes:
(a) To a US government MMF via tri-party or FICC-cleared repo. Bank books: Dr Cash $1bn / Cr Repo payable $1bn, collateral out. The MMF books Dr Repo receivable $1bn / Cr Shareholder shares $1bn. The MMF share is the money-like liability. This shows in the FSR table twice — once as MMF assets, once as repo — hence the acknowledged double count.
(b) Through its own US branch, which does have a Fed account. The branch raises a wholesale dollar deposit (H.8 deposits at foreign-related institutions, +11.3% y/y) and books an internal claim on head office. In H.8 this moves "net due to related foreign offices" in the direction of a smaller net liability — which is what the data show, -$120bn over the year to August 2026.

Net effect on money. The fund's spendable balances are unchanged: it swapped cash for a levered Treasury position. The MMF shareholder's balances are unchanged: they moved from a bank deposit or the RRP into an MMF share. The offshore bank's balance sheet grew $1bn on each side. Gross claims in BIS LBS grew $1bn. "Money-like liabilities" in the FSR table grew by up to $2bn depending on which lines catch it. Real-economy purchasing power grew by zero.

Compare a genuine money-creating entry: a US bank writes a $1bn loan and books Dr Loans $1bn / Cr Demand deposit $1bn. New spendable claim created ex nihilo, funded by nothing. The offshore transaction above is nowhere near that. It is a chain of secured borrowing against a pre-existing security — a collateral multiplier, not a deposit multiplier.

The RRP substitution entry is even emptier. Before: MMF holds Dr Reverse repo with Federal Reserve $1 / Cr Shares $1. After: MMF holds Dr Reverse repo with dealer $1 / Cr Shares $1; dealer books Cr Repo payable $1. The FSR's repo line rises $1. Nothing was created; the counterparty changed from a central bank to a broker-dealer. That is ~$370bn of the +$2,896bn.

Where genuine offshore creation does occur: an offshore bank taking an unsecured dollar deposit and lending it on, or crediting a borrower's dollar account against a new loan and funding the resulting settlement need in the FX swap market. The first channel is measurably flat (MMF bank-related assets $467bn, unchanged since 2023). The second is unmeasured — the last BIS estimate of non-US banks' off-balance-sheet forward dollar obligations is $39trn at mid-2022, with no 2026 vintage. That is the hole in this account and it is not a small one.

## Analysis

**1. The arithmetic first.** Fed FSR (May 2026, data 2025:Q4) gives total $27,033bn, +12.0%. Implied prior-year total $24,137bn, so Δ = +$2,896bn. Component contributions (level, implied prior level, Δ, pp of the 12.0):

- Repurchase agreements: 5,887 / 4,943 / +944 / **3.91pp (33%)**
- Domestic MMFs: 7,746 / 6,855 / +891 / **3.69pp (31%)**
- Uninsured deposits: 7,608 / 7,064 / +544 / **2.25pp (19%)**
- Other (residual): 3,223 / 3,000 / +223 / **0.92pp (8%)**
- Commercial paper: 1,368 / 1,219 / +149 / **0.62pp (5%)**
- Securities lending cash collateral: 1,201 / 1,055 / +146 / **0.60pp (5%)**

Sums to 12.00pp exactly. Caveat the Fed states itself: the total exceeds the sum of listed components, and the table is basis-mixed. MMFs' repo assets are simultaneously somebody's repo liability, so lines 1 and 2 overlap economically. Do not read 33%+31% as 64% of independent growth.

**2. Ranked verdict on the six candidate drivers.**

**(3) RRP drawdown — largest identifiable single mechanism, and it is a reclassification.** ON RRP: $473.5bn (2024-12-31) → $106.0bn (2025-12-31) → $0.225bn (2026-08-20), from a $2,553.7bn peak (2022-12-30). Against that, OFR tri-party transaction volume *excluding Fed transactions* rose $1,716.5bn → $2,092.2bn (+$376bn) over the identical window, while tri-party *including* Fed shows only +$83bn. The residual between the two series is precisely the RRP balance ($473bn at 2024-12-31; the series difference reproduces it). MMF cash migrated from the Fed's repo book to dealers' repo books roughly one-for-one. That converts a Federal Reserve liability into a private "money-like liability" with no new claim on the real economy. ≈$370bn ≈ **1.5pp of the 12.0%**. The honest underlying rate is therefore closer to **10.5%**. This driver is now dead: there is nothing left in the facility.

**(1) Policy rates — enabling, not causal, and the sign is counterintuitive.** The Fed cut 175bp: 5.50% upper (through 2024-09-18) → 3.75% (2025-12-11), held every meeting since; EFFR 3.63%, IORB 3.65% (2026-08-19/21). That *narrowed* the deposit-to-cash spread. 3-month bill 3.71% (2026-08-19) versus FDIC national money-market account 0.45% and savings 0.62% (August 2026) is ~326bp, against roughly 455bp at the 2023-24 peak. On the standard deposit-beta story MMF growth should have decelerated. It did not — MMFs grew 13.0%, with government and prime funds at an identical 13.1%, so this is not reach-for-yield either. What the rate path actually contributes is *curve shape*: with Warsh in the chair, three FOMC members dissenting to hike at the 29 July 2026 meeting and the SEP median carrying +25bp for 2026, cash has no incentive to extend duration. Front-end parking is the rational trade. Call it 1-3pp of the 12.0% with wide error bars — it explains why cash stayed short, not why the stock grew.

**(2) Bill supply — the KVJ crowding-out mechanism is real but the supply is not there.** MSPD (retrieved 2026-08-21): bills $6,187bn (2024-12-31) → $6,547bn (2025-12-31), +5.8%, versus total marketable +7.0%; bill share of marketable *fell* 21.9% → 21.6%. Money-like liabilities grew at more than twice the rate of bill supply. In 2026 bills accelerate ($6,989bn at 2026-07-31, share 22.2%) but the Fed is now the marginal buyer: ~$250bn of bills purchased since early January 2026, of which ~$160bn reserve-management purchases and ~$90bn MBS-principal reinvestment, running ~$10bn/month RMP plus ~$16.5bn/month reinvestment, with TBAC projecting ~$540bn of SOMA bill demand for full-year 2026. Public bill supply net of the Fed is not displacing private money-like issuance. Contribution: **mildly negative, ~-0.5pp, swamped.**

**(4) Deposit competition and regulation — smallest of the major drivers in 2025, largest forward risk for 2027.** Uninsured deposits grew 7.7%, the slowest of the five named components and well below their own 1997-2025 average of 10.6%. FDIC QBP Q1 2026: estimated uninsured domestic deposits +$233.5bn, +2.9% q/q; total domestic deposits +$389.7bn, +2.1%, a seventh consecutive quarterly rise. This is normalisation after 2022-23, not competition-driven creation. The regulatory turn is uniformly *loosening*: the 19 March 2026 Basel III endgame re-proposal delivers roughly $87.7bn of net capital **relief** versus the ~+19% increase in the 2023 version (comments closed 18 June 2026, finalisation expected late 2026, implementation 2027); Bowman's 3 March 2026 liquidity-framework reset; Bessent arguing LCR should recognise prepositioned discount-window collateral; the Discount Window Preparedness Act of 2026 mandating test borrowing. None of this drove 2025. All of it raises the ceiling for 2027.

**(5) Stablecoins — negligible and currently shrinking.** $320bn at 2025:Q4 per the FSR, growth having *moderated* to 16% over July-December 2025; $308.0bn at 2026-08-13, +14.3% y/y but *below* the year-end level. That is 1.1% of the $27trn total and roughly **0.15pp** of the 12.0%. They sit inside the residual "other" line alongside VRDOs, fed funds, funding-agreement-backed securities, private liquidity funds, offshore money market funds, STIFs and LGIPs. GENIUS Act implementation (OCC NPR February 2026; FDIC NPR approved 7 April 2026, Federal Register 10 April 2026; ≤40% of reserves at any single eligible source; two-business-day redemption; no pass-through insurance) formalises the instrument but imposes **no Treasury-buying mandate** — IDI demand deposits and government MMF shares are equally eligible reserve assets. This confirms the project's prior finding and strengthens it.

**(6) Foreign demand — mildly negative in 2026.** TIC monthly: foreign residents' bill holdings fell in January (-$10.2bn), March (-$16.8bn), April (-$13.6bn) and May (-$43.5bn) 2026, with February the exception (+$91.6bn). Net foreign official flows oscillated (+$18.0bn February, +$49.2bn April, -$39.9bn May). Not a driver of the 2025 acceleration; a small drag in 2026.

**3. The funding-side link to offshore dollar credit — the operator's crux.** The offshore bank does not need a deposit. It needs collateral, and it runs a matched book: borrow dollars from a US government MMF via tri-party or FICC-cleared repo against Treasuries; lend those dollars to a Cayman-domiciled fund via reverse repo against the same Treasuries. It is a collateral intermediary, and its balance sheet grows on both sides without a single new spendable dollar claim being created.

The evidence lines up. OFR DVP outstanding volume — the FICC-cleared bilateral channel where sponsored hedge-fund repo lives — rose $3,418bn (2024-12-31) → $4,378.8bn (2025-12-31), **+28.1%**, and +26.0% y/y at end-March 2026 ($4,047.2bn). That +$961bn almost exactly matches the FSR's +$944bn repo increase. On the borrower side, the Fed's 22 June 2026 FEDS note puts hedge-fund gross Treasury exposures at $4.0trn (September 2025), repo cash borrowing at $3.0trn, cash-futures basis trade at $830bn, 8.5% of privately held Treasuries, all more than doubled since early 2023, top-50 funds ~90% of the total. The 15 October 2025 FEDS note traces the same complex offshore: Cayman-domiciled funds held $1.85trn of Treasuries at end-2024, absorbed 37% of net note-and-bond issuance 2022-2024, and are undercounted in TIC by ~$1.4trn precisely because FICC sponsored DVP novation breaks the cross-border reporting chain.

Observable funding legs, with an honest negative: MMF repo with foreign financial institutions is $950.5bn (2026-07-31), a record level — but only **+4.0% y/y** against total MMF assets +11.6%. That leg is *not* the marginal driver. MMF bank-related assets (CDs, CP, time deposits) are $467.4bn, flat since 2023 — the unsecured Eurodollar funding channel is dead, killed by 2016 MMF reform and never revived. What *is* growing is the US-branch channel: H.8 deposits at foreign-related institutions $1,567.7bn (2026-08-05), **+11.3% y/y** against +6.5% for all US commercial banks. And net due to related foreign offices at those same institutions fell $954.3bn (2025-08-06) → $834.4bn (2026-08-05), -12.6%. On the standard sign convention (positive = US office is a net borrower from its affiliates), foreign banks' US offices cut their net draw on the group by ~$120bn while raising ~$159bn more in US deposits — roughly $280bn of dollars raised onshore and pushed out to the group's offshore book.

**4. Is the constraint binding?** No. SOFR–IORB averaged -1.5bp across 2026 YTD (38 of 158 days positive) versus -2.9bp in 2025; SOFR 99th percentile 3.70% against IORB 3.65% (2026-08-19) — a 5bp tail, not stress. Reserves $2,935bn (2026-08-19) with the Fed adding via RMPs, TGA at $953.6bn on a raised target. The collateral-funding channel has room to run.

## Cannot establish

**The FX swap leg has no current vintage.** The only quantification of non-US banks' off-balance-sheet forward dollar obligations remains ~$39trn at mid-2022 (BIS QR December 2022). There is no 2024, 2025 or 2026 update. Since this is the single largest potential offshore dollar funding channel and by construction invisible in LBS, any attribution of the funding side is incomplete by an unknown and possibly dominant margin. This is the largest hole in this account and I will not fill it with a plausible number.

**The FSR Table 4.1 total cannot be cleanly decomposed.** The Fed's own note states the total exceeds the sum of listed components. The residual "other" ($3,223bn) is reverse-engineered, not published, and bundles VRDOs, fed funds, funding-agreement-backed securities, private liquidity funds, offshore MMFs, STIFs, LGIPs and stablecoins with no sub-detail. MMF repo assets and dealer repo liabilities are the same transactions counted on both the MMF line and the repo line; the overlap is not published, so the 3.91pp and 3.69pp contributions cannot be added.

**The overlap between the FSR repo line and BIS cross-border NBFI claims cannot be measured.** I can show they move together and describe the same transactions, but I cannot quantify what fraction of the FSR's +$944bn repo increase is the same dollars as the BIS's +$651bn NBFI claim increase. No published crosswalk exists.

**FBO branch dollar lending booked offshore is not published.** H.8 gives deposits at foreign-related institutions and net due to related foreign offices, but not the destination or currency composition of what those branches fund. The ~$280bn inference (deposits +$159bn plus net-due reduction of $120bn) is a directional balance-sheet identity, not a measured flow, and rests on the standard H.8 sign convention (positive net due to = US office is net borrower from affiliates). Treat as indicative.

**MMF AUM growth cannot be attributed to investor sector.** N-MFP gives asset composition, not the sector of the shareholder. Whether the +$891bn came from corporate treasuries, retail, foreign investors or securities-lending cash pools is not determinable from published data.

**Basel III endgame's effect on offshore dollar lending capacity cannot be quantified.** The ~$87.7bn net capital relief is an aggregate agency estimate for US banking organisations. Its allocation to foreign banking organisations' US operations, and thence to offshore dollar books, is not disclosed and depends on a final rule that does not exist yet.

**No series measures offshore dollar deposit creation directly.** BIS LBS reports cross-border liabilities of banks located outside the US in USD ($14.11trn per project context), but with no counterparty sector, no counterparty country in the foreign-currency residence cut, NaN under confidentiality for Singapore, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia and Malaysia, and structural understatement of Chinese banks. The share of that stock that is genuinely a new deposit created by an offshore loan, versus a redeposited existing dollar, is unknowable from published statistics.

**The FSR MMF line, Z.1 and OFR N-MFP disagree and cannot be reconciled here.** 2025:Q4 domestic MMFs: FSR $7,746bn; Z.1 (MMMFFAQ027S) $8,190bn; OFR N-MFP total investments $8,305bn. Different universes and different bases. Do not treat them as substitutes.

## Challenges to project context

**1. "Unambiguously money creation" overstates what the BIS number contains.** At 2026:Q1 the fastest-growing component of cross-border USD credit is claims on non-bank financial institutions (+$651bn of the +$1.1trn to non-banks), with borrowers located in the US, UK, Cayman Islands and Japan. Cross-check: BIS GLI shows USD credit to non-bank borrowers outside the US growing only **+7.3%** ($14.7trn, end-March 2026) against **+12%** for total cross-border USD credit. The gap is the financial circuit — interbank and bank-to-fund. These are overwhelmingly reverse repo claims financing levered Treasury positions (Cayman funds held $1.85trn of Treasuries at end-2024 and absorbed 37% of net note/bond issuance 2022-24). Collateral re-use expands gross balance sheets on both sides without creating a spendable claim on the real economy. The channel is unambiguously *large* and unambiguously *outside every monetary aggregate*; "unambiguously money creation" should be softened to "unambiguously credit and collateral creation, with the money-creating share unmeasured."

**2. The headline vintage is stale and the number has accelerated.** The project cites +$1.90trn / +11.9% to 2026:Q1 from a bilateral-position cut. The BIS statistical release for end-March 2026 (rppb2607, published July 2026) gives +12% y/y with **+$793bn in Q1 2026 alone — the largest quarterly expansion since the onset of Covid**. That is a stronger and more citable framing than the four-quarter sum. Refresh to this release.

**3. ~1.5pp of the FSR's 12.0% is not creation.** Tri-party repo ex-Fed +$376bn against ON RRP -$367bn over 2025 is a near one-for-one counterparty substitution. The underlying rate is ~10.5%, not 12.0%. It matters because the source is now exhausted — RRP is $0.225bn (2026-08-20) — so this component cannot repeat.

**4. The dollar is not the fastest-growing offshore currency credit.** Euro foreign-currency credit grew **+12%** to €5.1trn at end-March 2026 versus +7.3% for the dollar on the same GLI measure. The dollar's case rests on level and systemic role, not growth rate. Any framing that says "the dollar channel is uniquely fast" is wrong on current data.

**5. Stablecoins have shrunk since the reference date.** $320bn at 2025:Q4 versus $308.0bn at 2026-08-13. The project's finding that the stablecoins-buy-bills story is weak is not just weak — the position is currently contracting in absolute terms. Also, the GENIUS framework as proposed creates **no** Treasury-buying mandate: IDI demand deposits and qualifying MMF shares are equally eligible reserve assets, and the FDIC's ≤40%-per-source diversification rule pushes *toward* spreading reserves across bank deposits, not toward bills.

**6. The deposit-competition story is inverted.** The 175bp of cuts narrowed the deposit-to-cash spread by roughly 130bp (455bp → 326bp), which on standard deposit-beta logic should have *slowed* the migration into MMFs. MMFs grew 13.0% anyway, with government and prime funds at an identical 13.1%. Whatever is driving MMF growth, it is not spread-chasing. Any model attributing the 2025 acceleration to the deposit-MMF spread has the sign wrong.

**7. The project should note that the US MMF-to-offshore-bank funding leg is NOT accelerating.** MMF repo with foreign financial institutions is a record $950.5bn (2026-07-31) but only +4.0% y/y against total MMF assets +11.6%. If the intuition is that US money funds are bankrolling the offshore dollar expansion at the margin, the N-MFP data say no. The growing leg is the FBO US branch (+11.3% y/y deposits).

# WHAT STOPS IT — constraints on money-like liability creation, and the failure modes: collateral, regulation, the run record and backstop map, and the price signals that reveal when manufacturing capacity binds.

**Headline.** The binding constraint on offshore dollar credit is not deposits, not collateral supply, and not capital — it is intraday settlement balances at a handful of US banks, and the market has published its exact price: dealers will not use the Fed's repo backstop until repo trades 10bp above the SRP rate, banks not until 25bp (NY Fed surveys, April 2026). Against the project context, the fastest-growing money-like liability is repo at +19.1% y/y — the $27trn stock is growing through its most collateral-dependent and most narrowly backstopped channel — and the $3,223bn "other" residual the project derived is not a rounding line: roughly $2.2trn of it is dollar-denominated offshore MMFs and short-term investment funds, i.e. the funding counterparty of the very offshore dollar credit creation the project's headline measures. The two findings are one trade seen from both sides, and neither side has a backstop.

## Mechanics

Entry by entry, four funding routes for a dollar loan booked outside the US.

**A. Funding by offshore deposit (no US settlement).** Offshore bank: Dr Loan to client (USD), Cr USD deposit of client. If the borrower pays another client of the same bank, nothing further happens — this is genuine offshore dollar money creation and it is what BIS LBS Q.F.C.A.USD.F.5J.A.5A.A.5J.N picks up. No reserve, no collateral. The constraint is only the bank's willingness to hold a USD open position and its NSFR/LCR in USD.

**B. The moment it settles.** Borrower pays a US resident. Offshore bank must deliver Fed reserves: Dr USD deposit (extinguished), Cr Due to US branch. US branch: Dr Due from head office, Cr Reserve balance at FRB. Receiving US bank: Dr Reserve balance, Cr customer deposit. Reserves are conserved system-wide; the FBO branch's reserve balance falls. H.8, week ended 5 Aug 2026: foreign-related institutions hold $1,149.2bn cash assets, net due to related foreign offices $803.8bn — the head office lends the branch the dollars, the branch holds the reserves. Total reserves $2,935.3bn (H.4.1, 19 Aug 2026).

**C. Funding by FX swap.** Offshore bank: Dr USD cash, Cr USD payable at maturity; Cr JPY cash, Dr JPY receivable. Full principal exchange, no netting, off balance sheet under accounting — this is the BIS "missing debt" (>$80trn of FX swap/forward/currency swap dollar obligations worldwide, 2022 Triennial; non-US banks >$35trn; non-US non-banks ~$26trn against $13trn on balance sheet). The dealer on the other side must source the dollars from its reserve account or from repo. The constraint lands back on a US bank's reserve position, and the price of the constraint is the cross-currency basis, not SOFR.

**D. Funding by repo.** Cash borrower: Dr Securities purchased under agreement to resell, Cr Repo liability. Cash lender (MMF): Dr Reverse repo, Cr — funded by shares. This is collateral-manufactured money and it is the fastest-growing runnable in Table 4.1 (+19.1% to $5,887bn, 2025Q4). Mechanically the collateral must be Treasury or agency for the government MMF pool to lend against it, which is why the MMF-reform-driven migration into government funds forced the money-like stock into the collateral channel.

**E. Where the two systems touch.** The residual "other" line of $3,223bn contains roughly $2.2trn of dollar-denominated offshore MMFs and STIFs (FSR, end-2025), $1–2trn of it prime-like. Those vehicles: Dr CD/CP of a non-US bank, Cr shares redeemable at par on demand. That is the missing liability side of the project's offshore credit finding — a US-measured, US-unbackstopped money-like claim funding a non-US bank's dollar book.

**F. Backstop entries, for completeness.** SRP: Fed Dr Securities purchased under agreement to resell, Cr Reserve balance of counterparty. It creates reserves against Treasury/agency/agency-MBS collateral only, for PDs and eligible depositories only, at 3.75%, capped at $40bn per security type per operation. Discount window: Fed Dr Loan, Cr Reserve balance, against prepositioned collateral. FIMA repo: Fed Dr Repo, Cr Foreign official account — the only standing facility reaching the offshore official sector, and the only one that converts a foreign central bank's Treasuries into reserves without a swap line. Nothing in this list reaches an offshore MMF, an offshore bank without a US depository, a securities-lending agent's reinvestment pool, or a stablecoin issuer.

## Analysis

**1. The operator's premise, sharpened.** He is right that a non-US bank must fund a dollar loan. The sharper statement is that deposits and collateral are not the constraint — they are two routes to the constraint, which is *settlement balances at the Federal Reserve at the moment the borrower spends*. A bank in Tokyo or London can book a USD loan against a USD time deposit or an FX swap and nothing happens; the constraint binds only when the borrower pays a US resident and the transfer must clear Fedwire.

That is measurable. H.8 (week ended 5 Aug 2026, released 14 Aug 2026): foreign-related institutions in the US held **$3,478.6bn** of assets, of which **$1,149.2bn cash assets** — overwhelmingly reserves — funded in part by **$803.8bn net due to related foreign offices**. Against total reserve balances of **$2,935.3bn** (H.4.1, week ended 19 Aug 2026), foreign branches hold roughly **39%** of the settlement asset. The offshore dollar system's entire unconditional claim on the Fed sits in that $1.15trn, and it is borrowed from head offices. It is the joint, and it is thin relative to $17.86trn of cross-border USD claims.

The Dallas Fed states the mechanism precisely (Levy & McCormick, 12 Feb 2026): "lending into FX swap or repo markets reduces the reserves on hand for an individual institution." Reserves are conserved in aggregate but not for the lender. So every incremental offshore dollar loan that actually settles requires a US bank to give up intraday liquidity.

**2. The collateral constraint does not bind on supply; it binds on the balance sheet that intermediates collateral.** Aggregate supply is ample: T-bills outstanding **$7.0trn** at 31 July 2026, **22.2%** of marketable debt (Treasury presentation to TBAC, Q3 FY2026). The repo market is **$12.6trn** in daily exposures at 2025Q3 — FICC $4.4trn, tri-party $3.1trn, NCCBR $5.0trn — about **$700bn larger** than pre-NCCBR estimates (OFR, 4 Dec 2025). Treasuries collateralise **69.4%** overall but only **61.8%** of NCCBR and **52.6%** of tri-party.

Two things bite. First, encumbrance in a specific form: the May 2026 FSR notes US G-SIBs hold much of their HQLA in **HTM long-duration agency MBS** priced well below book, so selling would taint the portfolio; they "would likely rely on repo market access rather than outright asset sales." Their liquidity is a claim on someone else's repo balance sheet, not on their own assets. Second, and undernoticed: the Fed's own fix is draining collateral from the non-bank system. Reserve management purchases began December 2025 at **$40bn/month, conducted entirely in Treasury bills** (Perli, 26 Mar 2026). RMPs swap bills — usable by MMFs, corporates and offshore cash pools — for reserves, usable only by Fed account holders. Easing the bank constraint tightens the non-bank one.

**3. The regulatory constraint has just been relaxed, and the relaxation was aimed at the backstop.** The eSLR final rule (25 Nov 2025) caps the depository-subsidiary eSLR buffer at 1% (4% total), effective **1 April 2026** with early adoption from 1 January 2026; aggregate BHC tier 1 requirement falls about **$13bn**, under 2%. Treasury frames the cost of the current framework bluntly: safe assets are **25% of large-bank balance sheets, up from roughly 10% pre-crisis** (Bessent remarks, delivered by McKernan, 3 March 2026). Bowman, the same day, called the LCR "an isolated, unusable buffer" and the twelve-Reserve-Bank discount window fragmented and stigmatised. The agencies rescinded the LCR FAQs in February 2026 and Treasury proposes capped LCR recognition of prepositioned discount window collateral with a demonstrated-usage multiplier.

But the regulation that actually binds intraday is none of these. It is the **internal liquidity stress test**: overnight ILSTs make banks conserve intraday liquidity, and since **64% of cleared repo trades by 8:30am** and roughly three-quarters of tri-party by 9am, a bank asked to lend at 7am does not yet know its own needs. The Dallas Fed's conclusion is the important one: relaxing ILSTs would lower steady-state reserve demand but would *not* make banks elastic marginal repo lenders — they would simply hold fewer reserves.

MMF reform is the other binding regulation, and its effect runs straight into the offshore question. The 2023 SEC rules impose a mandatory liquidity fee on institutional prime and tax-exempt funds when daily net redemptions exceed 5% of NAV. Institutional prime is now **$247.6bn** of **$7.93trn** total MMF assets (ICI, week ended 12 Aug 2026) — **3% of MMF assets** (FSR). The natural buyer of foreign-bank CP and CDs has been regulated out. What is left of that channel is small: total CP **$1,441.2bn**, financial CP **$623.0bn**, of which **foreign financial $369.5bn** (Fed CP release, 19 Aug 2026). Against $17.86trn of cross-border USD claims, unsecured US CP funds roughly 2%. The cash went instead into government MMFs (**$6,375bn**, +13.1%), which can hold only government paper and repo backed by it. That is the mechanical reason repo grew 19.1%.

**4. The run record and the backstop map — the useful output.** Taking FSR Table 4.1 (2025Q4) component by component:

*Uninsured deposits $7,608bn (+7.7%)* — ran March 2023. Stopped by the FDIC systemic risk exception (discretionary: two-thirds FDIC, two-thirds Fed, Treasury Secretary in consultation with the President), the BTFP (13(3), created 12 Mar 2023 on a $25bn ESF backstop, closed to new loans 11 Mar 2024), and the discount window at **$152.85bn** for the week ended 15 Mar 2023 against a prior record of $111bn in 2008. Standing backstop: the discount window, conditional on prepositioning. As of 2023 nearly **3,000 institutions** had prepledged collateral, two-thirds of it loans; prepledging raises borrowing probability by ~5%, and ~10.5% for banks with below-median reserve ratios (FEDS Notes, 29 Aug 2025). **PARTIAL, discretionary.**

*Government MMFs $6,375bn* — never runs out; runs in. ON RRP is an investment outlet, not a facility, and is now de minimis (**$225mn**, 20 Aug 2026, at 3.50%). **N/A as a run risk; it is the transmitter.**

*Prime MMFs $1,220bn* — ran 2008 and 2020. Public institutional prime peaked at **−35%** cumulative outflows in March 2020; roughly **$100bn / 30%** over 11–24 March; of ~$80bn of CP and CDs sold, **66% ($53bn) was pledged to the MMLF**, which peaked at $53.17bn and expired 31 Mar 2021. Standing backstop today: **NONE**. The 2023 rules substituted a *price* — the liquidity fee — for a facility. Untested.

*Repo $5,887bn (+19.1%)* — ran September 2019 and March 2020; PDCF peaked at $33.41bn and expired 31 Mar 2021. Standing backstop: the SRP, but scope-limited (FAQ, 10 Dec 2025): primary dealers and eligible depositories only; **only Treasuries, agency debt, agency MBS**; $40bn proposition cap per security type per operation; BNY tri-party only; two-year lagged name disclosure; no central clearing, therefore no netting. **PARTIAL** — the uncovered part is the ~30% of repo not on government collateral and every cash borrower that is not a PD or SRF-eligible depository: hedge funds, sponsored-repo clients, and non-US banks without a US depository.

*Commercial paper $1,368bn (+12.2%)* — froze 2008 and 2020 (CPFF peak $4.25bn in 2020; expired 2021). **NONE.**

*Securities-lending cash collateral $1,201bn (+13.8%)* — ran 2008. Agent-lender indemnities cover borrower default, **not** cash-collateral reinvestment loss. **NONE.**

*"Other" $3,223bn* — the Fed does not print this line; it is 27,033 minus the 23,810 of listed components. Roughly **$2.2trn** of it is dollar-denominated offshore MMFs and STIFs at end-2025, of which **$1–2trn** is prime-like (FSR, explicitly uncertain to ±$1trn). Stablecoins are **$320bn**, +16% Jul–Dec 2025, concentrated in two issuers; the GENIUS Act grants **no master account, no discount window, and no FDIC insurance**, and the FDIC's April 2026 NPRM confirms reserve deposits are not insured pass-through to holders. **NONE, statutorily.**

**Fragility map:** CP 1,368 + securities lending 1,201 + other 3,223 + prime MMFs 1,220 = **$7,012bn with no standing backstop — 26% of the $27,033bn total, about 22% of GDP** — before counting the uncovered slice of repo. And the offshore MMF/STIF pool inside "other" is precisely where the project's two headline findings meet.

**5. The price signals.** SRP is at the top of the target range (3.75%), ON RRP at the bottom (3.50%), IORB 15bp above the bottom (3.65%); range 3.50–3.75 effective 11 Dec 2025. On 19 Aug 2026 SOFR printed **3.62%** (−3bp to IORB) on $2,923bn, TGCR 3.60%, EFFR 3.63%. SRP take-up has been **zero to $2mn per operation** through 7–20 August 2026. Nothing is binding today.

The thresholds are published. Primary dealers' median hurdle spread to actively consider the SRP is **SRP + 10bp**; banks' is **SRP + 25bp**, narrowed from 38bp (NY Fed Teller Window, 14 May 2026, from April 2026 surveys). Dealers' single largest stated deterrent is the inability to net SRP against client activity — i.e. the leverage ratio constrains the *backstop*, which is why the eSLR recalibration matters more for crisis capacity than for normal-times lending. So: SOFR above IORB+10bp on a non-statement day means capacity is binding. The record is **+32bp on 31 October 2025**, the largest one-day SOFR-over-IORB spike in five years; SRP took $50.35bn that day and a record **$74.6bn on 31 December 2025** ($31.5bn Treasury, $43.1bn MBS). Perli's ampleness indicators — share of repo above IORB, share of late-day bank payments — approached Q1 2019 levels in late 2025.

For the offshore leg the signal is the FX swap premium, not SOFR. BoJ (FSR, 21 Apr 2026, Chart II-2-8) reports dollar funding premiums "generally at low levels," widening temporarily for year-end and Middle East tension. BoJ Chart IV-3-14 shows major Japanese banks' loan-to-funding gap with stable funding — deposits, corporate bonds, and **medium- and long-term FX and currency swaps** — exceeding loans at 2025Q4. That is the operator's own house's answer to his question, from his own regulator.

## Cannot establish

**1. Current collateral velocity / reuse.** No published series exists. Manmohan Singh's IMF work (WP/11/256, WP/17/113) is the only quantified estimate and it is a decade-plus old ($10trn of pledged-collateral funding pre-Lehman; a $4–5trn post-Lehman reduction in source collateral times velocity). The OFR's NCCBR collection captures haircuts, rates and collateral type but the OFR has published no reuse or velocity statistic. I could not verify the project's established finding that collateral velocity has been flat for 2–3 years from any primary source, and I doubt the framing (see challenges).

**2. Numerical cross-currency basis levels for 2026.** The BoJ FSR (21 Apr 2026) charts 3m FX-swap dollar funding premiums and 1y basis-swap alpha on an inverse scale to −0.8% and describes them as "generally at low levels" with temporary year-end and geopolitical widening, but publishes no table. Nothing I found for EUR/USD or JPY/USD 2026 levels came from a primary source; the search hits were vendor and blog material and I have not used them. This matters: the cross-currency basis is the single most informative price for the operator's question, and I cannot give him a sourced level.

**3. Bills–SOFR OIS spreads.** The Treasury presentation to TBAC (Q3 FY2026) charts them by tenor for Dec-25 to Jun-26 on a ±25bp axis, but publishes no underlying values and the PDF is a raster chart. Treasury–SOFR swap spread levels for 2026 are likewise unavailable to me from a primary source.

**4. The size of the offshore prime-like money-like pool.** The Fed itself will only say $1trn–$2trn within a $2.2trn offshore MMF/STIF aggregate, and the source is iMoneyNet, a commercial vendor. My fragility-map total of $7,012bn therefore carries roughly ±$1trn of uncertainty concentrated in its most fragile line.

**5. Decomposition of non-US banks' dollar funding by instrument.** BIS gives FX swap notionals, not a funding decomposition. BoJ Chart IV-3-15 decomposes major Japanese banks' dollar funding cost into deposits, repos, and FX/currency swaps but publishes no shares. There is no series that answers "what fraction of the $17.86trn of cross-border USD claims is funded by FX swaps."

**6. Whether hurdle spreads hold under stress.** The 10bp/25bp SRP hurdles are survey responses to hypothetical one-day scenarios, not revealed behaviour. The one revealed data point — $74.6bn taken on 31 Dec 2025 — is a year-end, not a stress.

**7. SRF counterparty composition.** The FRBNY counterparty list pages returned 404s in this session, so I cannot state how many US branches or subsidiaries of foreign banks are SRF-eligible depositories — which is exactly the fact a Japanese mega bank would want. Eligibility criteria are published (Treasury/agency/agency-MBS holdings >$5bn or total assets >$30bn) but the roster is not verified here.

**8. Depository-level eSLR capital release.** The final rule's press release says the depository-level reduction is larger than the ~$13bn holding-company figure but does not quantify it in the material I could read; the Federal Register text was behind a redirect wall and the PDF did not extract.

## Challenges to project context

**1. Vintage error.** The runnable-liabilities table is from the **May 2026** Financial Stability Report, published **8 May 2026**, data through **2025:Q4**, market data as of 23 April 2026 — not "April 2026." Under the project's own vintage rule this matters, and the next edition (~November 2026) will restate.

**2. The composition of the +12.0% is more important than the level, and it points the opposite way from the project's framing.** Of the ~$2.9trn increase in 2025, repo contributed roughly $944bn (+19.1%) and uninsured deposits only ~$544bn (+7.7%). The money-like stock is growing through the **collateral-manufactured** channel, not the deposit channel. That inverts the natural reading of the project's headline: the constraint the operator is asking about — "you need deposits or collateral" — has already resolved decisively toward collateral, and collateral-manufactured money is the piece with the narrowest backstop, the most concentrated infrastructure (FICC), and the highest sensitivity to dealer balance sheet.

**3. The "other" line and the offshore credit finding are the same phenomenon.** The project treats the $1.90trn/+11.9% offshore dollar credit creation and the $27,033bn money-like stock as two separate findings on a nine-channel map. They meet inside the $3,223bn residual: roughly $2.2trn of dollar-denominated offshore MMFs and STIFs, $1–2trn of it prime-like, whose portfolios are non-US bank CDs and CP. That is the missing liability side of the offshore credit expansion, it sits in a line the Fed does not print, its size is vendor-estimated with ±$1trn uncertainty, and it has no backstop on either the fund side or the issuer side. This should be promoted from residual to headline.

**4. "Collateral velocity has been flat for 2–3 years on Basel III constraints" is not supportable as stated, and may be measuring the wrong thing.** No primary series exists (see what_cannot_be_established). The observable substitutes point the other way: FICC sponsored repo up **150% in two years to $2.856trn** (Dec 2025), FICC total clearing above $12trn average daily, NCCBR revealed at **$5.0trn** — $700bn more repo than anyone previously measured. The likely truth is that collateral reuse has **migrated from dealer rehypothecation into CCP netting**, which no velocity metric captures, and that Basel III displaced reuse rather than suppressing it. If the project is going to keep that finding it needs a source and a definition.

**5. The binding regulatory constraint is misidentified in the usual telling.** It is not the leverage ratio and not the LCR. It is the **internal liquidity stress test** plus daylight-overdraft avoidance, operating at 7–8:30am when 64% of cleared repo trades. The Dallas Fed's finding is that relaxing ILSTs would lower steady-state reserve demand without making banks elastic marginal lenders — so the fashionable 2026 deregulatory agenda (eSLR done, LCR next) will not fix the elasticity problem the project should care about.

**6. The eSLR constraint has already been relaxed, effective 1 April 2026.** Any forward-looking statement in the project that treats leverage-ratio balance sheet as a fixed constraint on repo intermediation is now stale. Note the specific mechanism: dealers' single largest stated reason for not using the Fed's own repo backstop is the inability to net SRP against client activity — leverage regulation constrains the **backstop**, not only private manufacturing. That is the strongest argument that the eSLR change matters more for crisis capacity than for lending.

**7. A tension the project should own.** The Fed's remedy for the late-2025 reserve shortage — $40bn/month of RMPs conducted **entirely in Treasury bills** — swaps collateral usable by the non-bank and offshore money-like system for reserves usable only by Fed account holders. Against $7.0trn of bills outstanding this is small, but the direction is unambiguous: easing the bank constraint tightens the non-bank collateral constraint, and the offshore dollar system sits entirely on the non-bank side of that line.

**8. Supporting, not challenging, one project claim.** "US non-financial corporates fund 100% of capex from internal funds; there is no aggregate external funding gap requiring a monetary explanation." The leverage data strengthens this: hedge fund repo borrowing +154% since 2022, Treasury positions $4.1trn (+$1trn in 2025), broker-dealer assets +19.8%. The $27trn money-like stock is funding **financial positions**, not investment. That is the correct answer to "what is the money for," and it is why the run constraint — not the credit-demand constraint — is the one that matters.
