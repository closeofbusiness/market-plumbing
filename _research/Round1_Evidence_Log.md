(3) FABRICATED AND WRONG ATTRIBUTIONS. (4) BOTH CANONICAL EXAMPLES ARE HISTORICALLY SHAKY AT THE LOAD-BEARING LINK. ### Verifier 2 — `materially_flawed`

The underlying CONCEPT is sound and has a respectable literature behind it. The CASE LIBRARY as constituted cannot anchor a professional report without substantial rework. Four structural problems:

(1) FOLK HISTORY. The two cases the operator was told to check hardest split in opposite directions. The electricity story is BETTER supported than the library thinks: case 27 attacks the operator's canonical example by attributing to David Nye a claim that factories reorganised before electrification, but the standard peer-reviewed position (Paul David 1990, "The Dynamo and the Computer"; Warren Devine 1983, "From Shafts to Wires") is exactly the operator's — that the productivity payoff waited ~40 years for the shift from group drive to unit drive, which permitted floor layout by process flow rather than by proximity to a line shaft. Case 27 should be merged into case 8 as a caveat, not run as a rebuttal. The dark-fibre story is WORSE supported than case 20 thinks but case 28's correction is itself false. I verified that Cogent was founded in 1999 (during the bubble, not after the crash) and Internap in 1996 — case 28's central evidentiary claim, that these carriers were "founded AFTER the crash to buy stranded assets," is wrong on both counts. The defensible version, which the same source supports, is that Cogent acquired assets from 13 failing carriers in 2001-2004, obtaining roughly $14bn of book capital for about $60m. That is not "AWS lit dark fibre"; it is distressed recapitalisation collapsing the wholesale bandwidth price, which is Perez's installation-to-deployment transition. Rewrite the mechanism, keep the case.

(2) UNFALSIFIABILITY, demonstrated internally. The library does not need an outside critic to show that layers are assigned arbitrarily — it contradicts itself. The assembly line is layer 3 in case 8 and layer 1 in case 14. Semiconductors are layer 3 in case 11 and layer 1 in case 15. Crypto mining is layer 3 in case 24 and layer 1 in case 26. Cheap electricity is layer 3 in case 24 and layer 1 in cases 8/27. Railways are layer 3 in case 4 and layer 1 in cases 2/3/32. Every layer 3 in this set is some other entry's layer 1. That is inherent to a chain: the labels are indexical to where the analyst starts counting, so "third order" is a statement about the observer's window, not about the world. The framework only becomes falsifiable if the window is fixed ex ante and failure conditions are pre-specified. The null cases (29 nuclear, 33 3G) are what supply that discipline and are the most valuable entries in the library despite being labelled failures.

(3) THE "ev" FIELD MEASURES THE WRONG THING. It rates whether the facts are true, not whether the case instantiates the definition. Cases 4 (Bessemer), 6 (fluyt), 15 (transistor cost), 17 (GPS), 21 (smartphone components) are all rated "strong" and are all factually fine, but none has a bubble, a malinvestment, or a forced reorganisation at layer 2 — they are cost-curve diffusion stories with threshold effects. That is Bresnahan-Trajtenberg GPT diffusion, a different and much less interesting claim than the operator's. Roughly a third of the library is this. Sorting on "ev" therefore promotes the cases that fit the thesis least.

(4) THE OPERATOR'S OWN CRITERIA ARE NOT MET BY HIS OWN LIBRARY. The stated lag is 20-50 years. Library lags range from 3 years (COVID mRNA 2020 to oncology trials) to 300 (printing press to Scientific Revolution); cases 21, 22, 25, 26 are all under a decade. The stated "value accrues to different people" criterion fails outright in Japan (39) and Korea (40), where the same keiretsu and chaebol captured all three layers — Samsung is simultaneously the layer-2 malinvestor and the layer-3 winner. Either narrow the criteria or drop the cases; do not publish both.

Two further observations worth putting in the report. First, the single best falsification test in the library is one it does not notice: the Black Death (37) delivered the identical demographic shock across Europe, and west of the Elbe it produced wage labour while east of the Elbe it produced the "second serfdom" — intensified bondage. Same layer 1, same layer 2 labour scarcity, opposite layer 3, determined by pre-existing institutional and political structure. That case should anchor the report's honest section: cascades are contingent on institutions, and the framework has no predictive content without them. Second, case 34 is a category error — it applies the framework to itself and calls it a case. Its argument is correct and belongs in the report's methodology section, but it is not a data point and inflates the count.

### Verifier 3 — `materially_flawed`

## 1. The framework is not new, and its real names matter because they already contain its known failure modes

"Third derivative" is a re-description of four established literatures, each of which has already fought the battle the operator is about to lose. **General Purpose Technology theory** (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg 1995) names layer 1. **Co-invention / complementary organisational capital** (Paul David, "The Dynamo and the Computer," 1990; Devine 1983; Brynjolfsson–Hitt) names layer 2 as reorganisation, and is literally the source of the operator's own electricity example — the operator is citing David's argument back as an original observation. **Carlota Perez's installation/frenzy → turning point → deployment** (2002) names layer 2 as bubble. **Janeway's "productive bubble"** names the malinvestment-leaves-a-residue claim. The lag is the **productivity J-curve** (Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson 2021); the unpredictability is the **adjacent possible**.

This matters for one reason: Perez, who is the closest match, spent her later work (esp. "The Double Bubble at the Turn of the Century," 2009) insisting on a distinction the operator's framework collapses — between **technology bubbles**, which build an object, and **easy-money/asset-inflation bubbles**, which do not. The operator's dataset contains both, graded identically, and in one place grades the same event both ways.

## 2. The dataset does not survive contact with itself

Three internal contradictions, none of which require external facts to see:

- **"Securitization and Modern Finance" (ev: strong)** scores the CDO/shadow-banking complex as a successful third order. **"Subprime Housing Bubble" (ev: strong)** scores the same decade, the same instruments, as having *no* third order. Both cannot be right. That the framework can call a ~$2–3trn value destruction a "third order" shows it has no way to distinguish value creation from value destruction even *retrospectively*.
- **Containerization appears twice** (1956; 1950–1980) with different layer-2 content, different mechanisms and different evidence grades. **Dot-com appears twice** with opposite verdicts ("contested" vs "MECHANISM IS WRONG").
- **Layer assignment is provably non-unique.** The assembly line is layer 3 in "Factory Electrification" and layer 1 in "Automobile Mass Production." Semiconductors are layer 3 of Apollo and layer 1 of the PC case. Containerization is layer 1 in one entry and an unnamed layer 3 of the Interstate in another. If any event can occupy any layer depending on where you cut, then "find the third order" reduces to "wait, then draw the boundary so the answer is three steps away." This is the arbitrariness charge, and the dataset proves it against itself without any help from me.

## 3. The prospective claim: why it cannot work as stated

**a) No denominator.** 41 cases, ~35 positive, 6 negative. The sampling frame is "famous transitions." Not one case was selected by a rule stated *before* the outcome. A hit rate computed on this set is meaningless. The honest base rate would require enumerating every large overbuild of the last 200 years — bicycle boom, 1920s radio, 1920s Florida land, 1980s Japanese real estate, 1980s biotech, 1990s Iridium/LEO satellites, 2000s ethanol, 2010s on-demand delivery, 2014 shale itself as an equity story — and asking how many produced a distinct third generation. The operator's own "ten bubbles that left nothing" intuition is probably closer to the truth than his dataset.

**b) No stopping rule, therefore unfalsifiable.** Nuclear is graded "third order: ABSENT" in 2026 — but 2023–26 SMR and AI-datacentre nuclear PPAs are exactly the kind of thing that, in ten years, would be written up as a 70-year third order. Blockchain is graded "unclear." Every negative is convertible to "not yet"; every positive is convertible to "coincidence." A theory whose negatives have no expiry date makes no forecasts.

**c) No counterfactual, anywhere, in 41 cases.** The framework never once asks: would layer 3 have appeared without the overbuild? The available evidence cuts *against* necessity. South Korea and Japan built world-leading broadband by state directive with no fibre bubble. China built hyperscale cloud (Alibaba, Tencent) without a US-style telecom bust. Ford's Highland Park line and the cloud both had capacity built for them on demand elsewhere. If capacity would have been built anyway — slower, cheaper, without the bankruptcies — then the bubble transferred wealth from layer-2 shareholders to layer-3 shareholders and created nothing. That is a redistribution mechanism, not a value-creation mechanism, and you cannot invest in it prospectively because the transfer only exists at the trough.

**d) Reflexivity destroys the trade.** Every strong case's mechanism is *distressed pricing*. The single best verified datum in the whole file is one I confirmed this session: Cogent acquired roughly $14bn of book-value assets for about $60m. That is the mechanism, and it is a **liquidity strategy, not a foresight strategy** — it pays off if you have capital at the moment everyone else is a forced seller. If the framework becomes widely believed, layer-2 assets are bid up, the 200:1 discount never appears, and the mechanism is destroyed by its own adoption.

**e) "Different people capture it" is roughly a coin flip, and the exceptions are the biggest ones.** NVIDIA captured layer 2 *and* layer 3. Intel did. Samsung did. Amazon was a layer-2 dot-com survivor that became the layer-3 winner. The regularity holds only where a *forced separation* occurred (AT&T divestiture, bankruptcy liquidation) — which makes it a consequence of legal structure, not of the derivative structure.

**f) The mechanisms were written backwards, and several are demonstrably wrong.** I verified four inversions (see corrections): Southwest existed and was profitable for seven years *before* the layer-2 event that supposedly made it possible; Railway Time predates the Railway Mania; the distressed buyers were founded *before* the crash; shale volumes are off by ~10x. When you write mechanism text knowing the answer, you get plausible prose regardless of whether the arrow points that way. That is the hindsight charge, evidenced.

## 4. What survives

The core observation is real but it is not the operator's. **Capacity built ahead of demand, by owners who then lose title to it, sometimes becomes the cheap substrate for applications that were already known and already blocked on price.** That is defensible. What does not survive is: the three-layer counting (arbitrary), the "unpredictable" claim (false — see below), the evidence base (materially erroneous), and the prospective use as stated.

## 5. The single load-bearing assumption

**That the overbuild is counterfactually necessary — i.e. that layer 3 was supply-constrained, not demand-constrained.** If layer 3's arrival was gated by demand, capability or institutions rather than by the availability of cheap stranded capacity, then the overbuild is decoration on a story whose real driver is elsewhere, and there is nothing to act on. Nothing in the 41 cases tests this, and the Korea/Japan/China broadband comparisons suggest it is often false.

## 6. The discriminating test (the constructive part)

Seven filters. A layer-2 overbuild reliably produces a layer 3 only when it passes **all** of them; the six negative cases each fail at least two.

1. **Substrate, not product.** The residue must be application-agnostic. Fibre carries any bits; a GPU does any dense linear algebra; a container carries anything. A nuclear plant makes one product; 3G spectrum carries one operator's traffic; a house is already at its highest use. *Rule: if the asset has exactly one output, there is no optionality and no third order.*
2. **Positive salvage value and near-zero carry.** Buried fibre costs nothing to hold for a decade. Nuclear assets have *negative* salvage value — decommissioning liability travels with title, so no distressed buyer will take them. **This, not "cheap electricity failed," is why nuclear has no third order, and it is the sharpest discriminator in the set.** *Test: can a buyer with a different business model hold this asset for ten years for less than its option value?*
3. **Separable title plus a liquidating bankruptcy regime.** Value transfers only if the asset can be prised off the failed owner. 3G licences failed this — non-transferable legal claims re-auctioned to incumbents, pure rent extraction. Cogent's $14bn-for-$60m is the positive template.
4. **A scalar price crossing a threshold behind which sits a KNOWN, ALREADY-ARTICULATED application.** This is the most valuable inversion of the operator's own framing. **The third order is not unpredictable in kind.** Streaming video was demoed in the 1990s and known to be bandwidth-blocked. Neural networks were described in the 1980s and known to be compute-blocked. EVs existed in 1900 and were known to be battery-blocked. What is unpredictable is *who* and *when*, not *what*. Prospective use therefore is not "guess the novel thing" — it is: **maintain a register of applications that are technically demonstrated, economically rejected, and blocked on a single measurable scalar; then watch the scalar.** That is a doable discipline. Guessing third orders is not.
5. **Dispersed, transferable human capital.** The bubble must train a cohort in a portable skill and then fire them. Telecom engineers → cloud; canal engineers → rail. Mortgage originators and token marketers had bubble-specific skills; nothing transferred.
6. **No downstream chokepoint.** If the incumbent keeps the gate, the incumbent captures layer 3 and no new generation appears. AT&T divestiture is the clean case: forced open access at regulated rates was the enabling condition, not the overbuild per se.
7. **Demand-side independence from the bubble's own thesis.** Cloud did not need pets.com to be right. DeFi needs crypto prices to be right. *Rule: if layer 3's revenue is denominated in, or contingent on, layer 2's speculative asset, it is the same bubble wearing a hat.*

**Honest limit on the test:** these seven give you a **watchlist and a kill-list, not a trade**. Filters 1–3 are only observable *after* the bust, and filter 4's payoff requires being liquid precisely when everyone else is not. The framework's genuine, defensible use is *negative* — ruling out overbuilds that cannot possibly produce a third order (negative salvage, single-output, non-transferable title, thesis-dependent demand) — which would have correctly excluded 3G, subprime, nuclear and ICOs in advance. Ex-ante exclusion is a real and unglamorous product. Ex-ante selection is not on offer.

- Telegraph case: 'half of London's telegraph traffic by the 1850s was stock-market data' has no source and could not be verified. The '75+ companies by 1851' figure conflates US and British counts; Britain had a near-duopoly (Electric Telegraph Company and British and Irish Magnetic).
- ERISA case: 'index funds 40%+ of institutional assets by 2000s' is not supportable at that date; passive crossed roughly half of US equity fund assets only around 2019. Airline case: low-cost carriers reached about 25% of US domestic capacity around 2005, not 2000.

**Promote:**
- AT&T divestiture (1982-84) -> forced open access -> ISPs. The cleanest case in the set because the enabling condition is a legal act with an observable counterfactual: telecom markets that did not force separation had markedly slower ISP formation. It also correctly isolates that the operative variable is forced separation, not overbuild.
- Dot-com/telecom fibre -> cloud, CDN, streaming - but ONLY rebuilt around the verified distressed-transfer evidence (Cogent acquiring roughly $14bn book value for about $60m) plus the $/Mbps threshold, and with the vague 'AWS ran on dark fibre' claim deleted. The mechanism is title transfer at a 200:1 discount, which is specific, dated and checkable.
- Apollo and Minuteman IC procurement -> learning-curve cost collapse -> minicomputers and PCs (with figures corrected to ~60% of US production in 1963). Best documented example of guaranteed government offtake buying down a manufacturing learning curve, and the layer-3 applications were known and priced out in advance.
- US railroad overbuilding (1870s-90s) -> mail order and Chicago commodity markets. Documented in Cronon's 'Nature's Metropolis' and Chandler; the specific mechanism - overcapacity forcing marginal-cost freight pricing that Sears could exploit - is the operator's thesis in its most literal and best-evidenced form.
- GPU/CUDA -> deep learning (with revenue figures corrected). The strongest MODERN case, and the most instructive: neural networks were described in the 1980s and explicitly known to be compute-blocked. It demonstrates that the third order was known in kind and unknown only in timing - which is the operator's most usable prospective handle.
- The Black Death - promote specifically as the set's best natural experiment, and for the OPPOSITE reason the case file gives. Identical layer-1 shock and identical layer-2 labour scarcity produced the end of serfdom in Western Europe and the SECOND SERFDOM in Eastern Europe (the Brenner debate). Same shock, opposite third orders. This single case shows that institutions and bargaining power, not the shock or the overbuild, select layer 3 - and it should anchor the report's honest section.
- Factory electrification (1900-1930) - promote in David's and Devine's corrected form only: unit drive freed plant layout from line-shaft geometry, with a roughly 40-year productivity lag. Explicitly NOT as 'electricity caused the assembly line', which the operator's own contested case correctly rejects.
- US airline deregulation - promote with the Southwest correction, reframed: layer 2 supplied scale and cheap aircraft to a model already proven intrastate since 1971. This is a better case than the file makes it, because the corrected version separates 'invention of the model' from 'availability of distressed capacity', which is exactly the distinction the framework needs.

**Drop:**
- Bessemer Steel - has no second order at all. It is a pure input-cost curve with more downstream uses, which is just declining prices. Its inclusion shows the framework absorbing any cost-decline story, which is the strongest evidence for the unfalsifiability charge.
- Printing press -> Scientific Revolution - the entire mechanism rests on Eisenstein's typographical-fixity thesis, directly contested by Adrian Johns; the layer-2 'overcapacity relative to demand' is asserted with zero evidence; and a 350-year lag makes the case unfalsifiable in principle.
- Dutch fluyt -> VOC - mechanism is wrong (fluyts were not used on the armed Asia route) and the chronology is strained (VOC 1602 predates mass fluyt diffusion).
- Mechanical refrigeration / cold chain - no supporting evidence, generic mechanism, and the layer-3 items (industrial monoculture) are normative judgements rather than value-capture claims.
- Green Revolution - layer 2 is complementary input DEMAND, not overbuild; the mechanism runs backwards from the framework's own definition. Layer-3 items are contested normative claims about industrial agriculture.
- Chinese solar -> crypto/AI/hydrogen - the crypto link is factually false (Iceland, Kazakhstan and Sichuan power is geothermal, coal and hydro, not solar), and the AI and hydrogen links are prospective assertions, not evidence.
- COVID mRNA -> oncology pipeline - three factual mislabels, and more fundamentally it is a live PREDICTION being used as evidence for a framework whose disputed property is precisely its predictive validity. Circular.
- Mobile cellular buildout - the case concedes its own mechanism is weak (ongoing operator capex, not stranded overcapacity from a bust). Either drop it or reclassify it as a negative case, which is what it actually is.
- Containerization - the DUPLICATE entry must go. Two entries with different eras, mechanisms and evidence grades for the same event is itself a defect in the coding scheme and will be the first thing a hostile reader notices.
- Securitization -> derivatives complex - drop or merge with the subprime case. As written the two entries contradict each other, and grading a $2-3trn value destruction as a successful third order demonstrates that the framework has no value sign.
- Railway Mania -> British time standardization - chronologically inverted (Railway Time from 1840, before the Mania). Retain only the 1883 US time-zone adoption under the US railroad case.
- Crypto -> AI datacentres - retain only the narrow, verifiable claim (site, power-contract and shell reuse: CoreWeave, Core Scientific). Delete the GPU-supply and ASIC claims entirely.
- Eurodollar market - drop the Soviet Cold War origin narrative (contested by Schenk) and downgrade the remainder to weak; the layer-2 'overcapacity of offshore centres' is asserted, not shown.
- Bayh-Dole - the case concedes confounding with PCR and sequencing, and the econometric literature finds a smaller effect than the narrative. Demote to illustrative, not evidential.
- Interstate Highway System - the 'stranded rail assets freed capital' mechanism is asserted, and rail's decline was substantially a CONSEQUENCE of the highway subsidy. Layer 2 and layer 3 here are the same policy, so the case cannot support a causal chain.
- 'Hindsight Bias' meta-case - do not drop, but do not present it as one case among 41. It is the report's central finding and should be structural, not a row in a table. Presenting the critique as a case is itself a way of neutralising it.

## A. DISCIPLINES THAT WERE NEVER SEARCHED AT ALL

**A1. Transaction-cost economics / asset specificity.** Oliver Williamson — a Nobel laureate whose central concept, *asset specificity*, is precisely the variable that determines whether layer-2 wreckage can become layer-3 input — appears nowhere in 74 entries. Nor does the finance operationalisation of it (*asset redeployability*, Kim & Kung 2017), nor the macro version (*putty-clay vs putty-putty vintage capital*, Johansen/Solow).
→ *Question: Rank the 45 cases by an asset-redeployability measure (Williamson specificity, or Kim-Kung industry redeployability scores). Does redeployability, rather than "bubble-ness", separate the successes from nuclear/3G/subprime?*

**A2. The empirical economics of capital reallocation — the single largest hole.** The entire framework rests on stranded assets moving cheaply to new owners. There is a mature empirical literature on exactly this and none of it was cited: Shleifer & Vishny (1992) *Liquidation Values and Debt Capacity*; Pulvino (1998) on aircraft fire sales (which is literally the airline case); Ramey & Shapiro (2001, JPE) *Displaced Capital*, which measured aerospace plant liquidations and found equipment sold at roughly a quarter of replacement cost **with large real losses**; Eisfeldt & Rampini (2006), whose central finding is that **capital reallocation is procyclical — it is lowest in busts**, which cuts directly against the operator's mechanism.
→ *Question: Does Eisfeldt-Rampini procyclical reallocation falsify the claim that busts are when capacity changes hands productively? And what does Ramey-Shapiro's measured redeployment loss imply for how much of the fibre/rail/datacentre "residue" actually survived transfer?*

**A3. Bankruptcy and insolvency law as the enabling institution.** The verified Cogent datum ($14bn book for ~$60m) is a *Chapter 11 outcome*, not an economic law. Nobody asked whether the pattern is a US-legal-regime artefact. The obvious control is Japan post-1990: enormous infrastructure overbuild, banks forbore, assets never transferred, no third order. The zombie-lending literature (Caballero, Hoshi & Kashyap 2008; Banerjee & Hofmann at BIS) is absent.
→ *Question: Is "the third derivative" actually a function of liquidating bankruptcy regimes? Compare US telecom 2001-04 (assets transferred, layer 3 emerged) with Japanese real estate/construction 1991-2005 and European telecom post-3G (assets retained by incumbents, no layer 3).*

**A4. Economic geography and labour mobility.** No one asked *where* layer 3 appears. AnnaLee Saxenian's *Regional Advantage* (1994) — the definitive study of whether skills residue is portable, and why Route 128's collapse produced nothing while Silicon Valley's produced everything — is absent, as are Marshallian agglomeration, Jacobs externalities, and the inventor-mobility literature (Marx, Strumsky & Fleming on non-competes).
→ *Question: Do layer-3 firms cluster in the same labour market as the layer-2 wreckage? If yes, the mechanism is human capital, not physical capacity — and non-compete enforceability becomes a testable moderator.*

**A5. History of technology as a discipline (as opposed to economics of innovation).** Thomas P. Hughes' *Networks of Power* (1983) — the standard scholarly work on electrification, and the source of *technological momentum*, *reverse salient*, and *load factor* — is absent. So is David Hounshell's *From the American System to Mass Production, 1800–1932* (1984), which is **the** authority on where the assembly line came from and which would settle the operator's example (a) in one afternoon.
→ *Question: What do Hughes and Hounshell actually say about the electricity→shop-floor→assembly-line sequence, and does Hughes' "load factor" problem (Insull's need to fill off-peak capacity) reframe layer 2 as demand-creation rather than overcapacity?*

**A6. Industrial organisation: the shakeout literature.** Steven Klepper and Gort & Klepper built a formal, data-backed model of *entry wave → overcapacity → shakeout → surviving structure* across dozens of industries (autos, tyres, TV, penicillin). This is the closest thing in economics to a *measured base rate* for the operator's layer 2, and it appears nowhere.
→ *Question: Run the 45 cases against Klepper's shakeout model. In how many did a distinct new cohort capture post-shakeout value, versus the shakeout survivors (which is Klepper's finding, and the Capital Cycle's, and the opposite of the operator's claim)?*

**A7. Real options and the economics of capacity.** "Overcapacity is optionality" is asserted throughout and never formalised. Dixit & Pindyck (1994), and Baldwin & Clark's *Design Rules* (modularity as option value), are absent.
→ *Question: Can layer-2 overbuild be priced as a real option, and does that yield an ex-ante test — i.e. is the option value observable before the bust?*

**A8. Development economics: the big push and coordination failure.** Rosenstein-Rodan (1943), Murphy-Shleifer-Vishny (1989), Hirschman (1958, raised only by a verifier). This is the formal model for *why simultaneous over-investment is required at all*, and it predicts overbuild is efficient, not wasteful.
→ *Question: Is the operator's layer 2 a coordination-failure solution (efficient over-investment) rather than a malinvestment? These have opposite policy implications.*

**A9. Ecology / resilience theory.** Holling's *adaptive cycle* and *panarchy* (growth → conservation → **release** → **reorganisation**) is a four-phase model with exactly this structure, complete with the claim that the release phase is where accumulated capital becomes available. Entirely absent.
→ *Question: Does panarchy's "revolt and remember" cross-scale mechanism give the framework the stopping rule it lacks?*

**A10. Military procurement and war economy history.** Vernon Ruttan's *Is War Necessary for Economic Growth?* (2006), Mark Wilson's *Destructive Creation* (2016), Mazzucato's *Entrepreneurial State*. Nobody asked how many of the 45 layer-2 build-outs were **state-financed rather than bubble-financed**.
→ *Question: Classify all 45 cases by who paid for layer 2 — speculative private capital, public procurement, or regulated utility ratebase. If public money dominates the strong cases, the "bubble" framing is wrong and the correct concept is mission-oriented public infrastructure.*

**A11. Sociology of expectations / STS.** Borup, Brown, Konrad & van Lente on hype as a coordinating device; Goldfarb & Kirsch's *Bubbles and Crashes* (2019) was named by one verifier but never searched into.
→ *Question: Is the bubble's function narrative coordination (getting many actors to build compatible things simultaneously) rather than capital deployment?*

**A12. Formal small-N causal method.** Nobody proposed a method. Ragin's fuzzy-set QCA exists precisely for "which combination of conditions is necessary/sufficient across 40 cases".
→ *Question: Run fsQCA on the case library using the seven filters from verifier 3. Which conditions are necessary, which are sufficient in combination, and which are decorative?*

## Challenges researchers raised against the brief

- [
  "The operator's framing conflates two distinct second-order phenomena that economics literature treats separately: (1) Productive organizational reorganization required to exploit the GPT (documented by Paul David, Brynjolfsson), and (2) Speculative malinvestment and overcapacity (dot-com bubble, telecom fiber overbuilding). Both occur but operate via different mechanisms. The literature on complementary organizational capital and intangible investment does not frame reorganization as 'waste' or 'malinvestment'—it frames it as necessary, costly, and unmeasured. Bubble waste, by contrast, is documented as capital destruction that only sometimes yields later productive use. The operator's examples elide this distinction: factory reorganization (1920s) was arguably productive reorganization, while the dot-com bubble was malinvestment that *happened* to leave useful infrastructure. The contrast is real and should be flagged.",
  "The operator implies that 3rd-order value capture is due to 3rd-order actors exploiting 2nd-order 'overcapacity' left behind. Economics literature suggests a more precise mechanism: value accrues to whoever controls complementary assets and organizational capabilities required to exploit the platform (Teece framework on appropriability). The dot-com bubble overbuilt fiber optic capacity, but AWS/Cloudflare captured value not merely by using cheap fiber, but by building the organizational capabilities (co-specialized assets) to deliver scalable cloud infrastructure. The malinvestment is incidental; the complementary asset control is causal. The operator's framing undersells this.",
  "The operator states 3rd-order outcomes are 'frequently NOT predictable from 1st order.' Economics literature explains this more precisely: downstream demand is unpredictable due to coordination failures in complementary innovation (Bresnahan & Trajtenberg), and the specific organizational forms that will exploit the platform depend on market dynamics and asset availability, not technological trajectories. This is not inherent mystery; it's a consequence of decentralized innovation requiring simultaneous problem-solving across many firms.",
  "The lag timescales cited (20-50 years) are empirically correct but the literature offers multiple explanations with different time constants: organizational learning (David suggests 1-2 decades), measurement lag (Brynjolfsson's J-curve is 5-15 years of intangible accumulation), and diffusion to laggards (ongoing). These stack, not replace each other. The '20-50 year' frame risks conflating phases."
]
- 
1. **Primary innovation vs. the full paradigm shift.** The operator calls the primary innovation (electricity, internet) the "1st order" and treats reorganization/overcapacity as secondary (2nd order). Perez's framework does not separate these: the 1st and 2nd order are INTEGRATED into the installation phase of a single paradigm shift. Electricity was not just the invention; it included the adoption of new factory layouts, electrified machine tools, and new labor organization. These were not aftereffects—they were part of the paradigm. The operator risks treating them as sequential when they are simultaneous.

2. **Causality of overcapacity.** The operator frames the 2nd order as a "forced reorganisation" or "builds an overcapacity." Perez is more precise: the overcapacity is deliberately funded by financial capital (not accidentally created); the reorganization is driven by the need to use new infrastructure profitably. The bubble is not a side-effect—it is the financing mechanism for infrastructure that would otherwise be too capital-intensive for production capital to justify rationally at that early stage. This is a feature, not a bug.

3. **Time lag explanation.** The operator notes a 20–50 year lag but treats it as "mysterious" (the operator says the 3rd order is "frequently NOT predictable"). Perez explains the lag mechanistically: financial capital floods in immediately (installation phase); production capital waits (because ROI is genuinely uncertain); after the turning-point crash (20–30 years in), expectations reset and infrastructure use becomes obvious, so production capital deploys. This is predictable, not random. The operator may be conflating "hard to predict the specific winning firm/technology" with "unpredictable timing of value accrual," which are different things.

4. **Who captures value.** The operator correctly identifies that "durable value accrues to DIFFERENT people." Perez names these: installation-phase winners (venture capitalists, infrastructure builders, early movers like railroads companies or dot-com VCs) vs. deployment-phase winners (application-layer firms like AWS, automotive suburbia retailers, mass-production industrialists). The operator should note that this **redistribution of rents** (not just creation of new value) is a central feature. Financial capital loses; production capital and society gain (especially in a well-managed golden age).

5. **The frame "third derivative."** The operator uses calculus-like language (1st, 2nd, 3rd order) which is intuitive but potentially misleading because it suggests a linear, additive process. Perez's framework is **cyclical and overlapping**: the maturity of one paradigm gestates the irruption of the next; the 2nd-order infrastructure of one era becomes the 1st-order substrate of the next. This is not 1+2+3 but waves of overlapping cycles. AWS (3rd order of the internet wave) is also the 1st-order infrastructure enabling the AI wave's installation phase now.

6. **Institutional change as co-driver, not aftereffect.** The operator treats reorganization as a consequence of the primary innovation. Perez (following Freeman) treats institutional change as co-constitutive: a new paradigm cannot deploy without complementary institutional change—rules, regulations, labor training, corporate forms, financial instruments. These are not secondary—they are prerequisites for productivity. This matters for the operator's point about unpredictability: if you do not anticipate institutional bottlenecks, you will indeed find the 3rd order surprising.

- The brief conflates two distinct phenomena worth separating: (1) necessary production *reorganization* and (2) genuine *malinvestment* and stranded overcapacity. Austrian theory (Mises, Hayek) argues the problem is not overinvestment in aggregate but *misdirection* of capital into unsustainable sectors due to credit-induced interest rate distortion. This is different from saying "overcapacity in general is the second-order effect." The operator's examples actually show infrastructure malinvestment (railways, fiber) that leaves behind usable physical capital, not pure financial distortion or reorganization cost. Also, the brief's claim that value accrues to "different people" is correct but undersells the mechanism: the capital cycle ensures *existing participants lose money* while new entrants with no sunk costs in Phase 2 capture Phase 3 value. This isn't coincidence—it's structural.
- The operator's framing of "the third derivative" as a unitary concept actually conflates several related but distinct phenomena that should be disaggregated:

1. **Infrastructure accumulation vs. value migration**: Perez and Janeway focus on how bubbles build productive capacity that later waves exploit. But Christensen's value migration theory describes a different mechanism—value shifts between layers not because of infrastructure but because of architectural changes in modularity and competitive advantage. These are related but distinct.

2. **Malinvestment causation**: The operator assumes layer 2 overcapacity is "wasteful" or a "bubble," but some infrastructure investment may be rational even if initially underutilized—network effects, standardization benefits, and complementarity can justify early overbuilding. Perez and Arthur show this is often *productive waste* rather than pure malinvestment.

3. **Primary innovation capture**: The concept assumes layer 1 innovators capture less value directly, but this varies widely. Electricity generators and internet backbone providers did capture significant value; it's more accurate to say value *distribution* changes across layers rather than total value being captured later.

4. **Lag timing**: The operator states 20-50 years, but this is highly variable. Dark fiber deployed in 1999 was productively re-used by 2005-2010 (5-10 years). Assembly line production followed factory electrification within 10-20 years. The lag reflects how quickly complementary innovations and organizational learning accumulate, not a fixed property.

5. **Beneficiary assumption**: It's not always true that "different people" capture value at each layer. Amazon innovated in logistics (layer 1) then built AWS infrastructure (layer 2) then captured value from layer 3 applications. Vertical integration is common. The concept better describes *sectoral reorganization* than personal wealth transfer.

**Strongest matches** are Perez (Installation/Deployment framework), Janeway (productive bubbles), and the dark fiber metaphor—these directly name the phenomenon. **Conceptually related but distinct** are disruptive innovation (market displacement without infrastructure focus) and value migration (value shifts but not infrastructure mechanism). The concept is real and economically important, but it's not as unitary as the framing suggests.

- The concept under study appears to be what economic historians call the "cascade model" or "three-stage shock model" — innovations that force organizational/infrastructural overbuilding, which then enables structurally different (and more valuable) later businesses. This is more common than commonly recognized; the lag between stages 2-3 is often 20-50+ years, making causation historically invisible. Stage 2 typically looks wasteful in real-time but is the prerequisite for stage 3.
