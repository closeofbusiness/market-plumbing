Where the Money Isn't

We set out to find the hidden money creation behind a $400bn-a-year capital-spending boom. We did not find it. What we found instead is how the nonbank–bank nexus has re-plumbed itself around Basel III — and why the cash that feeds it answers to the level of rates, not to the supply of safe assets.

The question, and what it became

The opening question was the intuitive one: American companies are spending several hundred billion dollars a year on data centres while the household saving rate falls — so where is the money coming from? If nobody is saving more and nobody is selling other assets, something, somewhere, must be creating purchasing power.

That question was badly posed, and an external reviewer said so within minutes. In the Kalecki identity, falling household saving is one of the terms that produces corporate profits; US non-financial corporates have funded their capex entirely from internal funds, of which roughly 72 per cent is depreciation; the Financial Accounts' financing gap has been negative for four straight quarters; and gross private saving rose by more than a trillion dollars between 2019 and 2025. There was never an aggregate hole to fill.

What remained was the better question underneath it. Zoltan Pozsar and Manmohan Singh argued in 2011 that banks are funded not only by household deposits but by asset managers, who sit on both sides of a single mechanism: they are the dominant demanders of non-M2 money — repo, bills, money-fund shares — and they are the "mines" from which dealers extract collateral through securities lending and prime brokerage. Their line is the one we have been testing: that collateral does work analogous to base money, and that a banking system can lever up with M2 perfectly stable, on the portfolio choices of the asset-management complex alone.

So the project became an attempt to understand that nexus as it runs in 2026 — and to use the AI capex cycle, which is where the original worry lived, as the worked case.

How we worked

Adversarially. Every load-bearing number was re-pulled from the publisher that issues it — the Financial Accounts, the OFR's money-fund monitor, the FDIC's bank-level API, SEC filings parsed directly, Treasury's debt tables — and every claim that failed a check was written into a register with the correct position beside it. Fifty-two entries so far, including several of our own and several imported from large language models we used as scouts. On that last point, one finding about method that an economist may find useful: asked to locate a series or a publisher, these models are good; asked to supply a series identifier, a page reference or a post-2020 citation, roughly half of what they return is constructed. We now ask them only where to look.

What we found

$1.12trnleases signed, not yet commenced · six filers · mid-2026

The off-balance-sheet capex is a lease stack, not a guarantee

The ghost figures circulating for hyperscaler lease commitments — $662bn, $970bn — turn out to have a mechanical cause: the commitments are disclosed in the notes by all six large filers and tagged in machine-readable form by none, so every aggregate in the press is somebody's hand-sum. Ours comes to $1.12trn. The contingent-guarantee layer alongside it is far smaller, $86bn live and $228bn contracted, and the price of that support is visible only where the beneficiary discloses it: one Bitcoin-miner-turned-data-centre developer paid $545m of warrants for a backstop worth roughly $87m of interest saving. What is being bought is not basis points but the existence of the financing.

+$2.5trnTreasury bills outstanding, Jun 2023 → Jul 2026

Bill supply did not shrink private money. It drained the Fed.

Pozsar's 2011 policy prescription was to manage bill supply as a macroprudential tool: more bills, less private shadow money. The US has since run that experiment at scale. Over the same window the Fed's overnight reverse-repo facility fell by $2.0trn to effectively nothing, and money funds' Treasury holdings rose by $2.2trn — the cash-pool vehicle rotated almost one-for-one out of the Fed and into bills. Every private money-like claim we can measure rose: uninsured deposits by a trillion, large time deposits by half a trillion, asset-backed commercial paper by a fifth of one. Over 2013–25 bills rose $5.0trn and the private stack rose $5.0trn. And in 2026 the Fed has re-entered as a buyer: its bill holdings are up $304bn this year, roughly 69 per cent of net issuance. The lever was pulled hard and hit the central bank's own balance sheet.

0.13%share of US corporate bonds held by money-market funds · 2026Q1

The AI paper is held by long money

Money funds hold $22bn of a $17.2trn corporate-bond stock. A filing-by-filing census of every money fund that names an AI issuer finds a quarter of a billion of commercial paper and about $4bn of repo collateral — one can see CoreWeave's 2032 convertible pledged inside a BlackRock money fund's overnight repo, which is the mechanism exactly, and it is de minimis. The bonds sit with the rest of the world, life insurers, mutual funds and pensions. Meta's $27bn Hyperion financing, the largest off-balance-sheet deal of the cycle, shows up in 324 registered-fund filings holding $9.8bn — a little over a third of the deal, $6.2bn of it in PIMCO's own mutual funds at one to two per cent of net assets; the other two-thirds sit in insurers' separate accounts and private vehicles. The one place the original intuition is literally true is bank credit — about $150bn outstanding against $450bn committed to AI-adjacent borrowers, plus project loans such as the $38bn Oracle–Vantage package in their months on bank balance sheets. Real, growing, a minority.

$5.7trnwhat money-fund assets "should" be on the 2013–21 ratio · actual $8.3trn

Cash pools scale with the asset-management complex — until 2021

From 2013 to 2021 the complex — mutual funds, ETFs, pensions, insurers — grew 66 per cent and money funds 71 per cent, institutional ones 80 per cent; corporate liquid assets have held at 22–24 per cent of corporate financial assets in every year since 2013. Pozsar's "reverse maturity transformation" is the right account of the level. It is not the account of what happened next: from 2021 the complex grew 16 per cent and money funds 59 per cent, retail ones 122 per cent. Households moved deposits to 5 per cent money funds; institutions moved $1.3trn above the structural norm across precisely the window in which uninsured deposits fell from $8.8trn to $7.2trn. That is one money form replacing another. The level of rates explains the surge, twice, from independent data.

+$741bnmoney-fund repo cleared at FICC, Jun 2023 → Jul 2026

The plumbing has re-routed around the dealer

When the cash left the Fed it did not only buy bills. Money funds' Treasury repo with non-Fed counterparties rose by $1.1trn, of which $741bn through FICC's sponsored service — cash pools funding the dealer–hedge-fund complex through a cleared conduit, with the dealer intermediating but not carrying. On the equity side, non-bank-sponsored asset-backed commercial paper conduits are now funding prime-brokerage margin loans: ABCP is up $67bn this year on the Fed's count and more on DTCC's, and the cost of financing equity longs has hit records while Treasury repo stays calm. Two conduits, one logic: Pozsar and Singh's "collateral mining via prime brokerage," rebuilt to step around the G-SIB surcharge. It is also the one channel two external review passes failed to name, and a newspaper did.

What it adds up to

There is no hidden money creation behind the AI build-out, with one bounded exception: bank loans to AI-adjacent borrowers, around $150bn outstanding, which do create deposits in the textbook sense and which are a minority of roughly $1.2trn of AI-company debt. The bonds, the SPV notes and the lease commitments are funded by long money — insurers, pensions, bond funds, foreigners — and the daily-redeemable share of even the marquee deal is about a third: material, but a minority, and held at one to two per cent of net assets across 146 fund complexes. The fragility on that side, if there is one, is duration and operating leverage: flexible cost structures turned into bond-like overheads by $1.12trn of commitments that have not yet started.

The nexus itself is very much alive, but it no longer runs through dealer balance sheets the way Pozsar and Singh drew it. It runs through conduits — sponsored repo for Treasury collateral, alternative ABCP for equity collateral — that let cash pools fund levered accounts while the dealer intermediates without carrying. The growth is measurable and recent.

And the variable that moves the whole system is the policy rate, not the supply of safe assets. Pozsar's lever, pulled at scale, emptied the Fed's own facility and left private money untouched; asset-management growth explains the level of cash pools but not their surge; the surge is households and institutions following 5 per cent yields out of deposits. Nagel's account of the near-money premium — it follows the opportunity cost of holding money — fits both results, and the Fed's return as a bill buyer this year means the central bank is now the marginal cash pool as well as the marginal lender.

One inversion is worth dwelling on. The firms doing the capex are themselves the largest corporate cash pools in the world — about $630bn of cash and short-term investments at mid-2026, up $240bn in two years, Alphabet alone at $243bn. They are on both legs of the nexus at once: the issuers of the long paper that insurers buy, and the largest demanders of the short safe assets the bill surge supplies. That is, by definition, what Pozsar and Singh were describing.

What we do not know

  • A prediction with a number. If the surge in cash pools is rate-driven, a material cut should pull money-fund assets toward $5.7–6trn. We have pre-registered it. If assets hold at $8trn through a cutting cycle, the structural account is wrong and something else is going on.
  • The size of the conduit channel. We can see it in money-fund filings and in the CP data; we cannot yet see it whole. US securities-lending data under the SEC's new rule does not go public until March 2027, and the primary-dealer repo series by collateral type stopped being carried in 2021.
  • Who holds the other two-thirds. A full census of the SEC's bulk fund filings puts registered funds at $9.8bn of the $27bn Hyperion notes. The rest is in insurers' separate accounts and private vehicles, which are not public in aggregate. The same is true for every SPV in the cycle.
  • Whether the capex firms hold each other's paper. Alphabet's $187bn of marketable securities includes corporate bonds. If the AI complex's cash pools hold the AI complex's bonds, "who funds whom" has a reflexive answer.
  • The measurement structure. Our channel map is nine sector silos, and the literature is clear that sector aggregates cannot separate credit creation from intermediation layering. The answer is a from-whom-to-whom matrix. Nobody has built one for the non-bank system; the G20 data-gaps programme names its absence as unresolved.

Three questions for a central-bank economist

  1. The euro-area mirror. The ECB's MMSR and the securities-holdings statistics by sector see the money-fund and repo side of this at transaction level. Does the euro area show the same conduit growth — cash pools funding levered accounts around dealer balance sheets — or is sponsored clearing a US artefact?
  2. The buffer. In the US the ON RRP absorbed the bill surge and then vanished. In the euro area the deposit facility and the excess-liquidity stock have played that role for longer. When Bubill and T-bill supply moved, did private money-like claims in Europe move the way they did in the US — which is to say, not at all?
  3. The frame itself. Pozsar and Singh's claim was that collateral matters as much as base money. When the central bank has become both the marginal buyer of bills and, through its facilities, the marginal cash pool, is that still the right frame — or has base money quietly reclaimed the ground?
