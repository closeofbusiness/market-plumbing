# Third Derivative — Case Library

Cases scored against the discriminating test in `Third_Derivative_Concept_Map.md` §5.
Round 1 produced 45 cases that three adversarial verifiers found riddled with duplicated layers,
arbitrary layer assignment and factual errors; those are retained only as raw evidence in
`_research/`. **This library is the round-2 corpus**, gathered under the requirement that every
case name a specific transmission mechanism — which asset, what price change, who owned it before
and after.

**Read the contradicting and null cases first.** They carry more information than the confirming
ones, and they are the reason the framework's domain is as narrow as §5 says it is.

## Supports the framework

### Brazil ethanol: Government industrial policy and the role of prior sugar capacity

*1930-1989+* · evidence **medium**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Sugarcane production in Brazil (established centuries prior, especially 1960s-1970s expansion). Sugar was major commodity export. Land, agricultural expertise, mills existed. |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | Proálcool mandate (November 14, 1975, via Decree 76,593): Government required ethanol blending in gasoline (response to 1973 OPEC oil shock and Brazil's oil import dependency). Massive government investment in distilleries, agronomic research, engine R&D (1975-1989, ~$10B+ cumulative in 1975 dollars). By mid-1980s, ~400,000 hectares under ethanol sugarcane, capacity built from ~1.4B liters/year (1975) to ~11B liters/year (1989). This was state-directed industrial policy, not speculative bubble. However: in 1980s, oil prices fell; Proálcool became uneconomical and was scaled back (1989-1990). Substantial overcapacity built, then idled. This resembles an L2 'productive bubble' or malinvestment. |
| **L3 — third order** | Modern ethanol companies (1990s+): Cosan (founded 1997), Raízen (formed 2011 via merger), Vale Fertilizantes, and ~400 independent mills. Leveraged idle Proálcool-era distillery capacity and agricultural knowledge.; By 2024: Brazil produces ~30B liters/year ethanol; world's 2nd largest producer (after USA), largest exporter. Ethanol cost: ~$0.30-0.40/liter (world's cheapest). Sugar-ethanol industry employs ~1M people. |
| **Transmission mechanism** | ASSETS: Proálcool-era distilleries, agricultural research infrastructure, trained workforce, 400k hectares of productive sugarcane land. PRICE CHANGE: Distillery capacity built for ~$300-500M (1975-1989, per unit) became stranded 1986-1990 as oil prices fell; resale/leasing values dropped ~50% by 1992-1995. OWNERSHIP: Initially state-owned or state-subsidized; by late 1980s, some mills were privatized or leased to private operators. In 1990s-2000s, private companies (Cosan, others) acquired or leased distilleries at steep discounts (40-60% of replacement cost) and expanded into integrated sugar-ethanol production. |
| **Lag** | Proálcool start (1975) to peak overexpansion (1989) to consolidation wave (1992-2000) to modern scale-up (2000+). Peak-capex 1989 to modern company founding ~1997 (Cosan) = ~8 years. This is consistent with L2 → L3 lag. However, the causation is ambiguous: were modern companies enabled by cheap distillery assets, or would they have entered anyway (given rising oil prices post-1999)? Evidence quality medium because oil-price confound. |
| **Who captured the value** | Modern ethanol companies (Cosan, Raizen, independents) captured margin value by acquiring low-cost distillery assets post-1990. State lost via write-downs and subsidy cessation. Farmers benefited from sustained demand. Exported economy benefited. End consumers (fuel buyers) benefited from low-cost ethanol. |

### German Synthetic Dyes to Pharmaceuticals and Haber-Bosch

*1856-1914* · evidence **strong**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Perkin's aniline dye synthesis (1856) enables industrial synthetic dye production, displacing natural dyes |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | BASF (founded 1865, major lab investment 1870s-1890s), Bayer (founded 1863, Elberfeld labs), Hoechst (founded 1863) systematize research as institutional innovation. Cost per unit synthetic dye falls 10-100x; German firms capture >90% global dye production by 1880s. |
| **L3 — third order** | Aspirin: Bayer synthesizes acetylsalicylic acid and patents aspirin (1899) using dye-lab research capability; Haber-Bosch nitrogen fixation: BASF with Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch develop ammonia synthesis (1909) for fertilizers and explosives, using same systematized research infrastructure; Pharmaceutical dominance: German chemical firms dominate global pharma, fertilizers, and explosives through 1930s |
| **Transmission mechanism** | Asset: Institutional research laboratory capability (PhD scientists, experimental methods, patent generation system). Price change: Cost of dye production fell 10-100x (1870s-1890s); German firms captured 90%+ global production and premium patent position. Ownership before: Scattered academic chemistry. Ownership after: Concentrated in BASF, Bayer, Hoechst labs (no asset transfer between firms; same firms redeployed lab capacity to pharmaceuticals and nitrogen fixation). Critical: The research lab was an overbuild initially (capacity exceeded dye market demand by 1890s) but proved redeployable to adjacent high-value domains. |
| **Discount on transfer** | No asset transfer; same firms captured both L2 and L3 value. No discount applicable. |
| **Lag** | 1899 (Aspirin patent) - 1880s (peak BASF/Bayer lab investment) = ~20 years; 1909 (Haber-Bosch patent) - 1880s = ~25-30 years |
| **Who captured the value** | BASF, Bayer, Hoechst shareholders. BASF alone: ~5M Marks revenue (1865) to ~100M Marks (1914); pharmaceutical and nitrogen-fixation business dominated corporate value by 1914. |

<sub>Sources: Homburg, Ernst. The Transformation of the German Chemical Industry by World War I (2004); Murmann, Johann Peter. Knowledge and Competitive Advantage (2003); Willcocks, J.C. The Manufacture of Organic Chemicals (1921)</sub>

### Iridium Satellite Constellation Bankruptcy → Restart

*1999-2005* · evidence **strong**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Commercial satellite communication technology / LEO constellations |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | Iridium Corp (1990s build-out $5-6 billion; 1999 bankruptcy; 50,000 subscribers; service terminated 2000) |
| **L3 — third order** | Iridium Satellite LLC (Colussy-led rescue 2000-2001); Boeing operations contract $3.5M/month vs Motorola $45M/month; Profitable by 2005; 19% EBITDA growth 2010; free cash flow $135M by 2010 |
| **Transmission mechanism** | Physical satellite constellation (asset) purchased from bankruptcy at $25M (0.4% of $5-6B build cost). Boeing operating contract @ $3.5M/month replaced Motorola @ $45M/month (92% cost reduction). New management (Colussy, former airline executive) shifted from consumer to government/industrial customers. Debt relief under Chapter 11 eliminated $5B in interest payments (~40% cost reduction). These cost cascades enabled profitable operations on same physical asset that destroyed original investors. |
| **Discount on transfer** | 0.4% to 0.5% of build cost ($25M / $5-6B) |
| **Lag** | L2 peak investment ~1997-1999; bankruptcy Aug 1999; asset sale March 2001; service relaunch March 2001; profitability by 2005 = 2-6 year L2-L3 lag (fast for infrastructure) |
| **Who captured the value** | Iridium Satellite LLC shareholders and management (Colussy et al.); Boeing received operations contract; strategic shift to government contracts created new revenue model |

### Post-WWI Aircraft Surplus → Barnstorming → Airmail Contracts → Major Carriers

*1918-1934* · evidence **strong**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Military aircraft production (WWI) |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | Aircraft surplus and barnstorming era (L2): 6,000+ Jennys produced; post-1918 surplus sale $50-100 per aircraft; Curtiss acquired $20M worth (Jennys+engines) for 13 cents on dollar; thousands of surplus pilots; barnstorming 1920-1925 popularized aviation, created trained pilot base and aircraft-operations culture |
| **L3 — third order** | Kelly Act (Contract Air Mail Act) Feb 2 1925 authorized private airmail contracts; First 5 CAM routes Nov 7 1925: (1) Colonial Air Transport CAM-1 (Boston-New York) → Eastern Airlines, (2) Robertson Aircraft CAM-2 (Chicago-St. Louis, Lindbergh) → American Airways (1930, consolidated 80+ carriers) → American Airlines, (3) National Air Transport CAM-3 (Chicago-Dallas) → United Airlines (via consolidation 1930s), (4) Western Air Express CAM-4 (Salt Lake City-Los Angeles), (5) Walter T. Varney CAM-5 (Elko-Pasco); Airmail revenue: 95% of early airline revenues; carriers received 80% of mail revenue (~$0.80/pound rate); By 1927: 2.5M miles flown, 22M letters delivered; By 1934: American Airlines assembled 80+ airmail carriers into single entity through acquisitions |
| **Transmission mechanism** | L1 (military aircraft)→L2 (surplus+barnstorming): 6,000 Jennys sold post-WWI at 13 cents on dollar; thousands of pilots; barnstorming normalized aviation 1920-1925. L2→L3 (airmail contracts): Kelly Act (1925) enabled Post Office to contract private carriers. Airmail contracts provided 95% of early revenues, enabling Robertson/Colonial/NAT/etc. to build infrastructure, routes, and brand. These airmail operators then consolidated (Robertson+others→American Airways 1930, listed as American Airlines 1934 with 80+ merged carriers; NAT→United; Colonial→Eastern via Pitcairn Aviation). Transmission: surplus military aircraft (effectively free/cheap) + trained pilots enabled low-cost L2 barnstorming. Government mail subsidy (80% revenue) then enabled L3 carriers to build passenger networks from airmail base. By 1934, barnstorming-era knowledge and airmail-route infrastructure were embedded in American/United/Eastern as major carriers with passenger service. |
| **Discount on transfer** | Jennys post-WWI: ~$50-100 vs peacetime production ~$500-1000 = ~90% discount; pilot surplus: no wage cost (market compensation fell from military to civilian rates); government airmail subsidy effectively subsidized L3 at 95% of revenue (80% of ~$0.80/pound) |
| **Lag** | WWI ends Nov 1918, surplus aircraft available 1918-1920, peak barnstorming 1920-1925, Kelly Act Feb 1925, first airmail contracts Nov 1925, consolidations 1930-1934 = 12-16 year L2-L3 lag (ecosystem consolidation, not single-asset handoff) |
| **Who captured the value** | Robertson Aircraft shareholders → American Airways/American Airlines; National Air Transport → United; Colonial Air Transport → Eastern; government airmail subsidy transferred surplus military assets (aircraft + pilot knowledge) into commercial airline equity |

<sub>Sources: https://www.history.com/articles/us-aviation-airmail-passenger-flight; https://airandspace.si.edu/explore/stories/air-mail; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robertson_Aircraft_Corporation; https://postalmuseum.si.edu/exhibition/airmail-in-america/building-the-commercial-aviation-network</sub>

### Route 128 vs Silicon Valley: Labor Mobility as Differentiator

*1950-2000* · evidence **strong**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Semiconductor and integrated circuit design (Bell Labs/Shockley/fairchildren, 1947-1957) |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | Minicomputer specialization (Route 128: DEC established 1957, grew to 100k+ employees; closed architecture, proprietary designs, lifetime employment). Silicon Valley: Horizontal specialization (Intel, Fairchild, modular designs, high job mobility, VC formation 1958+). Both regions had minicomputer L2 overbuild by 1970s-1980s. |
| **L3 — third order** | Silicon Valley: YES. PCs/workstations (Apple 1976, Sun 1982) emerged using spinoff engineers + VC capital. Fairchild → Intel, AMD, LSI Logic, etc. recombination enabled IBM-compatible PC ecosystem.; Route 128: NO. DEC/Data General failed to pivot to PCs; minicomputer decline in 1980s; cluster dissolved. IBM (outsider, not DEC-derivative) captured PC market. |
| **Transmission mechanism** | Silicon Valley: Low-cost Fairchild/Intel engineer talent (spinoffs, founders could retain equity) + Draper's 1958 VC fund → PC/workstation startups acquired/imitated minicomputer reliability at 10x lower cost. Engineers' skills transferred to assemblers (Apple, Compaq) at <50% wages, recombined with new supply chains. Route 128: DEC engineers locked in closed org (lifetime employment norm, no equity spinoff rights); minicomputer proprietary designs (COBOL, PDP-11, VMS) not transferable; no VC exit paths. PCs/workstations developed OUTSIDE Route 128 (IBM, Intel in SV). Asset/knowledge: minicomputer engineering expertise was non-transferable under DEC's employment model; spinoff generation never happened. |
| **Discount on transfer** | SV talent redeployment: engineers at 50-70% of Fairchild/DEC wages, but with startup equity compensation. Apple/Compaq/Sun wafer fabs and designs acquired/imitated at <50% of R&D cost (learning curve, shared open IP). Route 128: DEC's Compaq acquisition (1998) at steep discount; DEC liquidated, not recombined. |
| **Lag** | SV: Fairchild peak capex/minicomputer boom ~1970; Apple founded 1976, Sun founded 1982 = 6-12 year lag. Route 128: DEC peak ~1970s, decline began early 1980s, collapse by 1990s = no L3, 20-year forbearance before liquidation (DEC acquired by Compaq 1998 at distressed prices). |
| **Who captured the value** | Silicon Valley: Founders (Jobs, Wozniak, McNealy), early investors (Draper, Gaither), 2nd/3rd-gen entrepreneurs (Ellison @ Oracle). Route 128: IBM (PC market), later Intel/AMD (processors) — but NOT Route 128 incumbents (DEC shareholders captured declining value; employees captured wages but not equity upside). |

<sub>Sources: AnnaLee Saxenian, Regional Advantage (1994), Oxford University Press. Definitive comparison of cluster trajectories.; Fairchild Semiconductor Spinoff Genealogy: multiple academic studies (e.g., Helms, 2008) documenting 20+ major spinoffs; DEC corporate history: Edgar Schein (1997), organizational culture analysis; DEC's later decline well-documented in business press</sub>

### The 2000 Genomics Bubble

*1998-2007* · evidence **strong**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | DNA sequencing technology: Celera's shotgun sequencing method and publicly-funded Human Genome Project established the technical capability |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | 2000-2001 genomics bubble: Celera Genomics (founded May 1998, $300M investment) peaked at $14B market cap in early 2000, crashed to $37 by mid-2001. Incyte collapsed when government-released free genomic data destroyed the proprietary data business model (2000). Human Genome Sciences failed. Peak capex: 2000-2001. |
| **L3 — third order** | Illumina: founded April 1998, IPO July 2000 ($100M), acquired Solexa 2007 ($650M), dominated sequencing market by 2010s; 23andMe: founded April 2006, launched consumer service late 2007 ($999), became dominant direct-to-consumer genomics company; CRISPR Therapeutics: founded October 2013, IPO 2014, led gene-editing therapeutics market; Editas Medicine: founded November 2013, IPO 2015, gene-editing therapeutics pioneer |
| **Transmission mechanism** | Solexa Technologies developed sequencing-by-synthesis (SBS) technology at Cambridge circa 2000-2005. Solexa went public via reverse merger with Lynx Therapeutics (March 2005, NASDAQ). Illumina—struggling as a microarray company since its own 2000 IPO—acquired Solexa in early 2007 for $650M. This $650M price reflected normalization post-bubble; the same technology would have commanded much higher prices during the 2000 peak, but Solexa's owners had already pivoted to platform development during the crash. SBS technology became the foundation for Illumina's dominance and enabled the 23andMe/CRISPR ecosystem that followed. |
| **Discount on transfer** | Solexa acquisition: $650M in 2007 for technology worth ~$2-3B in 2000 bubble pricing; discount of ~70% from bubble valuations. Solyndra facility: $90.2M sale vs $300M construction cost (70% loss, but different asset class). |
| **Lag** | Largest L3 founder: Illumina (IPO 2000 but Solexa acquisition inflection 2007). Peak L2 capex: 2000-2001. Using 2007 (Solexa/Illumina inflection) minus 2001 (peak R&D): 6 years. Alternatively, 23andMe founded 2006, launched 2007: 2007 minus 2001 = 6 years. |
| **Who captured the value** | Illumina shareholders, 23andMe shareholders, CRISPR Therapeutics and Editas shareholders. Bubble-era founders (Celera, Venter, Incyte) did not capture L3 value. Solexa's creators (Balasubramanian, Klenerman, Cambridge University) captured value indirectly through Illumina. |

### The ASP Wave (Application Service Provider)

*1998-2005* · evidence **strong**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Internet infrastructure and broadband connectivity: enabled application hosting over public networks |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | 1998-2001 ASP bubble: ASP Industry Consortium formed May 1999 (25 founding companies: IBM, Cisco, AT&T, Sun, Compaq, GTE, UUNET). Global ASP market $1B (1999) expanded to $11.3B (2001). Dataquest predicted $22B by 2003 (never materialized). Dot-com bust 2000-2002 destroyed funding. Failed companies: Pandesic (SAP-Intel JV, closed July 2000), HotOffice.com (closed December 2000), Red Gorilla (closed October 2000), Agillion (closed March 2001), FutureLink (filed Chapter 11 August 2001), Breakaway Solutions (filed Chapter 11 September 2001). Peak capex: 1999-2001. |
| **L3 — third order** | Salesforce.com: founded February 1999 (during ASP boom), IPO March 2004, dominated CRM and SaaS market 2010s+; SaaS market: early SaaS pioneers generated $4.2B collectively by 2005, expanded to 100s of billions by 2020s; Enterprise SaaS companies: Workday (founded 2005, IPO 2012), Box (founded 2005), ServiceNow (founded 2006), etc. |
| **Transmission mechanism** | Indirect transmission via architecture learning and infrastructure maturation, not asset acquisition. ASPs failed due to single-tenant architecture requiring $12k-$18k operational cost per customer annually. Salesforce, founded during ASP bubble (Feb 1999) but built multi-tenant architecture costing under $2k per customer (85-93% cost reduction). Internet speeds improved from 200 Kbps (2000) to 21 Mbps (2010s), enabling cloud delivery. Virtualization technology matured post-2001, enabling multi-tenant scalability. ASP bubble built broadband infrastructure and established enterprise appetite for hosted software; subsequent failures validated that single-tenant architecture was economically unviable. Salesforce IPO (March 2004) occurred just as these conditions aligned, capturing the SaaS market that ASPs could not. |
| **Discount on transfer** | No direct asset acquisition. ASP infrastructure (servers, data centers, networks) was neither liquidated as a separate asset class nor transferred intact. Internet infrastructure was absorbed into the broader cloud computing stack (AWS founded 2006, Azure 2008). The 'discount' was implicit: ASP investors lost capital; Salesforce benefited from matured infrastructure and customer willingness to adopt hosted software without having to rebuild it. |
| **Lag** | Peak L2 capex: 2000-2001 (ASP market peaked at $11.3B in 2001). Salesforce founded 1999 (during boom), IPO March 2004. L3 inflection: Salesforce IPO 2004, broader SaaS market dominance 2010s. Using IPO as L3 inflection: 2004 - 2001 = 3 years. Using 2005 as SaaS market takeoff (post-ASP collapse): 2005 - 2001 = 4 years. |
| **Who captured the value** | Salesforce shareholders, subsequent SaaS company shareholders (Workday, Box, ServiceNow, etc.). ASP founders did not transition to SaaS dominance. |

### Unit 8200 to Israeli Cybersecurity Industry

*1952-2020s* · evidence **strong**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Unit 8200 formed (1952) within Israeli Defense Force as signals intelligence unit. Recruits elite mathematical and engineering talent via mandatory military service. Develops deep expertise in cryptography, code-breaking, network reconnaissance. |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | Continuous operation and capability development (no collapse or overbuild event). Demobilization: soldiers complete 3-4 year service and exit to civilian tech sector. Repeated talent drain creates consistent supply of ex-8200 founders and early employees to civilian tech. |
| **L3 — third order** | Check Point Software: Founded 1993 by Gil Shwed (ex-IDF Signals Corps), core team included Unit 8200 alumni; NASDAQ IPO (1996), market cap $20-30B; Wiz: Founded 2020 by Assaf Rappaport (publicly identified as ex-Unit 8200); $10B+ valuation (2021-2023); Israeli cybersecurity sector: $6-7B annual exports (2020s); dozens of firms with Unit 8200 lineage (Imperva, SailPoint, others) |
| **Transmission mechanism** | Asset: PURE HUMAN CAPITAL - expertise in cryptography, signals processing, network analysis, code-breaking, signals intercept theory. Price change: NOT an asset price change; Israeli cybersecurity exports grew 10-20x (1995-2020). Ownership before: Individual engineers employed by Israeli military. Ownership after: Individual engineers owned their own expertise; founded or joined civilian startups. No physical asset transferred; no classic bubble or overcapacity event. This is steady-state demobilization, not crisis-driven. |
| **Discount on transfer** | No discount on transfer; human capital not sold, only demobilized. Competitive advantage derived from credibility and peer network, not fire-sale pricing. |
| **Lag** | Check Point founded (1993) - Unit 8200 formation (1952) = 41 years; but 'peak capex' is undefined (continuous operation, not ended). Suggest instead: Check Point IPO (1996) - peak Unit 8200 activity (1980s-1990s overlap) = mechanism is ongoing, not time-bound. |
| **Who captured the value** | Founders and early employees of Check Point (~$20-30B market cap), Wiz ($10B+ unicorn), and dozens of other Israeli cybersecurity firms. Israeli VC ecosystem and employees captured billions in equity. |

<sub>Sources: Check Point IPO filings and SEC 10-K (1996 onward); Wiz funding rounds and valuations (Crunchbase, PitchBook 2020-2023); Israeli news: Globes, The Marker (Unit 8200 linkage to tech sector); Bessemer Venture Partners Cybersecurity Insights reports</sub>

### Shenzhen Shanzhai → DJI and consumer drone industry

*1988-2006+* · evidence **medium**

| | |
|---|---|
| **L1 — primary** | Electronics contract manufacturing capacity (Foxconn established 1988, others 1990s). Shenzhen population 30,000 (1979) → 12.4M (2020); factories proliferated with cheap land, labor, and regulatory flexibility. |
| **L2 — overbuild / reorganisation** | Shanzhai boom (early-mid 2000s): informal copycat/adaptive manufacturing of phones, DVDs, MP3 players by thousands of small producers. Shanzhai shops iterated designs in weeks vs. 12-18 months for international brands. Accumulated capital: ~RMB billions in unsold inventory, failed product runs, thin-margin price wars. Expertise diffusion: supply chain management, cost optimization, hardware reverse-engineering. |
| **L3 — third order** | DJI (founded 2006, Frank Wang age 23): used Shenzhen manufacturing ecosystem for flight controller stabilization systems; later integrated sensors, batteries, materials all available from shanzhai suppliers. By 2016, 70% global consumer drone market. Valued $10B+ by 2015.; Anker Innovations (founded 2011, Steven Yang ex-Google): entered replacement batteries during laptop manufacturing glut, pivoted 2012 to phone charging. By 2020 IPO, valued at $7B+, leveraged same supply chain. ; Xiaomi (founded 2010): explicitly positioned as 'internet company' but sourced heavily from shanzhai-era supplier networks and hired engineers trained in shanzhai informal production. |
| **Transmission mechanism** | ASSETS: Shanzhai producers overbuilt small-batch manufacturing capacity for phone chassis, PCBs, power management ICs; when shanzhai phone market contracted (post-2010 as smartphones consolidated), this equipment and inventory became cheap. PRICE CHANGE: shanzhai-era components dropped 40-70% in unit cost by 2009-2011 as competition collapsed; battery cells, motion sensors cost 60% less than 2006.
