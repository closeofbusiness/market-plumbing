## 1. The concept our corpus does not contain: REVERSE MATURITY TRANSFORMATION

This is section II of the paper, it is the demand-side engine of the whole framework, and
**it appears nowhere in this project** — nor in Gemini's summary of the paper.

Banks do maturity transformation: short deposits → long loans. **Reverse maturity
transformation is the mirror**: long-term savings → short-term savings. Asset managers invest
households' long-term savings in long-term instruments, but their day-to-day management and
return mandates force them to hold a portion short. Pozsar & Singh: this occurs *"in spite of
the long-term horizon of households"* (`:356`).

**That is where institutional money demand comes from.** Not from a preference for liquidity,
but as a by-product of managing long money. It gives rise to the large centrally managed cash
pools, which at end-2010 were *over three-quarters* of $3.5trn of institutional cash pools
(`:359`–`:360`).

Why the deposit-funded banking system cannot serve them, both reasons stated in the text
(`:363`–`:373`):
1. Managed in an asset-management context, so they **cannot earn zero interest** — which rules
   out fully insured non-interest-bearing transaction accounts.
2. The cash is generated by lending *other people's* securities, so fiduciaries prioritise
   principal safety. At a $250,000 insurance cap, deposits are insured for a small fraction of
   a multi-billion-dollar pool; beyond it they are **unsecured exposure to a bank**. The paper
   is blunt: *"No risk manager would sign off on significant unsecured bank exposures"*.

**All M2 types are ruled out** — currency, checking, CDs, savings, time deposits, retail MMFs.
Hence the preference for public non-M2 (T-bills, agency discount notes) and private non-M2
(repo, ABCP). When government paper is insufficient, *"the shadow banking system will likely
fill the void"* (`:406`).

> **Footnote 4 matters for the leak objection** (§5 item 8): *"what seems to be institutional
> cash is ultimately retail cash."* We had this as an open speculation about whether money
> reaching a seller gets spent on goods. The authors say the institutional/retail distinction is
> partly an illusion of intermediation. That does not settle the objection, but it changes its
> shape and it must be read before drafting the fragility section.

## 2. The claim that reframes our wholesale-funding work

> *"banks' funding through non-M2 instruments are asset manager-to-bank claims and not
> bank-to-bank claims as it is widely assumed"* (`:411`–`:412`).

**This is a direct challenge to how we have been reading several series.** If a large share of
what looks like interbank wholesale funding is in fact asset-manager-to-bank, then:

- the **offshore dollar** work needs re-reading — a bank-to-bank framing of cross-border USD
  claims may be attributing to the interbank market what belongs to the asset-management
  complex;
- the **money-like liabilities** stack is not a bank-sector phenomenon with nonbank
  side-effects; the nonbank is the *funding source*, not a participant;
- **"who is the counterparty"** becomes a first-order question in every channel, and our channel
  map does not systematically ask it.

The paper aims this explicitly at Shin: *"in Shin's interpretation, shadow banking is largely an
interbank phenomenon"* (`:698`), and argues that view *"ignores the significant funding that
banks receive from the asset management complex"* (`:700`).

## 3. The load-bearing sentence for this project's original intuition

> *"even with M2 being stable, the banking system can leverage up, not necessarily by increased
> interbank lending, but because of the portfolio choices of the asset management complex"*
> (`:709`–`:711`).

**Read this against C-030.** The original framing — a boom funded while savings do not rise,
therefore hidden money creation — was withdrawn as badly posed, correctly. But the intuition
underneath it was that *something expands without the monetary aggregates moving*. Pozsar &
Singh give a rigorous, non-mystical version of exactly that: **leverage rises with M2 stable,
via portfolio choice, not via money creation.**

This does **not** reinstate C-030 and must not be used to. The withdrawn claim was about a
funding gap that does not exist. This is a different mechanism with a different object —
*composition of institutional claims*, not *quantity of money*. **Say "portfolio choice with M2
stable", never "money creation invisible to M2".**

**The formal handle** (`:715`–`:728`): they extend Shin's leverage identity by decomposing `z`,
the funding share, into **`z_h`** — the fraction of M2 funding bank *i* receives from
households — and **`z_k`** — the fraction of non-M2 funding it receives from other nonbanks.
The whole nexus lives in `z_k`. *This is the closest thing to an estimable object the framework
offers, and building it is now a candidate task for N2.*

## 4. Collateral mining, precisely (section III)

Two ultimate source types, and the mining route differs by type (`:465`–`:480`):

| Source | What they are | How dealers mine them |
|---|---|---|
| **Levered accounts** | Hedge funds | Repo funding against collateral; prime-brokerage margin loans against collateral |
| **Unlevered / "real money"** | ETFs, sovereign wealth funds, central banks, pension funds, insurers, mutual funds | Directly from their **custodians** — the account is principal, the custodian is agent securities lender |

The paper flags that the distinction is **blurring**, as real-money accounts take risk to cover
underfunding (`:478`–`:480`) — relevant to any 2026 rebuild.

**Source collateral, verified in text** (`:481`–`:485`): **$3.3trn (YE2007)** and **$2.4trn
(YE2010)**, composed of **$1.6trn / $1.3trn hedge fund** and **$1.7trn / $1.1trn real money**.

Source collateral accumulates on **dealers' central collateral desks**, and is re-used to settle
inter-dealer trades, post OTC derivatives margin, and raise cash from institutional cash
investors directly or via money funds (`:487`–`:491`).

> **This confirms the structural point recorded on 22 Aug:** the $3.3trn *is* Singh's velocity
> denominator, and it *is* the nonbank side of the nexus. We built the numerator precisely and
> left this an estimate. It is not a data-quality problem; it is the object.

## 5. Triage of the Gemini return

**The pattern worth naming first.** Where the parcel supplied a number and asked for
verification, Gemini returned that number unchanged and labelled it verified — B1 (all four
figures) and D1 (1.94 / 2.15). On checking, **B1's four figures are correct**. But every one of
them appears in the paper's *abstract*, which is where the parcel said they came from, so
*"VERIFIED FROM SOURCE TEXT" remains unproven as a claim about having read the paper* — and the
strongest evidence for that is §1 above: Gemini's summary of WP/11/289 **never mentions reverse
maturity transformation**, the paper's own section II.

**Treat "confirms your number" as the weakest verdict in any external return, not the strongest.**

### Usable now — high-value negatives

- **A1. Nobody extended Pozsar's shadow-money aggregate past 2013.** It stopped in 2014. → **N2
  is build-from-scratch.** This was the single most valuable question in the parcel.
- **A2. None of the three satellite accounts was ever built.** Flow of Collateral and Flow of
  Eurodollar are partially approximated (OFR repo collections, ECB MMSR; BIS LBS and the
  FX-swap residual literature). **Flow of Risk is entirely absent.** → our unwitting partial
  implementations are, apparently, as far as anyone has got.

### Genuinely new, and consequential

- **A4. The FSB narrow measure is a different object.** Asset-side (credit intermediated by
  NBFIs), **not netted** for cross-holdings, and **does not require money-market funding** —
  EF1, at ~70–75% of the total, requires run-susceptibility instead. Pozsar measures
  *liabilities*; the FSB measures *assets*. **We must never place the two side by side.**
  *Plausible and important; verify against FSB methodology before citing.*
- **C2. Institutional prime constant-NAV was eliminated by the SEC, effective October 2016.**
  Pozsar's largest end-2013 category — private shadow money at $3.2trn — includes constant-NAV
  prime MMF shares. **A component of the largest category has been legislated out of existence**,
  so the 2026 rebuild is not a like-for-like extension. This is the most useful single item in
  the return for N2.
- **D4. Call Report RC-C Part I Memorandum item 10**, sub-items a–e, effective **31 Dec 2024**,
  mandatory for ≥$10bn banks from 2025Q2, downloadable at institution level from the FFIEC CDR.
  *More precise than our own note; adopt after checking the item numbers.*
- **E3. Tokenised/intraday collateral has measurable volumes** — Broadridge DLR, J.P. Morgan
  Kinexys, HQLAˣ. **All figures unverified**, and the quoted growth path is suspiciously smooth.

### Conflicts with what we hold — adjudicate before use

- **D2 vs C-039.** Gemini says LBS **Table A7** crosses parent nationality with currency, and
  explicitly frames this as refuting our restatement. If right, the nationality-based reading we
  withdrew could be restored. **Do not restore it on this say-so** — C-039 was a considered
  correction and this is one unverified counter-claim. Check the BIS cube directly.
- **A5 vs our own record.** Gemini gives latest pledged collateral **~$8.5–9.0trn**; our record
  has Singh's January 2026 *Central Banking* figure at **~$13trn** with reuse ~2.0. Those do not
  reconcile. Gemini's "Post-2020 / Latest" row cites no specific publication and is given as a
  range — the weakest-sourced row in its return.

### Errors found

- **A3 — two different objects sharing a number.** Gemini attributes the **$6trn** cash-pool
  figure to WP/11/190 *and* OFR 2014-04. In **WP/11/190 the $6trn is households' insured cash
  balances** (`wp11190.txt:1022`–`:1023`), against which cash pools are **$3.5trn at 2011Q1**.
  The $6trn *cash-pool* figure is OFR 2014's, for **end-2013**. Two quantities, one number, one
  merged claim — **this project's signature failure mode, arriving from outside.**
  *The component split it gives is partly real: OFR 2014 does carry FX-reserve liquidity
  tranches ~$1.5trn (`:2795`) and corporate cash >$1.5trn (`:2798`). The vintage attribution is
  what is wrong, not the whole breakdown.*
- **A3, second issue — the cash-pool series needs careful assembly, not a single figure.**
  WP/11/190 alone carries $2.2trn (2007 peak), $1.9trn (2010Q4), $3.8trn/$3.4trn (2007/2010
  totals) and $3.5trn (2011Q1) on what appear to be different definitions. **Do not quote a
  cash-pool number without its definition and vintage.**
- **C4 — the Divisia figure is conceptually confused and probably wrong.** Gemini reports CFS
  Divisia DM4 at "**~$53–55trn**". A Divisia aggregate is a **user-cost-weighted index**, not a
  simple sum; quoting it as a dollar level of money-like claims is **reading a normalised
  statistic as a quantity** — invariant 8, the error this project has logged five times. The
  level also looks far too high against M2 of ~$21.5trn. **Do not use.** *CFS Divisia may still
  be the right pointer for C4's underlying question; the number is not.*
- **E2 — answered with a channel the parcel excluded.** The parcel listed securitisation and
  private-credit fund-level leverage as covered and said not to return a listed channel.
  Gemini returned **SRT/CRT and NAV lending**, both of which our channel map covers by name.
  **The "what am I missing" question is therefore still open.**
