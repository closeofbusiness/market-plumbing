# Input Substrate — Forward-Thinker Theses

What serious forward thinkers are currently claiming, gathered as panel input. *2 August 2026.*

## AI, compute and energy — where the buildout leads (state of named forward-thinker opinion as at 2 August 2026)

### Michael Burry (Scion Asset Management)
*Core claim 11 November 2025 (X posts, reported by CNBC); extended and re-argued 8 July 2026 (Substack, 'Short Thoughts: NVDA, Neos, Hyperscalers, Jevons Paradox, and Compression')*

Hyperscaler earnings are an accounting artefact. Depreciation is a lever, not a physical measurement, and the useful lives being applied to Nvidia-based datacenter hardware (5–6 years) do not survive contact with an annual product cadence. His framing: the frontier moves faster than the depreciation schedules. He treats the CoreWeave/neocloud counter-narrative — H100s re-leasing near original price, six-to-seven-year rental terms — as conflating logistics and scarcity-driven hoarding with genuine economic durability, and notes the tell that operators move lives in whichever direction flatters the quarter (Amazon shortening to 5 years and taking $677m of extra expense over nine months; Meta extending to 5.5 years and saving $2.9bn of depreciation).

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: reported hyperscaler/neocloud earnings are overstated because economic GPU life is ~2–3 years, not 5–6. Reorganisation: once the market re-underwrites on cash-based or shorter-life economics, the equity of asset-heavy compute intermediaries (Oracle, CoreWeave-type neoclouds) reprices hardest, and hyperscaler multiples compress toward capital-intensity peers rather than software peers. Third order: the cost of capital for GPU-backed debt rises, the vendor-financing and SPV structures funding the buildout become expensive or unavailable, and the marginal datacenter does not get built — meaning the physical constraint that eventually binds is credit, not power or silicon.
- **Testable content:** Quantified and directly checkable against filings: ~$176bn of understated depreciation cumulatively 2026–2028 across the industry; Oracle earnings overstated ~27% and Meta ~21% by 2028 on his adjustment. Individually verifiable: Amazon's $677m nine-month expense from shortening server lives; Meta's $2.9bn reduction from extending to 5.5 years; Microsoft's move from 4 to 6 years. Checkable going forward: whether any hyperscaler shortens stated useful lives in a 10-K/10-Q between now and 2028; whether resale/lease-renewal prices for H100/A100 published by neoclouds hold at the claimed ~95% of original; his own positioning (13F/13F-HR — puts on NVDA ~$187m notional and PLTR ~$912m notional as of the September 2025 filing).
- **Where they could be wrong:** Depreciation life and cash economics are different questions — if utilisation stays near 100% and older silicon migrates to inference and batch work rather than being retired, a 6-year life can be economically honest even with an annual cadence. Jensen Huang's 'you couldn't give Hoppers away' line, which Burry uses, is about relative desirability at the frontier, not about zero residual value. And an accounting-quality short is not a timing thesis: he was early and visibly wrong-footed through the 2025 rally, and understated depreciation only becomes a stock event if someone forces recognition. If token demand keeps compounding, revenue growth simply outruns the depreciation catch-up.

### Jim Chanos (Chanos & Co. / Kynikos)
*15 June 2026 (Global Alts New York 2026); earlier and more granular in the 'AI Data Center Short' episode of Monetary Matters with Jack Farley and in Colossus's 'A Short Thesis on Data Centers'*

Hosting GPUs is a commodity business dressed up as a growth business. Established datacenter operators earn mid-to-low single-digit pretax returns on capital, which he calls a really bad business; the neoclouds are equipment leasing with the whole equity value sitting in the depreciation assumption. His structural rule: middlemen should never trade at higher multiples than the companies that control their supply — TSMC, Nvidia, AMD. He extends the same 'hopes and dreams' valuation critique to the adjacent private complex (SpaceX at ~$2tn on ~$19bn revenue and negative FCF).

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: at honest useful lives, GPU-hosting ROIC rounds to zero. Reorganisation: capital stops flowing to the intermediating layer; neoclouds and datacenter REIT-like vehicles cannot roll their financing; the assets get absorbed at distressed prices by the hyperscalers who actually have the workloads. Third order: value concentrates further in the supply-controlling chokepoints (foundry, accelerator design, advanced packaging, memory) and in whoever ends up owning distressed physical capacity cheaply — i.e. the buildout still happens, but the equity of the people who financed round one is wiped and the assets change hands.
- **Testable content:** CoreWeave arithmetic: on the CEO's own 2–3 year useful-life estimate, annual depreciation ~$2.7bn against EBITDA ~$2.6bn, leaving nothing for equity — recomputable each quarter from the 10-Q. He says even a lenient 10-year schedule fails to make most datacenter economics work. Two dated forward predictions: 2026 sets an all-time record for US equity issuance, exceeding 1999, 2000 and 2021 (checkable from SIFMA/Dealogic issuance data at year end); and the reckoning arrives between late 2026 and 2027 as hyperscaler incremental ROIC falls and rates back up. Also checkable: whether datacenter operators' reported pretax ROIC stays in the mid-to-low single digits.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The take-or-pay contract structure matters — if a neocloud's GPUs are pre-sold under multi-year contracts to investment-grade counterparties, the depreciation-versus-EBITDA framing understates contracted cash coverage. Chanos also underweights the possibility that scarcity persists long enough for a second and third re-lease cycle at good prices (see Patel, Epoch). His CoreWeave short has been painful before, and he is explicitly using rising rates as the trigger, which makes the thesis partly a macro bet he does not control.

### Paul Kedrosky (SK Ventures)
*16 June 2026 ('AI Set to be Largest CapEx Cycle Ever … and Soon Majority Externally Financed'); earlier posts 'Honey, AI CapEx Ate the Economy' and 'Chart of the Day: AI Capex Ate the Economy in Q4' (Q4 2025 data)*

This is the largest capex cycle in history and, more importantly, the first one where the marginal dollar comes from outside the cash-generating firms. He argues the cycle's fate is tied to debt market appetite, not to hyperscaler conviction — a distinction he treats as the whole ballgame. He has also documented that AI capex has become a majority of measured US GDP growth, which makes it a macro object rather than a sector story.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: as internal cash flow stops covering capex, external debt and equity become the majority of funding. Reorganisation: the buildout's clearing mechanism moves from corporate boardrooms to credit spreads — private credit, ABS backed by GPUs and leases, and SPV structures become the transmission channel. Third order: a credit contraction stops the buildout cold regardless of AI demand, and because AI capex is now the dominant GDP growth contributor, a spread-widening event transmits straight into US growth and employment — the AI cycle and the credit cycle become the same cycle.
- **Testable content:** Highly quantified and all checkable: trajectory toward ~$1.5tn/yr of hyperscaler and datacenter investment by 2030, which he says would exceed in real terms the 2005–06 residential construction boom, the peak year of the 1990s fibre build and the 1882 railroad peak combined; externally financed share approaching ~90% by 2030; AI datacenter capex nearing ~2% of US GDP and roughly 20% of peak railroad spending as a share of GDP. Most testable of all: his Q4 2025 BEA decomposition — the information-processing equipment line contributed +0.66pp to GDP growth while the entire rest of the economy contributed ~+0.04pp. Rerun the BEA series each quarter. Also checkable: hyperscaler free cash flow versus capex, and the volume/spread of GPU- and datacenter-backed ABS issuance.
- **Where they could be wrong:** External financing is not intrinsically fragile — the 90%-external figure is an extrapolation, and a large share of it is investment-grade corporate debt at companies with enormous EBITDA, not the non-recourse project debt the framing implies. His railroad and fibre analogies also cut the other way: both overbuilds destroyed investor capital while producing enormous, durable social surplus, which is a very different conclusion from 'misallocated'. And the GDP-contribution statistic is arithmetically true but partly reflects an unusually weak non-AI economy, not AI dominance.

### Ed Zitron (Where's Your Ed At)
*Throughout 2026; the audited-financials analysis and the 'Lehman moment' framing circulated mid-July 2026 (Quartz, 17 July 2026); his $852bn cumulative-funding estimate reported by AI Weekly in 2026; Apple 'watch everything burn' remarks 27 July 2026*

The buildout rests on a single entity whose unit economics do not work, and the demand is substantially manufactured rather than discovered. He argues generative AI is not systemically important, and that the correct policy response to its failure is to let it break — explicitly opposing any bailout. His mechanism for the break is that subscription pricing cannot cover agentic token consumption, advertising cannot fill the gap, and compute costs keep rising.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: OpenAI's cash burn compounds faster than revenue and it cannot fund ~$665bn of cloud commitments running to 2030. Reorganisation: OpenAI's failure to raise forces a repricing of every counterparty that booked its compute commitments as revenue or backlog — Oracle, Microsoft, CoreWeave, Nvidia — exposing the circularity of the flows. Third order: an 'AI Lehman' — the collapse propagates through private credit and the vendor-financing web rather than through public equity alone, and the affected parties are pension and insurance balance sheets that bought the paper, not the labs.
- **Testable content:** The most directly checkable numbers in the whole set, because he claims to be working from obtained audited statements: OpenAI 2025 revenue $13.07bn against $34bn total expenses, operating loss ~$20.9bn, net loss $38.5bn; Q1 2026 revenue $5.7bn against $3.7bn cash burn; projected burn $25bn in 2026 rising to $57bn in 2027; cloud/compute commitments ~$665bn through 2030; cumulative funding need >$852bn by 2030. Each is falsifiable against any future OpenAI disclosure, S-1, or investor letter. Also checkable and unusually specific: his claim that once Uber, Zillow and Meta were made to pay real token cost in early 2026 they revolted within three months for lack of demonstrable ROI — that is a named-customer claim that can be checked against those companies' own disclosures.
- **Where they could be wrong:** He has been directionally early and rhetorically absolute for two years while OpenAI revenue roughly tripled year-on-year on his own figures — a company tripling revenue while burning cash is the standard profile of a scaling platform, not obviously of a fraud. 'Demand is manufactured' is the weakest link and the hardest to falsify. The systemic-importance claim is also contestable in both directions: if it is not systemically important, the Lehman analogy fails; if the Lehman analogy holds, it is systemically important. Sourcing on the audited figures is second-hand and should be treated as unconfirmed until a primary document surfaces.

### Daron Acemoglu (MIT)
*'The Simple Macroeconomics of AI' — NBER WP 32487, May 2024, published in Economic Policy (2025); reaffirmed and updated in a May 2026 MIT Technology Review interview*

The measured macroeconomic return to AI is an order of magnitude smaller than what the capex implies. Working from task-level exposure and task-level productivity gains, he puts the total factor productivity effect at no more than ~0.66% over ten years, and argues even that is likely overstated because the observed gains come from easy-to-learn tasks with objective outcome measures, while the remaining exposed tasks are context-dependent and lack a learnable success signal. In 2026 he has been pointing at the still-missing layer of broadly usable applications as the reason capability has not converted into measured output.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: aggregate TFP gains from AI over a decade are ~0.5–0.7%, not the 7–10% implied by bullish forecasts. Reorganisation: at that level of productivity uplift, the willingness-to-pay for inference is bounded well below what current capex requires, so the revenue to service the buildout never appears; the gap closes through write-downs rather than through demand. Third order: value accrues not to compute or to models but to the small number of firms that solve the last-mile deployment problem in hard-to-learn, context-dependent tasks — and possibly to nobody, if the tasks are genuinely not learnable from available signal.
- **Testable content:** Point forecasts that can be scored against published statistics: ~1.1% cumulative US GDP uplift over a decade, ~0.05% annual productivity uplift, TFP effect ≤0.66% and plausibly <0.53% over ten years. These are directly comparable with BLS multifactor productivity and BEA output-per-hour series, and with Goldman Sachs's competing ~7% estimate — the two are far enough apart to be resolved within a few years of data. Corroborating and independently checkable: Anthropic's own head of economics Peter McCrory published an analysis on 24 July 2026 finding no relative deterioration in unemployment among highly AI-exposed occupations, prime-age employment near multi-decade highs, and no occupation with all its tasks handled by Claude — which is the labour-market shadow of Acemoglu's productivity claim, from an unexpectedly aligned source.
- **Where they could be wrong:** His method assumes the task distribution is roughly fixed and that AI substitutes into existing tasks — it is essentially a Baumol/Nordhaus decomposition, which structurally cannot capture new-task creation or capability discontinuities. If agents materially change what a firm can attempt rather than how fast it does what it already does, the task-share denominator is wrong. The framework is also silent on the physical-science and R&D acceleration channel that Amodei's case rests on. And 'no signal yet' in 2026 is consistent with both his thesis and with a normal J-curve of intangible-investment lag (Brynjolfsson's counter).

### Jensen Huang (Nvidia)
*GTC 2026 keynote, 16 March 2026 (reported by CNBC same day); Computex 2026 for the HBM4E gesture*

Demand for AI compute has repeatedly and materially exceeded the industry's own forecasts, and the correct posture is to plan for supply shortfall rather than glut. His stated position at GTC 2026 was that the $500bn of high-confidence Blackwell-plus-Rubin demand he described a year earlier had doubled, that actual demand might be higher still, and that Nvidia is prepared for demand outstripping supply. He is publicly signalling that his own inputs are the binding constraint — at Computex 2026 he wrote 'Please Make More' on an SK Hynix HBM4E wafer.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: order book of ~$1tn through 2027 across Blackwell and Vera Rubin, with demand still exceeding supply. Reorganisation: the constraint migrates off the accelerator die and onto everything adjacent — HBM, advanced packaging, power delivery, and grid interconnection — so the marginal scarcity rent moves to memory makers, packaging capacity and power. Third order: if the order book is real, the buildout is under-built rather than over-built, and the parties who capture the third-order value are the ones who own the newly-binding constraint: HBM oligopoly (SK Hynix, Micron, Samsung), CoWoS-class packaging, turbine and transformer manufacturers, and interconnection-queue positions.
- **Testable content:** The single most checkable claim in the set, because it is an order book with a date on it: $1tn of purchase orders for Blackwell and Vera Rubin through 2027, double the $500bn cited in March 2025. Verifiable against Nvidia's quarterly revenue run-rate, purchase commitments and supply obligations disclosed in the 10-Q, and against customer concentration disclosures. Corroborating and separately checkable: Vera Rubin in full production during 2026; TSMC's CoWoS capacity ramp; whether HBM4/4E remains sold out. Note the counterparty problem — an order book is only as good as the balance sheets behind it, and the same names appear in Zitron's and Chanos's analyses as the weak links.
- **Where they could be wrong:** 'High-confidence demand' and 'purchase orders' are Nvidia-defined terms with soft cancellation and reschedule provisions, and a meaningful slice of the book traces back to entities Nvidia has itself invested in or vendor-financed — the circularity critics point to. He is also the single most conflicted narrator in the debate. Satya Nadella's November 2025 observation on the Bg2 Pod that Microsoft has GPUs sitting in inventory it cannot plug in because it lacks warm shells is the sharpest internal contradiction: if chips ship faster than power arrives, the order book converts to revenue but not to deployed compute, and the resulting idle-asset depreciation is exactly Burry's point.

### Dylan Patel (SemiAnalysis)
*Predictions published 1 July 2026; memory-shortage and CPU-bottleneck arguments through the SemiAnalysis newsletter in 2026 ('CPUs are Back: The Datacenter CPU Landscape in 2026'); earlier framing in the GSA/EmTech webinar, November 2025*

Compute shortage is structural and effectively permanent, because each capability increment expands the universe of economically addressable tasks faster than supply can grow. He is explicit that he is not forecasting the end of shortage. His scale claim is that AI inference becomes one of the largest markets in the world, exceeding oil, at several percentage points of GDP. Separately he argues memory is the acute near-term chokepoint — capacity grows 20–30% a year while AI demand doubles — and that CPU supply for RL training and agentic tool-calling has become an unexpected second bottleneck, with frontier labs bidding against cloud providers for allocation.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: demand for inference expands with capability, so shortage persists indefinitely and prices do not clear downward at the system level even as unit costs collapse. Reorganisation: the scarcity rent moves down the stack to whatever is least elastic — currently HBM and DRAM, then advanced packaging, then power, then eventually orbital/space compute. Third order: if inference really is an oil-scale market, then compute becomes a strategic commodity with sovereign-level procurement, geographic arbitrage on power price, and a physical logistics layer — and the durable value accrues to owners of inelastic inputs, not to model developers or to datacenter operators.
- **Testable content:** Several hard, dated numbers: >100 GW of AI compute by 2030; more than half of incremental compute in space by 2040 with less than 1% by 2030; DRAM/HBM capacity growth 20–30%/yr against demand doubling. His InferenceX benchmark claims inference cost falls ~60x per year at equivalent quality and intelligence-per-watt improves ~40x per year — those are proprietary but published series that can be tracked and compared against Epoch AI's independent estimate of 9x–900x per year depending on capability threshold. The 60x figure is materially more aggressive than most published estimates and is the load-bearing number for his 'shortage persists despite cost collapse' argument. Immediately checkable: DRAM contract prices, SK Hynix/Micron/Samsung sold-out status, and hyperscaler CPU procurement.
- **Where they could be wrong:** 'Shortage forever' is a Jevons argument, and Jevons requires demand elasticity to exceed the cost decline — if enterprise willingness-to-pay saturates (Acemoglu's ceiling, the FinOps evidence that 73% of enterprises overran AI budgets and pushed back), the elasticity assumption fails and shortage resolves into glut abruptly. He also sells research to the industry he is forecasting, so his incentives run bullish. The space-compute prediction is far enough out to be unfalsifiable on any relevant horizon and should be discounted entirely for panel purposes.

### Luke Emberson and Jaime Sevilla (Epoch AI)
*25 May 2026, Epoch AI Gradient Updates, 'Is a compute crunch coming?'*

Token supply and token demand are growing at different rates and the gap is closing on a schedule that forces price increases. Their estimate is that global inference supply is growing at roughly 3.4x annually while demand grows at roughly 10x. Their stated consequence is not a capacity crisis but a price event: the price of tokens from large models will rise. They also identify context length as the hidden variable that determines whether there is a shortage at all — the same fleet serves 20 billion output tokens/sec at short context and 500 million at long context.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: demand growth (~10x/yr) outruns supply growth (~3.4x/yr), and long-context agentic workloads consume the fleet 40x faster than short-context chat. Reorganisation: frontier-model token prices rise for the first time; the market stratifies into a premium tier paying real scarcity prices for frontier long-context inference and a mass tier pushed onto smaller distilled models. Third order: rising inference prices break the flat-rate subscription business model across the entire application layer simultaneously, transferring margin from AI application companies to compute owners — the opposite of the Benedict Evans conclusion — and making context-length efficiency (KV-cache, sparse attention, distillation) the highest-leverage research direction in the industry, because it is the only lever that relieves the constraint without new capex.
- **Testable content:** Unusually well-specified and falsifiable within months: Q4 2025 global Blackwell fleet capable of 500m–20bn output tokens/sec depending on context ratio (20bn at 8k:1k, 6bn at 25k:1k, 500m at 128k:1k); demand estimated at 200m–4bn tokens/sec; Google alone ~1.2bn tokens/sec across platforms; supply +3.4x/yr, demand +10x/yr. The central prediction — frontier-model token prices rise — is directly observable on published API price cards and is the cleanest single falsifier in this whole document. Corroborating 2026 evidence already visible: Alibaba Cloud and Baidu AI Cloud raised AI compute prices on 18 March 2026 following AWS, Google Cloud and Tencent Cloud; Cursor replaced its $20 flat plan with credit-based pricing; Salesforce repriced Agentforce three times in eighteen months. Countervailing and equally checkable: blended cost per million tokens fell from $18.40 to $6.07 between Q1 2025 and Q1 2026.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The demand estimate has a twentyfold uncertainty band (200m–4bn tokens/sec), which is wide enough that supply may already exceed demand comfortably at the short-context end. The 10x demand growth rate is extrapolated from a short and unusual window (the agentic step-change) and may be a level shift rather than a trend. Their own escape valve is real: if distillation keeps producing small models matching last year's frontier, effective supply grows much faster than 3.4x in capability-adjusted terms, and prices fall rather than rise. Note also the direct conflict with Patel's 60x/yr cost decline — both cannot be describing the same market.

### Scott Strazik (GE Vernova)
*Q1 2026 results, 22 April 2026 (Form 8-K); backlog figure updated through mid-2026; capacity roadmap reiterated across 2026 disclosures*

The physical energy constraint on the AI buildout is not fuel or policy but turbine manufacturing capacity, and it is already fully committed. His company's position is that gas equipment backlog plus slot reservations have reached 116 GW against roughly 10 GW/yr of heavy-duty output — approximately a decade of production already spoken for — and he expects reservations to be booked out through 2030 by the end of 2026. The corollary he does not need to state is that the capacity to convert capital into new dispatchable power sits with three firms worldwide.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: turbine slots are sold out through 2030; the bottleneck on new firm power is a three-company manufacturing oligopoly (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries). Reorganisation: datacenter developers who did not reserve slots in 2024–25 cannot buy their way to firm power at any price this decade, so they are pushed to behind-the-meter alternatives, to existing-grid interconnection queues with five-to-twelve-year waits, or to demand flexibility. Third order: power availability, not capital or silicon, sorts the winners of the AI buildout — a compute plan without a 2027-vintage turbine slot or an interconnection position is not a plan, and the option value sits with incumbents holding queue positions, retiring coal sites, existing nuclear PPAs, and turbine reservations. Scarcity rent accrues to the three turbine makers and to whoever already holds interconnection rights.
- **Testable content:** Everything here is in SEC filings and therefore auditable: 116 GW combined gas equipment backlog and slot reservation agreements; 52 heavy-duty and 61 aeroderivative turbine orders; 10 GW of reservations converted to firm orders in the quarter; guidance of at least 110 GW combined by year-end 2026; manufacturing capacity roadmap of 20 GW annualised in Q3 2026, 24 GW in 2028, 30 GW by 2030; datacenter-linked electrification orders of $2.4bn in Q1 and >$5bn year-to-date. Independently checkable adjacent facts: over 2,060 GW in US interconnection queues at end-2025 with median time-to-COD approaching five years; 198 GW of large-load interconnection applications in ERCOT in Q1 2026 alone, with 86 GW under review — roughly ERCOT's entire current peak.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Backlog and 'slot reservations' are not the same thing, and the reservation component carries cancellation optionality — if the capex cycle turns, reservations evaporate before firm orders do, and GE Vernova is a highly cyclical equipment maker being valued on a peak book. The oligopoly is also contestable on a 2030 horizon: Chinese and Korean heavy-turbine capacity, plus the possibility that solar-plus-storage undercuts gas entirely (Casey Handmer's position, arguing that at sub-$150/kWh integrated storage and sub-$200/kW-DC solar shipping from China in early 2026, pure solar-and-battery datacenters at 99–99.9% availability beat turbine-constrained gas), would make the turbine backlog a description of a bottleneck that gets routed around rather than one that binds.

### Tyler Norris (Duke Nicholas Institute; subsequently energy policy, Google)
*'Rethinking Load Growth: Assessing the Potential for Integration of Large Flexible Loads in US Power Systems' — Duke Nicholas Institute, 13 February 2025 (Norris, Profeta, Patino-Echeverri, Cowie-Haskell); follow-on work by the same institute published 17 February 2026 (authored by Martin Ross and Jackson Ewing, not Norris, who has moved to Google)*

The grid constraint is largely an artefact of how interconnection is administered, not of physical capacity. His concept is curtailment-enabled headroom: if new large loads accept curtailment of a quarter of one percent of annual consumption for short durations, the existing US power system can absorb roughly 76 GW of new load — about a 10% expansion of US peak demand, which exceeds upper-end forecasts for datacenter additions through the early 2030s. Across balancing authorities the figure approaches 100 GW.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: ~76–100 GW of load can be added to the existing system with trivial flexibility commitments, because the system is sized for a handful of peak hours a year. Reorganisation: if flexibility is priced and contracted, the binding constraint stops being generation and transmission and becomes the institutional willingness of utilities and ISOs to offer non-firm and conditional-firm interconnection service — a tariff problem, not an engineering one. Third order: this inverts the entire energy-scarcity trade. If flexibility is adopted, turbine backlogs and interconnection queues stop being the chokepoint and the scarcity rent evaporates; the value instead accrues to whoever can make AI workloads schedulable — batch inference orchestration, geographically distributed training, on-site storage — and datacenter operators capable of curtailment get to the front of the queue for free.
- **Testable content:** The headline numbers are model outputs against real utility data and can be re-derived: 0.25% annual curtailment unlocking 76 GW; ~100 GW across balancing authorities. The February 2026 follow-on adds separately checkable figures — disconnecting during the costliest 1–2% of peak hours cuts new gas construction by up to 15% (~12 GW); 20% flexible demand cuts gas build by nearly 20% and lowers electricity prices by up to $3/MWh; up to $150bn in cumulative customer savings. The strongest real-world test now underway: FERC's 2026 order requiring six grid operators to rewrite large-load interconnection rules for datacenters above 20 MW, and whether the resulting tariffs actually create a flexible-load service class. Also checkable: how many announced datacenters sign non-firm or curtailable interconnection agreements versus building on-site gas.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The theoretical potential and the contracted reality are very different things, and the gap is behavioural. Training runs and latency-sensitive inference are exactly the workloads operators are least willing to curtail, and a hyperscaler that has pre-committed $50bn of GPUs has an overwhelming incentive to pay for firm power rather than accept even 0.25% curtailment risk. Utilities also earn a regulated return on capital additions and are structurally disinclined to substitute a tariff for a build. Brian Janous's observation that AEP's anticipated PJM datacenter demand was revised from ~4 GW to ~15 GW between January and May 2026 suggests the load forecast itself is moving faster than the headroom, which would consume the 76 GW quickly even if it is real.

### Benedict Evans (independent)
*Podcast appearance 25 June 2026 ('If AI Models Have No Moat, What Are Investors Buying?'); the 'no moat except access to capital' formulation reported by Fortune, 7 May 2025; positions restated in the 2026 edition of his semi-annual 'AI Eats the World' deck*

Frontier models are commoditising and there is no structural moat at the model layer — no network effect, no winner-take-all dynamic, nothing preventing three to six well-capitalised labs and a dozen fast followers from converging. His explanation is that they are converging because they share inputs: the same infrastructure, the same training data, the same algorithms. The only moat he grants the labs is access to capital. His conclusion is that durable value comes from products and use cases, not from better models.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: model capability converges; benchmark differentiation stops being monetisable. Reorganisation: models become utility infrastructure on the telecom pattern — very large revenue, low margins, poor returns on invested capital. His analogy is explicit: telecoms run ~$200bn/yr of capex in a trillion-dollar industry and have delivered negligible shareholder returns for two decades. Third order: value accrues at the two ends and hollows out in the middle — upstream to chips and energy, downstream to applications, distribution and go-to-market — with the model layer capturing least despite spending most. This is the direct antithesis of the Epoch conclusion, which has compute owners capturing the margin as inference prices rise.
- **Testable content:** The convergence claim is measurable now: the spread between frontier closed models and open-weight models on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index and standard benchmarks. Concrete 2026 datapoints to check — z.ai's GLM 5.2 (753bn parameters, MIT licence, June 2026) reportedly matching frontier scores at roughly one-sixth the cost, and Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 (2.8tn parameters, July 2026) placing fourth of 189 models on that index. The economic version is checkable from disclosed gross margins: whether frontier lab inference gross margin compresses toward cloud-infrastructure margins over the next four to six quarters, and whether pricing power shows up as list-price increases (which would support Epoch and refute Evans) or as continued price cuts. The telecom analogy is checkable as a base rate: telecom sector capex, revenue and total shareholder return, 2005–2025.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Benchmark convergence and revenue convergence are different. If frontier labs retain the top 5% of workloads where reliability matters — agentic execution, long-horizon coding, regulated deployments — a small capability lead can carry a very large revenue share, which is the enterprise-software pattern rather than the telecom pattern. Evans also treats capital access as a weak moat, but at a $50bn+ annual training-plus-inference run rate capital access may be the strongest moat there is, since it is the one thing a fast follower cannot replicate. And the telecom analogy assumes an undifferentiated bit; inference is not undifferentiated if latency, context length and tool reliability differ materially.

### C.C. Wei (TSMC)
*Q1 2026 earnings call ('extremely robust' AI demand); Q2 2026 earnings call, 16 July 2026, where the CAGR was raised and full-year capex guidance lifted*

His stated position is that conviction in the multi-year AI megatrend remains very high, and he has revised the demand curve upward rather than down — raising the expected 2024–29 CAGR for AI accelerator revenue to 54–56% from a prior ~45%. He has also publicly conceded that his own advanced packaging capacity, not wafer output, is what is constraining customers, to the point of saying TSMC welcomes competitors' packaging offerings as a way to relieve pressure.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: AI accelerator demand compounds at 54–56% to 2029, and the near-term constraint is CoWoS-class advanced packaging rather than leading-edge lithography. Reorganisation: packaging capacity becomes the allocation mechanism for the whole industry — who gets accelerators in 2027 is decided by who holds CoWoS slots, which advantages Nvidia and Broadcom's largest customers and squeezes everyone else, including the custom-ASIC programmes trying to displace Nvidia. Third order: the sector's scarcity rent sits in a narrow, capital-intensive, hard-to-replicate process step with essentially one qualified supplier at scale, concentrated in Taiwan — making advanced packaging simultaneously the highest-return position in the chain and the single largest geopolitical concentration risk in the AI buildout. It also means the marginal accelerator's cost floor is set by packaging and HBM, not by the logic die.
- **Testable content:** All of it is in the earnings calls and quarterly reports: AI accelerator revenue CAGR raised to 54–56% from ~45% for 2024–29; FY2026 capex guidance raised to $60–64bn from $52–56bn; CoWoS capacity ~35k wafers/month at end-2024, ~75k at end-2025, target 125–130k by end-2026 (roughly 80%/yr expansion) — trackable quarterly and the cleanest physical proxy for whether the buildout is accelerating or stalling. TSMC full-year growth outlook above 40%. Because TSMC sits upstream of every accelerator vendor, its CoWoS ramp is the best available independent check on both Huang's $1tn order book and the bears' claim that the buildout is about to stall: the order book cannot be real if packaging capacity does not arrive.
- **Where they could be wrong:** TSMC's visibility is customer-order visibility, and its customers' orders are in turn conditioned on financing that Kedrosky and Chanos argue is fragile — a 54–56% CAGR guided in mid-2026 reflects a 2026 order book, not 2029 demand. Wei has also guided margin caution alongside the capex raise, which is a tell that he expects cost or pricing pressure. The bigger structural risk to his framing is substitution rather than demand collapse: custom ASICs (Broadcom's programmes for Google TPU, Meta MTIA, Microsoft Maia, and OpenAI/Anthropic accelerators) are projected around 44% shipment growth in 2026 and roughly 27.8% of AI server shipments, which changes who buys the packaging but also changes the mix and margin of what is packaged.

### Dario Amodei (Anthropic)
*'End of the exponential' interview with Dwarkesh Patel, February 2026; jobs warning originally Axios, May 2025, doubled down February 2026 (Forbes, 21 February 2026); Jevons-paradox reframing reported by Fortune, 5 May 2026*

His position is unusual in being simultaneously the most aggressive capability forecast and an argument for capital restraint. He describes approaching the end of the exponential — not a slowdown but the saturation of benchmarks calibrated to human performance — and holds ~90% confidence in AGI within ten years, with a personal hunch of one to three years for 'a country of geniuses in a data centre'. Yet he explicitly declines to bet Anthropic's balance sheet, because scaling compute 10x annually is existentially dangerous if the timeline slips by even one year: revenue growth, not conviction, sets the safe rate of compute purchase. On labour he has doubled down on his warning that roughly half of entry-level white-collar roles could disappear within five years and unemployment could reach 10–20%, while in 2026 shifting toward a Jevons-paradox framing of the offsetting job creation.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: capability keeps compounding to human-saturation across cognitive tasks within a few years, and compute demand compounds ~10x/yr to match. Reorganisation: the labs that can fund 10x/yr compute growth from revenue survive; those funding it from capital markets are exposed to a single year of timeline slippage — which is the same fragility Kedrosky identifies, argued from inside the buildout by a believer. Third order: if he is right on capability, the labour-substitution wave is a genuine reorganisation of white-collar output and the value accrues to whoever owns the automated labour, not to whoever owns the compute — because compute is a commodity input to a service being sold at labour-replacement prices. If he is right on the financing fragility but wrong on timing, the labs most convinced of short timelines are the ones that fail first.
- **Testable content:** Several dated, scoreable predictions: end-to-end coding automation within one to two years of February 2026 (i.e. by early 2028) — 'there's no way we will not be there'; 90% confidence in AGI within ten years; 50% of entry-level white-collar roles gone within five years of May 2025; unemployment 10–20%. The financing claim is checkable against Anthropic's disclosed compute commitments versus revenue: whether Anthropic's compute purchase growth stays at or below its revenue growth, in contrast to OpenAI's. The labour claim has an unusually clean and immediate falsifier from inside his own company — Peter McCrory, Anthropic's head of economics, published on 24 July 2026 after eighteen months of BLS and internal-usage analysis that there is no relative unemployment deterioration among highly AI-exposed workers, prime-age employment is near multi-decade highs, no occupation has all its tasks handled by Claude, and observed usage is iterate-and-refine rather than task handover. Also checkable: the Yale Budget Lab finding of no meaningful change in unemployment for AI-exposed workers since late 2022.
- **Where they could be wrong:** His own chief economist's data contradicts the labour timeline, and both Amodei and Altman have publicly conceded in 2026 that they were roughly right on technological predictions and wrong on the social and economic implications. 'End of the exponential' is also a claim about benchmarks rather than about economic output — saturating human-calibrated evals is compatible with Acemoglu's small TFP number if the saturated tasks are not the economically load-bearing ones. He is additionally the most conflicted possible narrator of an AGI timeline that determines his company's valuation, and the Jevons pivot on jobs is a soft retreat from a falsifiable claim to an unfalsifiable one.

## Energy, Materials and Industrial — Chinese solar/battery overcapacity, electrification, grid and transmission, critical minerals, nuclear and SMRs, reindustrialisation and supply-chain relocation. Substrate assembled 2 August 2026. Important shared context all four analysts should hold: (a) US/Israeli strikes on Iran 28 Feb 2026 and the Iranian declaration of Strait of Hormuz closure from 4 Mar 2026 produced what the IEA called the largest supply disruption in oil market history, with Brent around $105/bbl in early May 2026 — this contaminates every "energy security" thesis below and is the live macro variable; (b) China's H1 2026 solar installations fell 66% year-on-year to 72.07 GW from 212.21 GW, the first annual decline in seven years, following the 1 June 2025 shift from feed-in tariffs to market pricing; (c) China is phasing in a 2% consumption tax on batteries from 1 Sept 2026 and on solar cells from 1 Apr 2027, cancelling PV export VAT rebates from 1 Apr 2026 and cutting battery export rebates from 9% to 6%, eliminated 1 Jan 2027; (d) US manufacturing construction spending was ~$174.8bn SAAR in May 2026, down ~21.9% year-on-year, with electronics/semiconductor fab spend down 44% from its mid-2024 peak.

### Brad Setser, Council on Foreign Relations
*27 July 2026 (CFR piece, 'Is China's Surplus Really Shrinking?'); developing continuously through 2025-26*

Setser rejects the claim that China's trade surplus has peaked. He argues the headline goods surplus is being flattered downwards by an enormous, anomalous gold import line, and that once you strip gold out the 'real', price-adjusted surplus is still expanding. Manufacturing exports excluding chips and gold are rising steadily, auto exports in his words are "absolutely booming", and imports are flat. His broader position is that the second China shock is still in its early innings and that tariffs to date have rerouted rather than resolved it — his stated expectation is that the established trajectory has the US tariffing everyone else roughly as much as China.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: Chinese manufactured-goods supply keeps outrunning Chinese domestic absorption, so the surplus in real terms keeps growing and manufactured-goods prices keep falling. Reorganisation: the adjustment cannot land on the US alone (already tariffed), so it lands on the EU, ASEAN, Latin America, India and Turkey, forcing them into their own tariff and safeguard regimes through 2026-27 — a generalised, non-US-centred protection wave. Third order: value accrues to whoever owns the scarce non-tradeable complements to cheap Chinese goods — installation labour, grid connections, permits, land, distribution and service networks — while it is destroyed in anyone owning tradeable clean-tech manufacturing assets outside China. Secondarily, persistent goods deflation caps global core inflation and keeps duration bid even while energy is spiking, which is a genuinely unusual macro configuration.
- **Testable content:** Gold imports YTD 2026 of ~$146bn, ~$100bn above H1 2025, with Q2 gold imports alone at 1.5% of GDP. Ex-gold, the goods surplus would have risen by over $80bn in H1 2026 and would be running at roughly 7% of GDP. Net exports contributed positively to Q1 and Q2 2026 growth. All of this is checkable against China customs monthly data, the SAFE BoP series and the GDP expenditure decomposition. Also checkable: whether the US-China bilateral deficit, which narrowed by almost a third to $202.1bn in 2025, keeps narrowing while China's global surplus widens — that divergence is the whole argument.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The gold adjustment is a judgement call: if the gold is genuinely being absorbed as a reserve/household asset it is a real import and the ex-gold number is a construct. A sustained RMB appreciation, a real consumption stimulus, or the Sept 2026 battery consumption tax plus April 2026 export-rebate cancellation biting harder than expected could turn the export volume series before the price series. He has also been directionally early before: the surplus can be huge and still not translate into the terms-of-trade or corporate-margin consequences he implies, because Chinese exporters have been eating the tariff in margin rather than raising prices.

### Michael Pettis, Carnegie Endowment for International Peace
*29 April 2026 (Carnegie, 'Is China's High-Quality Investment Output Economically Viable?'); earlier framing in 'What's New about Involution?', August 2025, and on Monetary Matters, 30 November 2025*

Pettis argues that the quality of what China builds is irrelevant to whether it is overinvesting. High-quality output — world-leading solar, batteries, EVs, grid, rail — is exactly what an overinvestment regime produces; the test is whether the investment generates more value than the resources it consumes. He claims it does not, that the losses are being capitalised rather than expensed, which artificially inflates both GDP and equity valuations, and that manufacturing now shows weak pricing power, rising inventories and excess capacity — the 'involution' crisis. He draws explicit parallels to the USSR in the 1960s, Brazil in the 1970s and Japan in the 1980s, all of which delivered genuine technological breakthroughs shortly before debt crises and prolonged stagnation, and predicts China will eventually be forced into a difficult adjustment in which growth slows and the burden of past excesses is distributed across the economy.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: the clean-tech capex boom is a balance-sheet event, not an earnings event; unrecognised losses are accumulating on bank and local-government balance sheets. Reorganisation: at some point the losses have to be allocated — to households (via financial repression), to local governments, to banks, or to foreign creditors. Whichever it is determines the path. If it lands on households, Chinese consumption stays weak and the export surplus persists; if Beijing finally forces recognition, Chinese clean-tech capacity growth stops abruptly. Third order: the second case is the violent one — a sudden end to Chinese overcapacity would flip solar, battery and EV equipment prices from a two-decade deflation into an inflation, repricing every Western project economics model built on assumed continuing cost declines, and would make surviving non-Chinese capacity suddenly valuable. The market is currently priced for the deflation continuing indefinitely.
- **Testable content:** Whether China's debt-to-GDP continues rising faster than GDP; whether the GDP deflator, which has now fallen for nine consecutive quarters (the longest deflationary run since the 1990s), turns positive; whether reported profits in PV, battery and EV manufacturing recover — polysilicon utilisation fell below 40% in 2025 and battery utilisation is running ~50-55% against ~4,800 GWh planned capacity versus ~1,000 GWh demand, with the anti-involution target being >60% in 2026. Also checkable: GCL Technology's swing from a RMB1.81bn loss to a RMB960mn profit in Q3 2025, and whether that persists or reverses.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Tom Aitken's 10 May 2026 critique is the sharpest available: Pettis leans on the S=I accounting identity to present what are normative and empirical claims as mathematical necessities; the cross-country link between current-account change and manufacturing-share change over 2000-2020 is close to zero; and Pettis has repeatedly predicted a hard adjustment ceiling of 3-4% growth that has not bound. The 'economic complexity' counter — that Chinese manufacturing dominance comes from accumulated capabilities and supply-chain ecosystems, not from financial repression — is the substantive rival explanation and it has better out-of-sample performance on the export-share data.

### Michael Davidson (CSIS) and Sandy Qian (UC San Diego)
*12 March 2026 (CSIS, 'China's Solar Industry Is in Upheaval — The Effects Will Be Global')*

Davidson and Qian argue that the Chinese solar shakeout is a genuine industrial restructuring rather than a cyclical trough, and that its consequences will be felt mainly outside China. Their claim is that the consolidation now under way will leave a smaller number of technologically dominant Chinese firms, and that the cost and IP gap facing everyone else is widening rather than narrowing even as Chinese firms bleed cash. The policy response — anti-involution, output discipline, capacity retirement — is not a return to price stability so much as a transition from price competition to technology competition, which is worse for foreign incumbents, not better.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: bankruptcy and consolidation remove marginal Chinese capacity and put a floor under module prices. Reorganisation: Western manufacturers, who had been arguing that Chinese prices were unsustainably below cost, discover that the post-consolidation Chinese price is still below their cost, because the 50-100% cost gap was structural and not just dumping. Third order: the value in Western solar does not accrue to module manufacturers even under protection — it accrues to whoever controls the parts of the chain China does not want or cannot export: interconnection queue positions, land with grid access, O&M, and the tracker/inverter/BOS layer where trade rules and FEOC compliance create real rents. Protection creates a permanently subsidised, permanently subscale Western cell/wafer segment, which is a policy annuity, not a business.
- **Testable content:** China's 2024 shares: 93.2% polysilicon, 96.6% wafers, 92.3% cells, 86.4% modules. Module prices fell 50% in 2023 and a further 25% in 2024; polysilicon fell from RMB230,000/t to RMB65,000/t in 2023 and ~40% more in 2024, breaking below RMB1/W. Top five firms cut headcount by over 30% in 2024; over 40 smaller firms have gone bankrupt, been acquired or exited. Non-Chinese production costs run 50-100% above Chinese import prices. Chinese firms held 65.0% of global PV patent applications by 2024, up from 13% in 2004. US module capacity passed 50 GW in early 2025 but remains import-dependent for wafers and cells. Near-term checks: whether the SAMR-blocked $7bn polysilicon capacity-retirement fund is revived; whether the ~50% polysilicon price rally from July to October 2025 holds; whether US median module pricing stays near $0.28/W (Anza Q1 2026) or moves on a Section 232 polysilicon action.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Consolidation may simply not happen: SAMR blocked the $7bn fund in early January 2026 on monopoly grounds and has given no signal it will resume, and provincial governments have consistently protected local capacity. Prices may keep falling — one forecast has China module prices at $0.12/W in H2 2026. Conversely, the 66% H1 2026 collapse in Chinese installations could force a much faster and deeper capacity cull than they model, in which case the price floor arrives sooner and harder than the thesis implies and the sequencing of their cascade is wrong.

### Kingsmill Bond, Ember (with Aditya Lolla, Ember)
*Ember report 'The Electrotech Revolution', 16 September 2025; extended interview on Volts, 21 November 2025; Bloomberg framing 23 January 2026; Ember Global Electricity Review 2026, April 2026 (Lolla)*

Bond's claim is that three technology families — renewable supply (solar, wind), electric demand (EVs, heat pumps) and connective technology (batteries, digitalisation) — have converged into a single system, 'electrotech', that wins on physics and economics regardless of climate policy. The core physical argument is that fossil systems waste 40-80% of their energy as heat while electrotech wastes roughly 10%, so the same service needs about a third of the primary energy. The core economic argument is a learning rate of ~20% cost decline per doubling of deployment. His framing is that "a century of evolution is converging into a decade of revolution", and that fossil demand is already peaking sector by sector — industry 2014, buildings 2018, road transport 2019, electricity now. Lolla's version of the same claim, from Ember's April 2026 review, is that we have "firmly entered the era of clean growth".

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: solar and wind take essentially all growth in electricity generation, so fossil generation plateaus and then declines even as total electricity demand grows. Reorganisation: fossil-fuel importers — three-quarters of the world's population — get a security and balance-of-payments reason to electrify that is independent of climate politics, which is why Bond expects emerging markets to leapfrog the US on solar share. Third order: value migrates from molecules to the electron system's bottlenecks — storage (he expects 2-3x more storage capacity than renewable capacity within twenty years, against 400 GWh of storage versus 4,000 GW of solar/wind today), grid connection, and the digital layer that shifts electrons in time. And Chinese solar export tooling of ~1,200 GW versus US LNG export tooling of ~300 bcm is, on his framing, the real energy-export competition.
- **Testable content:** Ember's April 2026 data: solar generation up a record 636 TWh to 2,778 TWh in 2025, +30% year-on-year, the fastest percentage growth in eight years; record 647 GW of global solar additions in 2025; fossil generation fell 38 TWh (-0.2%), only the fifth fall this century; renewables passed one-third of global generation and overtook coal for the first time in a century. Bond's specific falsifiable numbers: battery storage at $100/kWh globally and $60 in China; solar+battery at $70/MWh now and $40 by 2035 versus LNG break-even around $10/MMBtu (~$80/MWh); 90% EV penetration in China by 2030; over 1,000 GW/yr of solar additions by 2030; AI demand reaching ~500 TWh by 2030 and only ~10% of global electricity demand growth to 2030. The single cleanest test is whether global fossil generation falls again in 2026 and 2027 rather than reverting.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The 2025 fossil-generation fall was 0.2% — inside the revision noise, and achieved in a year when Chinese installations were front-loaded by the June 2025 tariff deadline. H1 2026 Chinese installations then fell 66%, which means the 2026 global number may look nothing like 2025 and the 'exponential' may prove to have been a policy pull-forward. His waste-heat efficiency arithmetic is directly contested by Smil (below) on measured rather than rated performance. And the Hormuz shock cuts both ways: it raises the security value of electrification but also raises the cost of capital and the cost of building it.

### Vaclav Smil, University of Manitoba (writing as an invited contributor in Cembalest's 16th Annual Energy Paper)
*3 March 2026 (published in J.P. Morgan's 16th Annual Energy Paper); drawn from Smil's forthcoming 'Global energy 2000-2025: status and outlook'*

Smil's position is that the efficiency case for electrification is real but is being systematically overstated by conflating rated maxima with measured long-term performance, and that a set of fundamental industrial and transport tasks simply cannot be electrified on any visible timescale. His illustrative case is heating: new gas furnaces are at least 90% efficient and the best heat pumps claim a COP of 4.0, but measured seasonal COP in climates like New England is most commonly about 2.0 and lower in colder regions — so prudent conversion implies fully electrified heating needs about half of today's energy input, not a fifth. The efficiency gap between hybrids/PHEVs and full EVs is only about twofold (30-35% versus 60-70%), not the order of magnitude implied. And as of 2025 there are no commercially available large container ships, jetliners or large combines running on electricity, with no imminent prospect of rapid mass adoption. He also notes vehicle replacement cycles have stretched from 3-5 years fifty years ago to 12-14 years now, with US EVs $6,000-10,000 more expensive and Class 8 electric trucks up to $500,000, roughly twice diesel.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: the 'primary energy fallacy' — the claim that an electrified world needs only a fraction of today's primary energy — is wrong by roughly a factor of two, so the residual hydrocarbon demand in 2050 is far larger than transition scenarios assume. Reorganisation: capital keeps flowing to electrification of the easy 60-70% (light vehicles, mild-climate space heat, low-temperature industrial process heat) while the hard residual — heavy freight, aviation, marine, high-temperature process heat, agriculture — stays on liquids and gas and gets no substitute investment at all. Third order: the residual becomes a shrinking-supply, non-shrinking-demand market. Refining, marine fuels, jet fuel and the specialised diesel fleet become scarcity assets precisely because nobody will fund new capacity in a sector everyone has declared dead. Meanwhile the fleet turnover constraint means transport electrification is capped by a 12-14 year replacement cycle regardless of sales share.
- **Testable content:** Measured seasonal COP of installed air-source heat pumps in cold climates versus rated COP — field data exists and is checkable. Atmospheric CO2 annual increase accelerating from 1.5 ppm in the 1990s to 2.4 ppm in 2010-20 to 3.5 ppm in 2024 (NOAA, directly checkable). Absence of commercial electric large container ships, jetliners and combines — a binary, checkable fact. US EV price premium of $6,000-10,000 and Class 8 electric truck cost up to $500,000 versus diesel. Average US vehicle age of 12-14 years (S&P Global Mobility publishes this annually).
- **Where they could be wrong:** Smil has consistently been right on physical constraints and consistently conservative on rates of change in the specific technologies where cost curves rather than physics bind — he underestimated solar and battery cost declines. His container-ship and aviation points are true but may be the wrong constraint: those sectors are a small share of final energy, so being right about them does not falsify the electrotech thesis for the 70% that is electrifiable. The heat-pump COP point is also partly a cold-climate artefact — the global installed base is skewing towards mild climates and China, where measured COP is much closer to rated.

### Michael Cembalest, J.P. Morgan Asset & Wealth Management
*3 March 2026 ('Fighting Words', 16th Annual Eye on the Market Energy Paper)*

Cembalest's central methodological claim is that LCOE for intermittent sources is, in his phrase, the cocktail napkin of energy math, and that only systemwide levelised cost matters. He applies this as a direct rebuttal to Ember's June 2025 finding that solar-plus-storage can serve baseload for only a modest premium over gas. Reproducing Ember's own assumptions for Las Vegas he gets $108/MWh against Ember's $104 — he agrees the arithmetic — but notes this is a ~$60/MWh premium over an all-gas system, requires a full 1 GW gas backup because there are hours with no solar and no stored energy, and curtails roughly 40% of total generation. On data centres he argues the evidence for a power-price effect is circumstantial but that the political backlash is the material fact regardless. On EVs he argues the industry is mostly profitless. On SMRs he argues the burden of proof for economic viability still sits with the industry.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: firm, dispatchable capacity is priced far below its true system value, and intermittent capacity far above. Reorganisation: as load growth forces the issue, markets and regulators re-price capacity, accreditation and ancillary services — PJM's declining capacity accreditation for solar and storage is the leading example, and the push to allocate incremental cost to large loads follows. Third order: value accrues to owners of existing firm capacity and to the ancillary-service and flexibility layer, not to marginal renewable developers; and the political economy of who pays becomes the dominant variable in utility equity returns. His PJM observation is the sharpest third-order point: power is only 10-15% of data-centre lifetime cost, so the opportunity cost of a delay vastly exceeds the cost of paying up for firm power — meaning data centres will absorb almost any price increase rather than relocate, which transfers the entire adjustment onto retail ratepayers and therefore onto politics.
- **Testable content:** His Las Vegas model: 6 GW solar, 17 GWh storage, 1 GW gas, $108/MWh systemwide versus ~$48/MWh all-gas, with ~40% curtailment — fully specified with published assumptions (solar capex $436/kW, storage $165/kWh, gas $1,400/kW, gas $3.50/MMBtu, 7.7% discount rate) and therefore directly reproducible. PJM: 67 GW of existing and planned data-centre capacity; capacity auction payments attributable to data-centre load of $9.3bn against $14.7bn total BRA payments. US average retail power price up from 13 to 15 cents since 2022; 34% of transmission and 37% of distribution investment driven by resilience rather than load. EV operating margins table: only Xiaomi (11%), BYD (6%), Li Auto (5%), Tesla (5%) and Seres (3%) positive; Ford -72%, GM -43%, Toyota -60%, Rivian -67%, Lucid -259%; writedowns of $26bn (Stellantis), $19.5bn (Ford), $6bn (GM).
- **Where they could be wrong:** He concedes he agrees with Ember's arithmetic and is arguing about the word 'affordable' — which means the disagreement is partly semantic and partly about the counterfactual gas price, and a Hormuz-driven gas price shock moves his $60/MWh premium a long way. His model assumes a constant 1 GW hourly load, which he flags as unrealistic and which biases against solar (real load is daytime-peaked in hot climates). His capital cost assumptions are 2025 vintage in a market where battery costs are still falling ~20% per doubling. And on data centres he explicitly says the LBNL study found data-centre share not correlated with electricity prices — he is arguing from selected local studies against a national null result.

### Rob Gramlich, Grid Strategies (testifying to House Energy & Commerce)
*13 May 2026 (US House Energy & Commerce, Energy Subcommittee hearing 'Wires, Rates, and States')*

Gramlich argues that transmission, not generation, is the binding constraint and the cheapest available intervention, and that it is now technology-neutral: five years ago only wind and solar were stuck in interconnection queues, now large loads, gas plants and soon nuclear and geothermal face the same process. His memorable framing is that it is as if there are dozens of Straits of Hormuz around the power grid, where delivery constraints raise prices for consumers. He explicitly argues behind-the-meter generation is inferior on both cost and reliability and will remain a small share, because redundancy on a single customer's site is prohibitively expensive whether the customer uses 1 kW or 1 GW — the plan in most on-site cases is to build the grid connection as soon as possible.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: congestion is rising because transmission capacity is not, so the least-cost generator cannot reach load and consumers pay the difference. Reorganisation: as congestion costs compound and load growth accelerates, the political economy shifts — federal-state jurisdiction over siting, cost allocation and interregional transfer becomes the fight, and utility incentives (which he says are not aligned with high-net-benefit capacity expansion) get restructured. Third order: because most near-term capacity gains come from reconductoring and upgrades within existing rights of way rather than greenfield lines (about 90% of MISO's first LRTP tranche), the value accrues to high-performance conductor manufacturers, grid-enhancing technology vendors, transformer and HVDC supply chains and engineering capacity — not to the developers of the marquee new lines. Copper and aluminium demand follows the conductor, not the megawatt.
- **Testable content:** National congestion costs: $6-8bn/yr before 2021, above $10bn since, $12bn in 2024, with preliminary 2025 higher. Well-planned high-capacity transmission could save residential consumers $6.3-10.4bn/yr net of cost. NERC's interregional transfer study: 35 GW of additional capacity needed, ~40% above current, costing $1.8bn for $7.8-10.6bn of benefits, a 4.3-5.8x benefit-cost ratio; 1 GW more interregional capacity would have saved customers across 12 regions $466mn over five days during Winter Storm Elliott. Grid Strategies' aggregation of utility forecasts: 166 GW / 30% more capacity needed by 2030. China built more than 8,200 miles of UHV lines in the 2020s versus 375 miles in the US, with 15 more UHVDC lines planned by 2030 (~10,000 miles) taking west-to-east transfer capacity from 340 GW in 2026 to 420 GW by 2030. Close to 150,000 miles of high-performance conductor installed in India, Europe and China versus under 10% of that in the US. Transmission availability above 99.85%. Independently checkable: US data centre developers had announced nearly 90 GW of behind-the-meter gas as of April 2026 but only ~2 GW operating (almost all xAI Memphis) plus ~1 GW under construction — which is direct evidence for his behind-the-meter scepticism.
- **Where they could be wrong:** He is a paid advocate — his client list includes transmission developers and clean energy associations — and the benefit-cost studies he cites are largely from that ecosystem. The behind-the-meter argument may be right on steady-state economics and wrong on the transition: if interconnection takes seven years and a data centre's opportunity cost of delay is enormous, 90 GW of announced BTM gas may substantially convert even if it is economically inferior, and the gas turbine backlog (GE Vernova at $176bn backlog Q2 2026, no new units before late 2028; the big three booked to 2028-2030) is the actual binding constraint rather than the grid. Cembalest's point that behind-the-meter cost is only 10-15% of data-centre lifetime cost cuts against Gramlich's cost-inferiority argument.

### Robert Friedland, Ivanhoe Mines
*January 2026, Future Minerals Forum, Riyadh ('Dawn of the Copper Age'); reiterated at the USC Marshall Energy Business Summit; reported 26 February 2026*

Friedland's claim is that copper is entering a structural shortage that price alone must resolve, and that the copper price must roughly double to incentivise the required supply. His arithmetic: the world needs to mine another ~700 million tonnes over the next eighteen years — equal to everything mined in the previous ten thousand years of human civilisation — requiring six new tier-one mines every year through 2050, and 40% of that new production will be consumed by grid upgrades, electrification and data centres alone. He frames the difficulty as physical rather than financial: since 1900 the energy needed per unit of copper is up sixteenfold and water requirements have doubled. Without this he warns of a train wreck.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: demand from grid, electrification and AI compounds while ore grades fall and permitting timelines lengthen, so the marginal tonne requires a structurally higher price. Reorganisation: at a doubled price, substitution and scrap recovery activate — aluminium in transmission and busbars, higher recycling rates — and the marginal consumer of copper (construction, plumbing, low-value wiring) is priced out to make room for the inelastic consumer (grid, data centre, EV). Third order: the value does not accrue to copper miners generally but to the holders of permitted, high-grade, low-strip, water-secure, jurisdictionally safe assets, which is a very short list; and it accrues to whoever owns the substitution technologies and the scrap aggregation networks. A doubled copper price is also a direct tax on the electrification thesis — it raises the cost of exactly the grid buildout Gramlich and Bond require.
- **Testable content:** Copper reached ~$13,400/t nominal, an all-time high, at the time of his remarks — checkable against LME. His claim that AI data centres will consume as much electricity as Japan by end-2026. Microsoft's Chicago data centre requiring 2 million kg of copper. Six tier-one mines per year through 2050 — checkable against the actual project pipeline and FID announcements. 3 million miles of new US transmission to electrify America. Independent market checks: ICSG abandoned its 2025 surplus call and now forecasts a 150,000 t deficit for 2026; J.P. Morgan forecasts 330,000 t. Aluminium's 2026 balance, by contrast, flipped to a 112,000 t surplus from a 52,200 t deficit in the prior poll — the substitution channel is live.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Friedland is talking his book — he runs a copper company and has been forecasting the same shortage for over a decade while the market repeatedly delivered surpluses. The counter-argument, made by sell-side analysts through 2026, is that mining companies have promoted the shortage narrative so effectively that the market has priced future shortages prematurely, and that much of the metal that appears to be consumed is sitting in storage leveraged against the CME forward curve rather than being used: belief and fundamentals are not the same thing. Goldman's own bullish thesis explicitly rests on substitution to aluminium and scrap recovery being the release valves, which caps the price. And a doubled copper price is self-defeating — it kills the grid demand it is predicated on.

### Gracelin Baskaran, CSIS Critical Minerals Security Program
*October 2025 CSIS analysis of the new restrictions; Senate Finance Committee testimony 14 May 2025; interview in The Wire China 9 November 2025; CSIS 'Rare Earth Export Restrictions One Year Later', 2026*

Baskaran's argument is that China's October 2025 rare earth rules were a categorical escalation rather than an incremental one, because they adopted the structure of US semiconductor controls: a foreign direct product rule requiring licences for any product containing more than 0.1% by value of Chinese rare earths, or manufactured using Chinese rare earth equipment or magnets, with a presumption of denial for military end-uses. On her framing these are China's most consequential measures to date targeting the defence sector, and the design is intended to prevent direct or indirect contribution of Chinese-origin rare earths to foreign defence supply chains. The implication she draws is that the chokepoint is not mining but separation and magnet-making expertise, which is not replicable on a policy timescale.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: rare earth access becomes a licensing relationship with Beijing rather than a market, renewed in six-month increments, and therefore a permanent negotiating instrument. Reorganisation: because the private market cannot underwrite the capex against a counterparty that can crash the price at will, Western governments must become the price-setter of last resort — which is exactly what the DoD's ten-year MP Materials offtake at a $110/kg NdPr floor, structured as a contract-for-difference, does. Third order: the state price floor becomes the template and spreads to other minerals, which means the real asset is not the mine but the government contract; and it creates a two-tier global price — a subsidised Western price and a Chinese price — with all the arbitrage and smuggling consequences that implies. Downstream, the binding scarcity is heavy rare earths (dysprosium, terbium) and the industries that discover they are exposed are not just defence but robotics, medical imaging, aerospace and automotive.
- **Testable content:** The 0.1% value threshold and foreign-direct-product structure of the October 2025 rules — checkable in MOFCOM texts. The late-October 2025 pause of implementation (except military-linked cases) in exchange for the US fentanyl tariff cut from 20% to 10% and chip-control easing, with only six-month licences issued. China's graphite control suspension for the US running to 27 November 2026 — a hard, dated expiry to watch. The MP/DoD $110/kg NdPr floor and the fact that prices rallied above it by February 2026, meaning no subsidy was payable. Lynas produced just 8 tonnes of dysprosium and terbium combined in Q1 2026 against global demand in the thousands of tonnes. McKinsey, CRU and Benchmark all project non-Chinese producers meeting under 20% of dysprosium/terbium demand by 2035. Ultra-pure separation can require more than 1,000 chemical stages.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Baskaran herself has also argued the clampdown backfired — accelerating Western investment and diversification faster than Beijing intended — which sits uneasily with the chokepoint-is-permanent framing; the two claims need to be held separately. Neha Mukherjee of Benchmark Mineral Intelligence argued in February 2026 that the price rally was driven by firm magnet demand and deliberate Chinese supply management and was likely temporary with a correction expected by end-March, which if right means the price floor is load-bearing again and the state subsidy resumes. The deeper risk to the thesis is that magnet-free motor designs and heavy-rare-earth-reduced magnet chemistries advance faster than the substitution literature assumes, which would defuse the chokepoint from the demand side rather than the supply side.

### Chris Wright, US Secretary of Energy, and the DOE Reactor Pilot Program
*1 July 2026 (DOE announcement of third advanced reactor criticality); Antares Mark-0 and Valar Atomics Ward 250 critical early July 2026, Deployable Energy Unity critical 30 June 2026; underlying executive order May 2025*

Wright's claim is that advanced nuclear has moved from a licensing problem to an execution reality, and that the DOE pathway — bypassing the conventional NRC route for demonstration reactors on national laboratory and private sites — has compressed timelines by years. His stated framing is that America's nuclear renaissance is underway because of the administration's goals. The delivery claim is concrete: Trump's May 2025 executive order directed DOE to have three advanced reactors achieve criticality by 4 July 2026, and four did, making the US the first nation to achieve criticality in three distinct advanced microreactor designs within a single month.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: regulatory pathway, not physics or capital, was the binding constraint on advanced nuclear, and it has been removed for demonstration units. Reorganisation: if demonstration units can be built in months rather than a decade, the learning curve can actually start, and the private capital that has been waiting for a licensed-and-operating reference plant unlocks — the sector raised $1.3bn in 2025 and two SMR indices more than doubled over the year even after the 2025 correction. DOE put $800mn of cost-shared funding into TVA's BWRX-300 in Tennessee and Holtec's two 300 MW units at Palisades in December 2025. Third order: if it works, the value accrues to the fuel cycle (HALEU enrichment, which is the actual physical bottleneck), to the forging and pressure-vessel supply chain, and to sited-and-permitted brownfield nuclear land — not to the reactor designers, who are the most crowded part of the trade. If it does not work, the capital destruction is concentrated in exactly those designer equities.
- **Testable content:** Four criticalities by 4 July 2026 — a binary, verified fact. Valar's Ward250 generating 100 kW. Whether any of these units produce grid-connected commercial electricity at a stated price, and when. Whether TVA's BWRX-300 and Holtec's Palisades units hit their cost and schedule. Whether the $1.3bn/yr venture funding rate persists into 2026-27. Whether the non-binding character of most SMR offtake contracts — Oklo/Switch and prior NuScale agreements allowed buyers to exit on final project cost — converts into binding PPAs. Comparison point: Vistra's ~2,600 MW of long-term nuclear PPAs with Meta across three PJM plants, which is existing large LWR capacity, not SMR.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The sceptical case is strong and specific. Allison Macfarlane, former NRC chair, with Kim (Progress in Nuclear Energy, August 2025) reviewed four SMR types and estimated levelized costs of $200-400/MWh, higher still for Xe-100, concluding that capital and O&M may exceed those of standard large LWRs because of diseconomies of scale, and that learning, modularisation and shorter construction may not reduce costs significantly. TVA's own 2025 IRP assumed a first-of-a-kind SMR capital cost of $18,000/kW — above industry averages, on TVA's own experience at Clinch River — implying $196/MWh, falling to perhaps $150/MWh on a 30% future capital cost decline. Cembalest reports client studies putting SMR cost at 2.5-5.5x grid levels, and notes China's first SMR, a 200 MW HTGR, ran at a 30% energy availability factor in 2024 against 87% for the US LWR fleet, while CNNC conceded in 2021 that its ACP100 costs 2x per kW and ~50% more per kWh than its large plants. David Schlissel at IEEFA makes the same case from the project record. Cembalest's structural point is the sharpest: SMRs are a reverse learning curve — the industry moved from 100 MW to 1 GW units by 1980 precisely because fixed capital costs rewarded scale. A 100 kW microreactor achieving criticality proves nothing about $/MWh.

### Laura Alfaro (Harvard Business School) and Davin Chor (Dartmouth)
*22 January 2026 (VoxEU, 'An update on the great reallocation in US supply chain trade'; NBER Working Paper 34490)*

Alfaro and Chor argue, from detailed US import data covering 2013-2025, that the Great Reallocation is real in composition but much weaker in substance than the reshoring narrative claims. Direct imports from China have fallen — to roughly the level of 2001, when China joined the WTO — but total US merchandise imports have risen, and diversification beyond the top trading partners has been limited. Tariffs caused both cessation of some product flows and reduced volumes among continuing ones. Critically, their 2025 data show a further acceleration of reallocation toward partners facing lower announced tariff rates — that is, the reallocation is being driven by tariff arbitrage rather than by comparative advantage or by genuine relocation of productive capacity.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: firms are re-routing rather than re-shoring, and the routing is optimised against announced tariff schedules. Reorganisation: this makes the reallocation unstable — it re-optimises every time the tariff schedule changes, so no one builds the fixed capital that reshoring requires, because the arbitrage that justifies a Vietnam or Mexico plant can be closed by a rules-of-origin change overnight. Setser's observation that the trajectory is for the US to tariff everyone else as much as China is the terminal state of this game. Third order: the value accrues to logistics and customs-engineering capability, to bonded-warehouse and FTZ operators, and to the small set of intermediate-goods producers whose inputs are genuinely non-substitutable — not to US greenfield manufacturing, which is not being built. The corollary is that tariffs raise US manufacturers' input costs while failing to build domestic capacity, which is deindustrialising at the margin.
- **Testable content:** US imports from China back near 2001 levels while total merchandise imports rose — checkable in Census trade data at HS level. The correlation between a partner's announced tariff rate and its gain in US import share in 2025 — the central regression, reproducible. The reindustrialisation counter-evidence is unusually clean: US manufacturing construction spending at ~$174.8bn SAAR in May 2026, down ~21.9% year-on-year; the inflation-adjusted factory construction index peaked at 12,070 in December 2023; electronics and semiconductor fab construction down 44% from its mid-2024 peak; non-electronics manufacturing construction up only 5.6% from the start of the tariffs through mid-2026. AIA-surveyed economists expect further decline through 2026-27.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Their data end in 2025 and the 2026 tariff regime is materially different; the sunk-cost argument Setser makes — that once firms concluded tariffs were permanent under both administrations they stopped waiting and started incurring relocation costs — implies the real relocation shows up with a multi-year lag that their sample cannot see. Construction spending is also the wrong indicator if the shift is toward expansion and automation within existing footprints rather than greenfield, which is what Interact Analysis argues. And CHIPS-driven fab spending falling from a policy-induced peak is not evidence about tariff-driven reshoring in other sectors.

### Adam Tooze, Columbia University (with a data check from Hannah Ritchie, Our World in Data)
*2 April 2026 (Chartbook 439, 'Electrostates v. petrostates'); related Chartbooks 409 and 414; new book manuscript on the energy transition completed*

Tooze argues that the useful distinction is not producer versus consumer of fossil fuels but electrostate versus petrostate — an electrostate being an economy drawing a large and rising share of final energy consumption as electricity, which any economy can choose regardless of its hydrocarbon endowment. On his account China is the archetypal electrostate: electricity at ~30% of final energy consumption and rising steeply, against ~22% in the US and Europe and flat, plus dominance of EV manufacture and electrified high-speed rail. He argues China's logic is compelling because it produces 4 mb/d and consumes 15-16 mb/d. On the US he makes the sharper claim: it is not really a petrostate — oil and gas are only 7-8% of GDP — so a closed-petrostate outcome would be a political choice rather than an economic necessity. He expects China and the EU to conform ever more comprehensively to the electrostate model.

- **Implied cascade:** Primary: electrification share of final energy becomes the metric of industrial capability, displacing primary energy consumption and even GDP as the relevant measure. Reorganisation: the strategic chokepoints move from straits and pipelines to the electron system — inverters, grid control software, transformers, HVDC, and the firmware inside imported hardware (Tooze has flagged the May 2025 reports of undeclared communications equipment in Chinese-supplied inverters). Third order: if the chokepoint is the grid's digital control layer rather than the fuel, then national security spending reorients toward grid sovereignty, and the value accrues to domestically-controlled inverter, SCADA, protection-relay and transformer manufacture — a small, unglamorous, capacity-constrained industry currently priced as a utility supplier. The Hormuz closure of March 2026 is the natural experiment: it should accelerate every electrostate's electrification and impose the cost entirely on the import-dependent non-electrostates.
- **Testable content:** Electricity as a share of final energy consumption: ~30% China versus ~22% US and EU, and the trend in each — IEA and national energy balances publish this. Oil and gas at 7-8% of US GDP. China producing ~4 mb/d against 15-16 mb/d consumption. Hannah Ritchie's 5 March 2026 sectoral check is the important refinement: on a per-capita basis China now consumes slightly more industrial electricity than the US and on a very different trajectory, while Americans use more than four times as much electricity at home — meaning the US per-capita aggregate advantage is entirely residential and tells you nothing about industrial capability. Business electricity prices: ~11.6 c/kWh in China (April 2026) versus ~14.1 c/kWh in the US (2026 estimate). Wind and solar above 50% of Chinese installed capacity by early 2026. Checkable market consequence: whether Chinese industrial electricity prices fall relative to US and EU prices through 2026-27, and whether energy-intensive production (aluminium, silicon, chlor-alkali, data centres) relocates accordingly.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Electricity share of final energy is a composition metric, not a capability metric — an economy can raise it by deindustrialising into services, and China's number is partly a function of an unusually electricity-intensive industrial mix rather than a superior energy system. The inverter-firmware chokepoint claim rests on reporting that has not been fully substantiated publicly. And the Draghi diagnosis complicates the EU half of the prediction: EU companies still face electricity prices two to three times US levels and gas four to five times, and only 11.2% of the Draghi report's 383 recommendations had been fully implemented a year after publication — an economy cannot become an electrostate on electricity it cannot afford, so the EU may electrify by shrinking its industry rather than by growing its grid.

## Finance, macro and market structure

### Viral Acharya, Nicola Cetorelli and Bruce Tuckman (NYU Stern / NY Fed)
*Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report no. 1176, January 2026; also NBER Working Paper 34679. Builds on Acharya et al., 'Shadow Always Touches the Feet: Implications of Bank Credit Lines to Non-Bank Financial Intermediaries' (2025).*

Risk did not leave the banking system; it changed form. The growth of non-bank financial intermediaries relative to banks is not disintermediation but a two-way transformation: banks fund NBFIs through senior loans, warehouse lines and revolving credit facilities, and NBFIs use that funding to acquire junior credit claims. Banks therefore shed credit risk but retain liquidity risk and contingent exposure; NBFIs absorb credit risk but depend on bank liquidity. They show empirically that shocks to NBFIs spill back onto the banks that extend them credit lines, and that the whole configuration is consistent with arbitraging post-GFC bank capital rules while still harnessing the deposit franchise and the public safety net.

- **Implied cascade:** Post-GFC capital and liquidity rules → banks reprice direct lending and instead lend senior/secured to NBFIs → credit risk sits in funds, liquidity risk stays with banks → in stress, NBFI drawdowns on committed lines hit bank balance sheets simultaneously with market-wide funding tightening → the safety net is implicitly extended to the non-bank sector without the corresponding supervision → third-order: measured bank capital ratios understate true systemic exposure, and the Fed's crisis toolkit has to reach entities it does not supervise.
- **Testable content:** Directly checkable in the Fed's Y-14 and FR 2052a data and the new NFI (non-depository financial institution) lending line in H.8: growth in bank committed-but-undrawn facilities to NBFIs; drawdown rates on those lines during stress episodes; correlation of NBFI shocks with lending-bank equity returns and CDS. Also checkable: whether risk-weighted assets fall while committed exposure rises for the same bank.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The FSB's May 2026 report found banks' direct lending to private credit funds was under 0.5% of total bank assets across five reporting jurisdictions — if that is the right denominator, the spillover channel is real but small. Their evidence is largely on credit lines, which are secured, senior and heavily over-collateralised; a shock large enough to impair them may be one where the private-credit channel is not the marginal cause. The identification also relies on stress episodes (2020, 2023) whose generalisability to a slow-burn credit cycle is unproven.

### Michelle Bowman, Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision
*Speech, 'The Migration of Corporate Lending', 8 May 2026. Related: Basel III/bank capital speech 12 March 2026; testimony 4 June 2026; modernising financial regulation speech 13 July 2026.*

The migration of corporate lending out of banks was caused by regulation, is measurable, and is reversible by changing capital rules rather than by supervising the recipients. Her specific mechanism is a perverse relative price inside the capital framework: a bank gets more favourable treatment for lending to a private credit fund than for lending directly to a creditworthy corporate. That relative price, not any comparative advantage of private credit, explains why the bank share of corporate lending fell from roughly 48% in 2015 to 29% in 2025. Her remedy is to cut the risk weight on investment-grade corporate loans from 100% to 65% and to build reporting that tracks where bank money to non-banks actually ends up.

- **Implied cascade:** Basel-era risk weights price direct corporate lending punitively → banks intermediate at one remove, lending to the funds instead of the borrowers → credit risk migrates to vehicles with less disclosure and no supervisory reporting → opacity accumulates between the bank's exposure and the ultimate borrower → third-order: if the risk weight is cut, banks recapture spread-rich IG lending, direct lenders lose their cost-of-capital edge at the top of the credit quality range, BDC and direct-lending spreads compress from the 400bp+ she cites toward bank pricing nearer 200bp, and the marginal private credit fund is forced further down the quality curve to keep its return target.
- **Testable content:** Every load-bearing number is a published series: bank share of US non-financial corporate credit (Z.1 flow of funds); the private credit market at ~$1.4trn and ~10% of total US corporate borrowing; BDC yields versus large-bank corporate loan pricing. The forecast is falsifiable: if the IG risk weight is cut to 65%, does bank C&I lending share actually rise and do BDC spreads compress within 4-8 quarters? Also checkable: whether the new bank-to-NBFI reporting regime actually reduces the 'opacities' she names.
- **Where they could be wrong:** She may be reasoning backwards from a deregulatory prior. Migration also has non-regulatory causes — speed of execution, covenant flexibility, unitranche structures, sponsor relationships — that a risk-weight change will not reverse. And if she is right that regulation pushed the risk out, cutting capital requirements to pull it back means re-concentrating credit risk in leveraged, deposit-funded, safety-net-backed institutions at a point in the cycle when Fitch already has private credit defaults at a record. Acharya's framework implies the bank is already exposed; Bowman's remedy adds direct exposure on top of contingent exposure.

### Jamie Dimon (JPMorgan), extended by Mohamed El-Erian
*Dimon: JPMorgan Q3 earnings call, October 2025. El-Erian: March 2026. Counter-observation that the cascade did not arrive: reporting from May 2026 noting the cockroaches had not materialised.*

Dimon's claim is about inference, not arithmetic: isolated credit accidents are evidence about the underwriting distribution, not idiosyncratic noise. After the First Brands and Tricolor failures he said that when you see one cockroach, "there are probably more", and that everyone should be forewarned. El-Erian generalised the mechanism in March 2026, arguing that the specific bugs now crawling out are valuation gaps, liquidity strains, poor underwriting and outright fraud — i.e. that the failures are correlated because they share an origin in a period of loose origination standards and self-marked assets, not because of a common macro shock.

- **Implied cascade:** A multi-year origination boom with weak covenants and self-reported marks → early idiosyncratic failures reveal the underwriting distribution rather than its tail → PIK toggles let distress compound invisibly rather than default → marks lag reality, so NAVs are stale → retail semi-liquid vehicles face redemption demand against stale NAVs → gates → gating becomes the signal that repriced the whole asset class → third-order: the funding cost of every direct lender rises, sponsors cannot refinance, and the marginal middle-market borrower loses access to credit entirely.
- **Testable content:** Fitch's US private credit default rate: 5.6% (Dec 2025) → 5.8% TTM through Jan 2026 → a record 6.0% in April 2026; Morgan Stanley's warning of ~8% in 2026 is a dated, falsifiable forecast. PIK income as a share of BDC total investment income is disclosed quarterly and is the cleanest proxy for invisible compounding. Blackstone's BCRED: ~7.9% of shares (~$3.8bn) requested for repurchase against a 5% cap earlier in 2026; ~10% requested in Q2 2026 with the 5% cap held (reported 4 June 2026); the prior quarter was cleared at 7% only with ~$400m of Blackstone and employee money. Amend-and-extend and distressed-exchange volumes are tracked by Fitch and LSTA.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The base rate for 'one cockroach implies an infestation' is poor: First Brands and Tricolor were frauds and receivables-financing failures, not representative direct-lending credits, and by mid-2026 the systemic sequel had not arrived. Dimon also has an obvious commercial interest in a narrative that returns lending to banks. A 6% default rate on a book yielding SOFR+500 with 40-60% recoveries is a bad vintage, not a solvency event. The gates at BCRED can equally be read as the structure working as designed.

### Marc Rowan and Apollo (transparency programme set out by Eric Needleman, Head of Apollo Capital Solutions)
*Rowan on redemptions, 16 April 2026 (CNBC). Apollo transparency piece by Eric Needleman, published 29 June 2026 (dated May 2026 in Apollo's insights index). Rowan's convergence framing runs through 2025-2026. Apollo AUM $1.03trn at 31 March 2026, $115bn Q1 inflows, $300bn LTM.*

The systemic-risk framing mistakes an infrastructure gap for a risk. Rowan's position is that most private credit is investment grade and competes with public investment grade, that it has outperformed on both return and safety, and that the real difference between public and private is not risk but the absence of pricing and trading plumbing. Apollo's answer is to build the plumbing: standardised asset-level data (ICE Private Credit Intelligence, launched March 2026), a dedicated secondary desk, and daily pricing on the whole book. On redemptions Rowan was blunt — a manager who cannot meet a 5% quarterly redemption is, in his words, an "idiot", i.e. the gate is a manager-quality signal, not a structural flaw.

- **Implied cascade:** Standardised identifiers and asset-level data → observable secondary prices → daily NAVs → the stale-mark and liquidity-mismatch critiques lose their factual basis → semi-liquid and retail wrappers become defensible → private credit is accepted as a fixed-income substitute rather than an alternative → third-order: the illiquidity premium compresses toward zero, the excess return that justified the asset class disappears, capital that entered for yield leaves, and the industry's economics converge on those of a public credit manager. Rowan's own convergence thesis implies the destruction of his own margin: he has asked what the difference between public and private will be once competition arrives.
- **Testable content:** Hard, dated commitments: Apollo says 100% of its $830bn+ credit book will carry a daily price by 30 September 2026 — verifiable on that date. Its secondary desk claims $13bn+ of volume with 100+ counterparties in 18 months. State Street's PRIV ETF held ~$850m as of June 2026 — check whether it scaled or stalled. The falsifiable prediction is convergence itself: do private IG spreads converge on public IG, and does realised loss experience match public IG through this default cycle? Supporting the containment case: the FSB found bank direct lending to private credit funds below 0.5% of total bank assets across five jurisdictions.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Daily pricing is not daily liquidity — a model-derived daily mark on an unsold asset does not create a bid, and in stress the daily price may simply become a daily source of panic. Rowan is the single most conflicted possible source on whether private credit is safe; Apollo's economics depend on the answer. The 'most of it is investment grade' claim rests on a definition of private credit that includes asset-backed and insurance-linked origination, which is not what critics are worried about. And a manager's ability to meet a 5% gate says nothing about the system's ability to meet simultaneous redemptions across all managers.

### US insurance regulators (NAIC) and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, with the IMF on offshore reinsurance contagion
*NAIC identified life reinsurer investment practices as a 2026 strategic priority; Bessent convened with NAIC leaders (reported May 2026). Bermuda life reinsurance sector: ~$1.52trn of assets covering ~90m policyholders (BILTIR, 2026). Estimates of ~$2trn of US life liabilities moved into offshore and captive structures. IMF has flagged offshore reinsurance contagion.*

The terminal holder of private credit risk is not a fund or a bank but the US life insurance sector, and a large share of the liability has been moved offshore where state regulators cannot see the asset side. The structure — an asset manager, a US life insurer writing annuities, and an affiliated Bermuda or captive reinsurer — lets the same group originate the asset, sell the liability, and reinsure the downside, with capital charges set under a different regime from the one supervising the policyholder promise. The NAIC has made life reinsurer investment practices a 2026 strategic priority; the concern is that retirees' annuity claims are backed by opaque, affiliate-originated, illiquid credit.

- **Implied cascade:** Asset managers acquire or affiliate with life insurers → annuity liabilities are matched with affiliate-originated private credit at favourable capital treatment → the liability is reinsured to Bermuda or a captive, where asset disclosure and capital rules differ → risk leaves the perimeter of the state regulator who guarantees the policyholder → third-order: a private credit default cycle transmits not to bank capital but to annuity guarantee funds and to retirees, arriving as a consumer-protection and political crisis rather than a financial-stability one — which means the tools mobilised are legislative and state-level, not Fed liquidity, and are slower.
- **Testable content:** Statutory filings make much of this checkable: Schedule D asset composition and affiliate-origination share for the largest asset-manager-owned life insurers; the volume and counterparty of offshore reinsurance cessions in Schedule S; NAIC risk-based capital ratios before and after cession; the share of annuity reserves ceded to Bermuda. Also checkable: whether Bermuda's BMA capital regime and the NAIC's 2026 workstream actually change asset-eligibility or capital charges, and by when. Watch for any NAIC move on affiliate-transaction limits or a bespoke capital charge for privately-rated securities.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Life liabilities are long-dated and largely non-runnable, which is exactly the balance sheet that should hold illiquid credit — the maturity match is genuinely good, and this may be the most rational allocation of the risk in the system. Bermuda's regime has tightened materially and BILTIR argues stronger rules have driven the sector's growth rather than arbitrage. The $2trn figure aggregates structures with very different risk profiles. And annuity surrender is penalty-protected, so the run dynamic that makes this systemic may simply not exist.

### Bank for International Settlements (Annual Economic Report 2026, chapter on high public debt and shifting financial markets)
*BIS Annual Economic Report 2026, published 28 June 2026.*

The sovereign-financial nexus has moved from banks to non-banks, and the interaction of the two conditions — high public debt and a large NBFI footprint — is what produces market dysfunction, not either alone. High debt means enormous gross issuance intermediated by dealers with finite balance sheets; leveraged hedge funds have become the marginal intermediaries in sovereign bond markets, funded in repo at near-zero haircuts. That configuration makes the Treasury market's liquidity conditional on the funding conditions of a handful of levered funds and a handful of dealers. There is two-way feedback: fiscal surprises hit harder when markets are illiquid, and adverse fiscal news degrades liquidity further. High debt also blunts monetary policy — high-debt economies show a weaker disinflationary response to tightening.

- **Implied cascade:** Large deficits → heavy issuance absorbed by leveraged relative-value funds rather than by real-money buyers → sovereign market liquidity becomes a function of repo haircuts and dealer capacity → a margin or haircut shift forces deleveraging → fire sales amplify yield spikes far beyond the fiscal news itself → third-order: the central bank is forced into a market-functioning intervention that is indistinguishable from fiscal accommodation, which is how fiscal dominance actually arrives — not by decree but as the residual of a liquidity backstop. Non-US investors hedging dollar exposure in short-dated FX swaps convert currency risk into rollover risk, exporting the same fragility across borders.
- **Testable content:** Unusually quantified and therefore unusually falsifiable: probability of a Treasury-market stress event ~3.8% when debt/GDP is high versus ~0.3% when low (a ~10x conditional difference); hedge fund US Treasury exposures more than doubled since 2022; ~70% of bilateral USD repo with hedge funds carries zero haircut; FX swaps outstanding ~$130trn with ~75% maturing inside one year; in the 2022 UK LDI episode roughly half the post-announcement price move was attributed to fire sales rather than fundamentals; euro-area economies at ~120% debt/GDP show a weaker disinflationary response to tightening than those at ~60%. Corroborating: OFR sponsored-repo volumes and Fed data putting aggregate basis-trade positions near $830bn in September 2025, with hedge fund UST holdings and repo borrowing both at records. Each is a monitorable series.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The BIS has warned about the basis trade through several episodes in which it did not break, and the April 2025 stress resolved without a Fed facility. The conditional-probability figures come from a small sample of stress events across heterogeneous jurisdictions and may not identify a causal effect of debt as opposed to a common driver. The zero-haircut statistic describes bilateral repo, but central clearing of Treasury repo is being phased in, which changes the mechanism materially. And the claim that market-functioning intervention equals fiscal dominance elides a real distinction between a temporary liquidity backstop and a sustained transfer.

### Ray Dalio (Bridgewater)
*Big Debt Cycle framing developed through 2025 (October 2025 interviews warning of 'very, very dark times'); the Phase 5 and 'crisis comes from governments not banks' framing restated through early 2026, including a Fortune interview published 14 March 2026.*

The next debt crisis will come from governments, not banks, and the US has entered what he calls Phase 5 of the Big Debt Cycle — the stage where debt service can only be sustained by devaluing the currency. His mechanism is a supply-demand identity rather than a threshold: when the stock of outstanding debt is large, the deficit requires more bond sales than there is free demand for, so either the price of debt falls, the currency falls, or the central bank buys. Because the first two are politically intolerable, the third is the default outcome. He frames the trigger as bond vigilantes ceasing to buy, at which point restructuring or monetisation becomes forced rather than chosen.

- **Implied cascade:** Debt grows faster than income for long enough → interest expense crowds out primary spending → issuance exceeds private demand at prevailing yields → term premium rises and the currency weakens → the central bank is pressured to absorb supply → real returns on nominal bonds turn negative → third-order: capital rotates out of sovereign debt into hard and non-sovereign stores of value, the domestic savings base for government funding erodes further, and the process becomes self-reinforcing; he pairs the financial cycle with an internal-political-order and external-order cycle, arguing the three reinforce each other.
- **Testable content:** Checkable against series rather than sentiment: net interest outlays as a share of federal revenue and of GDP; CBO's projection of debt from ~100% of GDP in 2026 to over 150% within three decades; the 2026 deficit near $1.9trn; primary dealer take-down and bid-to-cover at long-end auctions; the 10y and 30y term premium (ACM/KW estimates); Fed balance sheet direction — Governor Miran argued in a 26 March 2026 speech for continued shrinkage, which is the opposite of monetisation and directly tests Dalio's claim; the foreign official share of Treasuries. Gold's rise to 27% of global official reserves versus Treasuries at 22% by end-2025 is the clearest market corroboration.
- **Where they could be wrong:** This is closer to a directional call than a mechanism claim, and the timing has been indefinite for years while Treasuries have repeatedly rallied in risk-off episodes. The identity ignores price: there is always demand at some yield, and higher term premium is an equilibrating mechanism, not a failure. Furman and Summers argue that real interest expense as a share of GDP — not debt/GDP — is the correct burden measure, and on that measure the position is closer to historical norms. Dalio also has an obvious book in gold and diversification away from Treasuries.

### Russell Napier
*Thesis developed from 2023 onward; restated and extended through 2025 and into 2026, including a podcast appearance in late 2025 framed around financial repression being back and investors being unprepared.*

The sovereign debt problem will not be resolved by a crisis but by financial repression, and the mechanism is already operating outside the central bank. His claim is that governments have taken covert control of money creation by steering commercial bank lending — through credit guarantees, guidance and directed lending to favoured sectors — which means the quantity of money is now set by fiscal authorities rather than by the policy rate. Central banks, in his framing, have become largely irrelevant and are doing the bidding of indebted sovereigns. The target is not price stability but a sustained gap between nominal GDP growth and the interest rate: inflation engineered at roughly 4-6%, nominal growth of 6-8%, and interest rates held below both, which liquidates the debt over a decade or two without a default.

- **Implied cascade:** State credit guarantees direct bank lending → broad money grows independently of central bank policy → structurally higher nominal growth and inflation → governments must then ensure the debt is absorbed at below-market yields → savers are compelled to hold sovereign bonds via regulation of pensions, insurers and banks → capital controls, formal or informal, become necessary to prevent leakage → third-order: a repatriation of capital and the end of the global savings pool that financed US deficits; cross-border allocation reverses, home bias returns by policy, and the marginal buyer of every country's debt becomes its own regulated savings institutions. For asset owners this is a transfer from creditors to debtors executed slowly and legally.
- **Testable content:** Directly measurable: broad money (M2/M3) growth versus central bank balance sheet direction — his claim requires the two to decouple; the stock of state-guaranteed bank lending in the EU, UK and US; the gap between nominal GDP growth and the average interest cost of government debt (the r-minus-g wedge) sustained over multiple years; realised inflation persistently in the 4-6% band rather than at 2%; regulatory changes forcing domestic institutions toward sovereign bonds (Solvency II reform, pension mandates, LCR treatment); and cross-border portfolio flow reversal in TIC and BoP data. Napier's inflation band is specific enough to be wrong.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Inflation in the major economies has not settled in a 4-6% band, and central banks have shown more willingness to tighten against fiscal preference than the thesis allows. Directed-lending guarantees are large in Europe but far less established in the US, where the mechanism would need to work through very different channels. Krugman, working the same problem from the other side (7 September 2025), locates fiscal dominance in overt political capture of the Fed — personnel changes and demands for a 300bp cut — rather than in covert control of bank credit; if Krugman is right about the channel, Napier's slow, technocratic repression is the wrong model and the risk is faster and more visible. And repression requires closed capital accounts to work, which is a far larger political step than Napier's gradualism implies.

### Kenneth Rogoff (Harvard)
*'Our Dollar, Your Problem' (Yale, 2025), with the peak-in-2015 and multipolar-within-a-decade claims restated in a New Statesman interview in January 2026 and reviewed through mid-2026. He has separately maintained the reserve-currency role is not in immediate jeopardy.*

Dollar dominance peaked around 2015 and is in structural decline, and the mechanism is trust rather than economics. His argument is that the dollar's role rests on confidence in US institutions, the political system and the ability to manage debt — not on the size of the US economy — and that this foundation is being eroded simultaneously by debt trajectory, political dysfunction, the weaponisation of dollar clearing for sanctions and surveillance, the emergence of credible alternatives in the yuan and in crypto rails, and the end of the low-inflation, low-rate regime that made dollar assets uniquely attractive. He expects the system to split more evenly between the US, China and Europe within roughly a decade, and characterises the transition as gradual and then abrupt — a run on a monopoly rather than a slow decay.

- **Implied cascade:** Sanctions weaponisation and fiscal deterioration erode the trust premium → reserve managers diversify at the margin, first into gold and then into non-dollar claims → the US loses the exorbitant privilege of financing deficits at a discount → term premium and real funding cost rise → higher debt service worsens the fiscal position that caused the erosion → third-order: the US loses the ability to run large deficits cheaply exactly when its debt stock requires it, monetary policy becomes constrained by external financing rather than by domestic inflation, and the dollar's decline and the fiscal problem become the same problem.
- **Testable content:** The single sharpest data point corroborating him: the ECB's June 2026 report found gold overtook US Treasuries as the world's second-largest official reserve asset by end-2025 — gold's share of official reserves rose from 20% in 2024 to 27% in 2025 while Treasuries fell to 22%, with the dollar at 42% of total reserve assets. Central banks have bought ~1,000 tonnes a year for four years against a prior ~500-tonne average; 89% of reserve managers expect further increases and 84% expect gold to be a higher share in five years (WGC 2026 survey, 76 central banks). Also checkable: COFER dollar share; the euro's composite international role index, which rose only 0.2pp at constant exchange rates in 2025; CIPS versus SWIFT volumes; the share of oil and commodity invoicing in non-dollar currencies; foreign official Treasury holdings.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Gold's share rising is substantially a price effect, not a reallocation — gold's move higher mechanically inflates its share of reserves without any central bank selling a Treasury. The euro gained only marginally in 2025, and Lagarde's own framing on 22 June 2026 was that there is an opening for the euro 'to enhance its global appeal' only if European policymakers act — i.e. the alternative does not yet exist. Rogoff himself has said the reserve role is not in near-term jeopardy, so the timeline is elastic enough to be unfalsifiable within any investment horizon. And Setser's work suggests the reallocation being measured is partly an accounting artefact.

### Brad Setser (Council on Foreign Relations), with George Saravelos (Deutsche Bank) on the FX channel
*Setser, 'China's Fake De-Dollarization', CFR, 30 April 2026. Saravelos's hedge-ratio work through 2025-2026; corroborated by a BIS bulletin attributing much of the April-May 2025 dollar weakness to hedging by non-US investors, with the largest moves in Asian hours.*

Two distinct 'the de-dollarisation story is measured wrong' claims. Setser argues China did not de-dollarise; it moved its dollars off SAFE's balance sheet. SAFE's visible Treasury holdings fell, but state commercial banks' and policy banks' dollar books rose by around a trillion each, so China Inc. may hold more dollars off SAFE's balance sheet than on it, with total US financial assets still roughly 50-55% of the reserve portfolio — the composition shifted from long-dated Treasuries toward bills and agency MBS, which is a duration decision, not a currency decision. Saravelos supplies the complementary mechanism for dollar weakness without any asset reallocation: roughly 80% of recent foreign inflows into US equities and 50% into US bonds have been hedged, so foreigners keep buying US assets while systematically shorting the dollar against them. Only unhedged inflows finance the current account.

- **Implied cascade:** Reserve managers and institutional allocators keep buying US assets but raise FX hedge ratios → sustained forward and swap selling of dollars → the dollar falls even as demand for Treasuries and US equities holds up → observers misread price action as capital flight and infer de-dollarisation → third-order: the correct trade is long US assets and short the dollar rather than short both, and the real vulnerability is not reserve reallocation but a rollover problem in short-dated FX swaps, since hedges must be continuously renewed; a dollar funding squeeze would force hedge unwinds and produce a violent dollar rally, which is the opposite of the consensus positioning.
- **Testable content:** Setser's numbers are auditable: SAFE dollar reserves ~$1.8trn of a $3.3trn total (~55%); state commercial banks' dollar assets ~$1.0-1.1trn; policy banks' dollar claims close to $1trn; the shift from long-dated Treasuries into bills and agency MBS is visible in TIC data. Saravelos's claim is testable against BoP and custodial data and against disclosed hedge ratios: Danish institutional investors raised dollar hedge ratios from ~60% in January 2025 to over 70% by December 2025 — Danish, Dutch, Japanese and Taiwanese institutions all publish these. The falsifiable prediction is a correlation structure: if the hedging thesis is right, dollar weakness should coincide with strong, not weak, foreign demand at Treasury auctions.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Setser's claim that hidden dollars are still dollars is right on stocks but may understate the flow signal: China stopped adding to formal reserves in 2014-2016 and has since routed growth through entities with minimal currency disclosure, which is itself evidence of intent to reduce visible exposure — and intent is what matters for tail risk. The hedging mechanism explains dollar weakness but is agnostic about the cause of hedging; if hedge ratios are rising because institutional investors have permanently downgraded their assessment of dollar reliability, then Saravelos is describing the transmission of exactly the structural shift he is used to rebut. Both arguments are about measurement, and neither disproves a regime change; they show it is not yet visible in the series usually cited.

### Hyun Song Shin and the BIS, against Larry Fink (BlackRock)
*Shin's three-tests and unified-ledger argument: BIS Annual Economic Report 2025, June 2025, with the trilogy framing restated through 2026. Fink: tokenisation featured in his 2025 and 2026 BlackRock annual letters; joint commentary with Rob Goldstein in 2026 on tokenisation redrawing market plumbing. IMF Notes 26/01 (April 2026) and 26/006 (2026) treat tokenisation as a structural reconfiguration with an uncertain net stability effect.*

A direct disagreement about what tokenisation is for. Shin's BIS analysis is that stablecoins fail the three tests of sound money — singleness (they trade at different rates by issuer rather than at par), elasticity (they require full upfront backing and so cannot expand the balance sheet to meet payment demand the way bank money does), and integrity (bearer instruments on pseudonymous ledgers are structurally poor at AML and sanctions enforcement). His alternative is not to reject tokenisation but to relocate it: a 'trilogy' of tokenised central bank reserves, commercial bank money and government bonds on a unified ledger, so that programmability is achieved without abandoning the hierarchy of money. Fink's claim runs the other way — tokenisation is a redesign of market plumbing whose gain is instantaneous settlement against today's T+1, and he wants the financial system on one common blockchain.

- **Implied cascade:** Shin's chain: stablecoins scale as a payment instrument → the par convertibility of money breaks, so a dollar's value depends on which issuer's token you hold → payment system fragments and the elasticity that banks provide intraday disappears → in stress, the absence of a lender of last resort to stablecoin issuers forces fire sales of the reserve assets, transmitting a crypto run into the T-bill market → third-order: central banks must either extend the safety net to token issuers (validating the arbitrage) or build the unified ledger themselves. Fink's chain: atomic settlement eliminates counterparty and margin exposure across the settlement window → collateral velocity rises and system-wide margin requirements fall → capital currently trapped in settlement buffers is released → third-order: intermediaries whose business is the settlement gap — clearing, custody, securities lending, some of prime brokerage — lose their economic function.
- **Testable content:** Singleness is directly observable: measure secondary-market deviation from $1.00 across USDT, USDC and bank-issued tokens during stress, and the dispersion between them. Elasticity is testable at quarter-ends and in redemption spikes. The unified-ledger claim is testable by adoption: which central banks have live tokenised reserve pilots and on what timetable, and whether the BIS's Project Agora and its successors reach production. Fink's claim is testable by settlement failure rates, by whether any major venue actually moves to atomic settlement, and by whether tokenised fund AUM (BUIDL and comparable vehicles) continues to compound. The Boston and New York Feds ran a joint assessment in May 2026 of whether stablecoins and tokenisation reshape the US financial system.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Shin's three tests are a normative standard imported from central banking, and a payment instrument can be economically useful without meeting it — dollar-clearing correspondent banking also fails integrity tests in practice. His unified-ledger alternative has been proposed for years with negligible production adoption, so the counterfactual he is defending may not exist. Fink's instantaneous-settlement benefit is overstated in the other direction: netting exists because gross atomic settlement is liquidity-hungry, and moving from T+1 netted to T+0 gross raises intraday liquidity needs sharply. Hilary Allen's objection cuts both ways — she argues that when systems become more efficient they also tend to become more fragile, and that smart contracts are not typically written to handle unexpected events, so the crisis-management discretion that stopped 2008 from being worse is precisely what atomic settlement removes.

### Geoff Kendrick and John Davies (Standard Chartered)
*Standard Chartered projection published February 2026 (reported 23 February 2026); Citi raised its 2030 stablecoin forecast to $4trn in July 2026 even as circulation was contracting.*

Stablecoin issuers are becoming a structurally price-insensitive marginal buyer at the front end of the Treasury curve, and that changes how the US finances itself. The mechanism is a reserve mandate rather than a portfolio decision: unlike a money fund or a hedge fund, which reallocates on relative value, a stablecoin issuer must hold short-dated Treasuries against every token in circulation regardless of yield. Every dollar minted therefore creates a dollar of mandatory bill demand. At the scale they project, this becomes large enough that the Treasury can change its issuance mix in response, shifting supply toward bills.

- **Implied cascade:** Stablecoin circulation grows → mandatory, yield-insensitive bill demand → bills richen relative to the curve and the front end becomes scarce → Treasury responds by increasing the bill share of issuance → duration supply to the private market falls → term premium is suppressed relative to what the fiscal position would otherwise imply → third-order: the true cost of the deficit is masked, the average maturity of the debt shortens, and the sovereign's rollover frequency rises — so a stablecoin redemption wave becomes simultaneously a crypto event and a Treasury funding event, and the government acquires a direct fiscal interest in the growth of an asset class it also has to regulate.
- **Testable content:** Fully quantified and dated. Stablecoin market cap ~$309bn at the time of the forecast, projected to $2trn by end-2028, generating $0.8-1.0trn of new T-bill demand; combined with ~$500-600bn of Fed reserve-management purchases and a similar amount from MBS reinvestment, total new bill demand of ~$2.2trn by 2028; potential excess demand of ~$0.9trn if the bill share of outstanding debt is not raised. Every element is monitorable: stablecoin market cap, issuer reserve disclosures (Tether and Circle attestations give exact bill holdings), the bill share of marketable debt in Treasury's quarterly refunding statements, and TBAC minutes. Citi's $4trn-by-2030 versus contracting current circulation is the cleanest live disconfirmation test available.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The forecast has already diverged from reality — circulation was contracting in mid-2026 while Citi was raising its 2030 number, which is the signature of a projection driven by narrative rather than run-rate. The price-insensitivity claim is weaker than stated: issuers can and do hold repo, bank deposits and money fund shares as well as bills, so the mandatory-demand channel is a choice within a set. At the top of the range the demand is large relative to the bill market but small relative to total marketable debt, so the term-premium suppression may be second-order. And the causality may run backwards: Treasury's own decision to concentrate issuance in bills predates stablecoin scale and has its own suppressive effect on term premium, which would mean stablecoins are absorbing a supply shift rather than causing one.

### Paul Atkins, SEC Chair (Project Crypto), against Hilary Allen (American University)
*Nasdaq and NYSE tokenised trading approvals in March and April 2026; innovation exemption targeted for release as soon as 18 May 2026, with a sandbox window of 12-36 months; Atkins and Peirce statement 18 February 2026; commentary in June 2026 questioning whether the exemption route delivers the resilience a full rule would.*

Atkins's claim is that the binding constraint on tokenised markets is regulatory permission, and that removing it changes market structure rather than merely adding a venue. His 'innovation exemption' is an explicitly cabined framework letting firms issue and trade tokenised securities on public blockchains and DeFi protocols without full registration for a defined window, while the SEC writes durable rules. Critically, the tokens carry economic exposure but are structured to exclude voting and dividend rights — the exemption therefore unbundles cash-flow exposure from ownership rights. Allen's counter is that the difficulty was never technological: tokenisation is a problem of legal recognition, market governance and regulatory alignment, and in her framing the technology is usually the easy part.

- **Implied cascade:** A time-limited exemption lets crypto-native venues trade US equities without full broker-dealer registration → 24/7 fractional trading of large-cap equities fragments liquidity across a new, unlinked set of venues → price formation moves partly outside NMS and outside the consolidated tape during hours when the primary market is closed → third-order: overnight and weekend gap risk is repriced, market-maker inventory economics change, and the equity ownership base bifurcates into holders of record with votes and holders of tokens without — which, layered on already-concentrated index ownership, further concentrates voting power in the institutions that still hold registered shares.
- **Testable content:** Adoption and structure are both observable. Check: whether the exemption was actually issued on the May 2026 timetable and what its scope is; the number of firms admitted to the sandbox; tokenised equity volume as a share of total US equity volume; the spread between a tokenised share and its registered equivalent, especially outside market hours (any persistent basis is the direct measure of whether the two are fungible); overnight and weekend volatility versus regular-session volatility. The unbundling claim is checkable in the token terms themselves — whether voting and dividend rights are conveyed. The market-structure baseline is published: Citadel Securities' 1H 2026 market structure and flows review.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Sandbox frameworks have repeatedly failed to produce meaningful volume, and 24/7 equity trading has been tried and has mostly attracted retail flow in thin conditions rather than institutional volume. A tokenised share without votes or dividends is not a substitute for a share, so the products may not compete for the same capital at all. Allen's objection is the load-bearing one: exempting these products from registration does not resolve who owns what when a chain forks, a custodian fails or a smart contract executes an unintended state — and an exemption that expires in 12-36 months creates a cliff that rational institutions will refuse to build against. If she is right, the exemption produces a small, retail, offshore-adjacent market rather than a structural change.

### Michael Green (Simplify Asset Management), with the quantitative version from Xavier Gabaix and Ralph Koijen
*Green has argued this since roughly 2016, with the demographic-reversal framing sharpened through 2025 and 2026 podcast and press appearances. Gabaix and Koijen, 'In Search of the Origins of Financial Fluctuations: The Inelastic Markets Hypothesis' (NBER 28967, 2021), with the multiplier estimated at roughly 4.7-5.9 in their 2023 revision.*
*Update 11 Sep 2026: his claims anchored to episodes, and his move from Simplify to Tier 1 Alpha (~Aug 2026), are in `2026-09-11-Green-Passive-Bid-Corpus-Dossier.md`. The figures here are as at 2 Aug and unverified.*

Green's claim is about liquidity, not valuation. His mechanism is that liquidity does not scale with market capitalisation — it scales with trading volume and volatility — so the largest index constituents are the most price-inelastic. A dollar of price-agnostic passive flow therefore pushes the largest names up more than the smallest, mechanically increasing concentration and mechanically causing cap-weighted strategies to outperform, independent of fundamentals. As the pool of price-conscious capital shrinks, the multiplier on each incremental dollar rises. The corollary is symmetric and is his actual warning: the same mechanism operates in reverse, and the trigger he identifies is demographic — retirees switching from contributing to withdrawing, with those withdrawals not recycling into active management but leaving the equity market entirely. Gabaix and Koijen supply the magnitude: using granular instrumental variables, they estimate that $1 into the aggregate stock market raises aggregate market value by roughly $5, with estimates ranging about 3-8.

- **Implied cascade:** Automatic, price-insensitive retirement flows into cap-weighted index products → largest and least elastic names absorb a disproportionate share → index concentration rises → cap-weighted benchmarks outperform → active managers underperform their benchmark and lose assets → the surviving price-conscious pool shrinks further → market elasticity falls and the flow multiplier rises → third-order: the market's response to any given dollar of outflow grows over time, so the eventual demographic reversal is amplified by exactly the mechanism that produced the melt-up; the sensitivity of the whole equity market becomes a function of pension and 401(k) net flow rather than of earnings.
- **Testable content:** The core claim decomposes into measurable pieces. Concentration: the top 10 S&P 500 names were ~19% of index weight at end-2015 and ~41% at end-2025; semiconductors are now near a fifth of the index, roughly quadrupled since June 2020. Passive share: passive is now around 55% of US fund assets, and Chinco and Sammon's work argues the true passive-ownership share is roughly double the conventionally measured figure. The multiplier is directly estimable and has been replicated and contested. Green's reversal claim is the most testable of all and is dated by demography: net flows into US equity mutual funds and ETFs by investor age cohort, 401(k) net contribution versus distribution data, and whether large passive vehicles show sustained net redemptions. If concentration is caused by flows rather than by earnings, then the top-10 share of index earnings should lag the top-10 share of index weight — that gap is directly observable and is the single cleanest test.
- **Where they could be wrong:** The top-10 share of index earnings has risen substantially alongside the weight share, which is the fundamentals-based explanation Green needs to rule out and largely has not. Cliff Asness's counter is that a shrinking pool of price-conscious capital should make markets less efficient and therefore more profitable for active managers — active managers have not captured that, which is awkward for the theory. The Gabaix-Koijen multiplier is estimated on aggregate flows over a specific sample and is contested on identification. And Green has been making the reversal call for close to a decade; the demographic trigger is real but slow, and the timing has been consistently wrong, which matters because his entire claim is about a threshold effect.

### Lucian Bebchuk and Scott Hirst (Harvard/BU), against Marcel Kahan and Edward Rock (NYU)
*Bebchuk and Hirst's 'Specter of the Giant Three' (2019) and 'Index Funds and the Future of Corporate Governance' (Columbia Law Review, 2019), with ownership data updated through 2023 in the Boston University Law Review. Kahan and Rock, 'Index Funds and Corporate Governance: Let Shareholders be Shareholders' (BU Law Review, 2020). The live test: Texas and 12 other states sued BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street in December 2024 over alleged coordinated suppression of coal output; motions to dismiss were largely denied on 1 August 2025; Vanguard settled on 25 February 2026 for $29.5m plus stewardship commitments; claims against BlackRock and State Street remain pending.*

An unresolved disagreement about whether concentrated index ownership converts into control. Bebchuk and Hirst argue that the Big Three's stake in S&P 500 companies rose from 13.5% in 2008 to 22.2% in 2023, that they hold over 20% in 438 S&P 500 companies, and that this trajectory produces a 'Giant Three' dominating voting at most large US public companies. Their causal claim is specifically about incentives, not size: index managers capture only a tiny fraction of the value created by good stewardship but bear its full cost, and they face business-tie pressures — 401(k) mandates, inclusion in fund menus, the risk of political backlash — that make deference to management the rational strategy. So concentration produces under-stewardship, not activism. Kahan and Rock reach the opposite conclusion from the same facts: scale makes votes more likely to be pivotal and makes the fee gain from higher portfolio value larger, so the Big Three have among the strongest direct financial incentives to become informed.

- **Implied cascade:** Index share of US equity ownership rises → voting power concentrates in three firms whose stewardship budgets do not scale with assets → per-company monitoring falls even as votes controlled rise → managements face a large but structurally passive shareholder base → agency costs at portfolio companies rise → third-order: the political system, not the market, resolves the accountability gap — through antitrust litigation, state-level mandates and forced pass-through voting — which means the resolution arrives as legal and regulatory risk to the asset managers themselves rather than as a market repricing.
- **Testable content:** Ownership is exactly measurable from 13F and N-PX filings: the Big Three's combined S&P 500 stake, the number of companies where they exceed 20%, and their combined S&P 500 index fund assets, now above $2.6trn. Stewardship intensity is measurable: stewardship staff headcount per portfolio company, number of engagements, and — most directly — voting records against management on contested items, all disclosed in N-PX. The Bebchuk deference hypothesis predicts a low and roughly constant against-management rate regardless of issue; Kahan and Rock predict variation with the stakes. The Texas litigation supplies a real-world resolution mechanism with dates: the Vanguard settlement requires it to withdraw its US businesses from climate-focused stewardship groups and to expand proxy choice, and the take-up rate of pass-through voting across the Big Three is now a published, trackable number — that take-up rate is the single cleanest measure of whether concentration is actually being unwound.
- **Where they could be wrong:** Bebchuk and Hirst's ownership data runs to 2023 and the environment has changed materially since: pass-through voting programmes, the Vanguard settlement's stewardship commitments and the broader retreat from coordinated ESG engagement all cut against the 'Giant Three exercise concentrated control' framing, and could mean the problem is being solved by litigation before it becomes acute. The deference thesis and the antitrust suit are also in tension — Texas alleges the Big Three used ownership to coerce output cuts, which requires exactly the active control Bebchuk says they have no incentive to exercise; both cannot be right. Kahan and Rock's counter has its own weakness: pivotality raises the value of an informed vote only if the manager can capture the gain, and in a fee-on-assets business the capture is a few basis points of a diffuse benefit. Neither side has produced a clean identification of index ownership causing worse corporate outcomes.
